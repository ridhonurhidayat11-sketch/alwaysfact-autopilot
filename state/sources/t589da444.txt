Sign Up Levent Alpöge got back to the place he was crashing in New York City around 4 A.M. on November 20. He was almost done. He could almost sleep. All that was left was to download the 17 million lines of code he’d spent the night preparing for public release. It needed to go up on his personal website, alongside a four-page statement he’d tinkered with all week. But for the moment, the code still sat on the servers of his employer, the artificial intelligence company Anthropic—which had gone public just hours earlier with the largest valuation in stock market history, making Alpöge a rich man. Once the upload was complete, he would also be one of history’s most famous mathematicians. The code was the product of untold computing resources, months of work and decades of dreaming. It proved the Riemann hypothesis , a 167-year-old math problem so formidable that it is almost beyond words; in public talks, experts have tended to simply treat it as an over-the-horizon aspiration, a “vanishing point” perspective against which all other math problems shrink. Just weeks earlier Terence Tao—arguably the world’s most famous mathematician—had declared the hypothesis out of AI’s reach (let alone that of humans) until perhaps 2050. Alpöge’s quiet upload capped off an epochal year in which AI has systematically conquered humanity’s oldest discipline in a sequence of escalating, dramatic and controversial discoveries. And in nearly every episode of this earthshaking war for math’s soul, Alpöge has figured somehow—part battlefield champion, part horseman of the AI apocalypse, part Forrest Gump. His eccentric ways have shaped this saga, maybe more than any other single person on Earth, and thereby the course of history. That is perhaps why people are so divided over Alpöge. “Who is this person who’s behind some of the most striking applications of AI to mathematics?” says Henry Cohn, a mathematician at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. “This is a really interesting question that deserves a deeper answer than you can get from following him on X.” Depending on who you ask, Alpöge is a reviled online troll—an agent of chaos laughing as his fellow mathematicians watch their intellectual landscape get carpet-bombed by his Silicon Valley cohort—or a reluctant public figure—a shy academic who has integrity and wants to forge a future where AI is mathematicians’ collaborator, not their usurper. Or maybe he’s an arrogant genius who has a chip on his shoulder and is bent on leveraging his access, his internal models and unlimited tokens, to prove his doubters wrong by snatching every remaining mathematical trophy off the shelf. Whatever the truth is, it’s hard to know because Alpöge won’t tell. He refuses to speak to media. Sean Zanni/Getty Images Alpöge’s online persona is confusing and tempestuous. He topples famous conjectures with glib and gleeful social media posts—and then gets baited into emotionally replying to anonymous commenters. His X bio starts with the self-effacing descriptor “idiot” but follows with a litany of world-class accolades. There are few photographs of Alpöge online, but they all present a consistent picture: thick eyebrows over an angular, almost-elfin face, thin bangs halfway between a Beatles mop top and a bowl cut and an impish smile that reveals a slight gap between his front teeth and an unmistakable capacity for mischief. According to numerous sources close to him, though, behind the irreverent visage is a fierce hunger for respect and recognition, paired with a boundless belief in his own capabilities—a drive shocking even to the world-class polymaths who know him. No goal is too ambitious. Even the Riemann hypothesis—math’s one true holy grail—proved to be no limit. “He has this will to take on the world and win,” says Scott Armstrong, a mathematician at New York University and a rare Alpöge confidant. “He’s got it in spades.” In today’s world, where large teams are the norm for any assault on frontier research problems, Alpöge is an anomaly: he mostly works alone, except, of course, for the powerful internal models he has privileged access to. “Here is a person that is larger than life,” Armstrong says. “I keep asking myself, ‘When they make the movie, who is going to play Levent?’” Levent Alpöge was born on April Fool’s Day, 1992, along the central stretch of Long Island. His parents, both Turkish immigrants, realized their son’s intellect was remarkable when he was just three years old. As his mother told a Turkish newspaper in 2014 , she was reading him one of his favorite books and said she was too tired to continue, so Alpöge picked up where she’d stopped. At first she thought he had memorized the page, but when she gave him another book, he kept reading. Somehow, he’d learned without instruction. On the advice of professionals, they enrolled him in a nearby gifted program. Levent Alpöge’s one sister, Elif, is a decade older, which made him practically an only child for much of the time. Alpöge was extremely athletic and especially into distance running and soccer—with his favorite soccer team being Istanbul’s Beşiktaş Gymnastics Club. He picked up coding early. In 2009, at age 17, he connected with bioinformatics professor Xenophon Papademetris of Yale University. Papademetris’s group assigned Alpöge a research problem the team had little time for. The idea was to use Nvidia graphical processing units (GPUs)—the technology now powering the AI revolution—to recognize vascular structures in medical images. “He helped do one of the first GPU implementations of an image analysis algorithm,” Papademetris says. “He was a 17-year-old kid who could say, ‘I will do it.’” The professor recalls what he wrote of Alpöge in his college recommendation letter, “His enthusiasm was contagious, his energy was boundless, and his confidence in his ability to overcome obstacles almost frightening.” Alpöge’s algorithm was enough to “punch your ticket to any university in the country,” Papademetris says. “My letter was irrelevant.” As a bonus for the achievement, a team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology’s Lincoln Laboratory named an asteroid “Alpöge.” The teenager ultimately chose Harvard University. He fell in love with math once he got there. “He distinguished himself even in that class of overachieving first-years,” says Noam Elkies of Harvard, who was his math professor that year, “finding more than half the corrections in class materials that I did not notice sooner.” Other instructors noted Alpöge’s fastidiousness could cause tensions: in a recent Bluesky post , physicist Matt Reece recalled how the young Alpöge joined Reece’s graduate physics course and so angered the students with his persistent mathematical questions that “I feared someone would actually punch him in class.” By his junior year, Alpöge was under the tutelage of renowned mathematician Jacob Tsimerman. (A few months ago Tsimerman won a Fields Medal, math’s highest honor, and then announced he was leaving academia to work on AI safety.)
