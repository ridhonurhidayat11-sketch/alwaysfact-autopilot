Science News from research organizations New nose drops restore youthful memory and fight Alzheimer’s in aging mice Date: November 24, 2026 Source: University of Sydney Summary: A revolutionary new nasal treatment has restored youthful learning and memory performance in aging mice while preventing severe cognitive decline in mice with Alzheimer’s-like disease. Developed using technology similar to COVID-19 mRNA vaccines, the nose drops stimulate production of a brain-protecting protein that strengthens neurons and supports memory. Remarkably, the treatment preserved brain function without removing Alzheimer’s-associated plaques, suggesting a completely different way to combat cognitive decline. Share: Facebook Twitter Pinterest LinkedIN Email FULL STORY Experimental mRNA nose drops restored youthful memory performance in aging mice and protected others from Alzheimer’s-like cognitive decline, offering a promising new approach to treating brain aging. Credit: Shutterstock Researchers at the University of Sydney have created experimental nose drops that significantly improved learning and memory in older mice, bringing their performance back to levels seen in younger animals. The treatment also prevented severe memory decline in mice genetically engineered to develop Alzheimer's-like disease. Remarkably, the approach relies on mRNA technology similar to that used in COVID-19 vaccines. The discovery, led by Professor Greg Neely at the University of Sydney's Charles Perkins Centre, could open a new path toward treating conditions that affect the brain. Potential applications extend beyond Alzheimer's disease to other neurodegenerative disorders, stroke, traumatic brain injury, and rare genetic and developmental conditions. "If we can proceed quickly to clinical trials in humans, this approach could represent a world-first non-invasive delivery system to treat Alzheimer's that bypasses the blood-brain barrier," Professor Neely said. The idea behind the research has an unusual origin. The team drew inspiration from Daniel Keyes' science fiction story Flowers for Algernon , which explores the limits of human intelligence through experiments designed to increase intelligence in laboratory mice and people. How mRNA Nose Drops Restore Youthful Memory The treatment works by delivering messenger RNA (mRNA), a type of genetic instruction that tells cells how to produce specific proteins. These instructions are enclosed in tiny fat-based particles called lipid nanoparticles (LNPs), which protect the mRNA and help it enter cells. Once delivered through the nose, the mRNA instructs cells to manufacture therapeutic proteins capable of reaching the brain. In effect, the nasal tissue becomes a small drug-producing factory, creating substances that can support brain function. "In aged mice, we saw learning and memory significantly increased, returning animal cognition to its youthful prime," Professor Neely said. "What's exciting is that this was achieved with a simple non-invasive nasal delivery system that builds on the same underlying mRNA-LNP technology platform many people became familiar with during the COVID-19 pandemic." The study, published in Advanced Science , used mRNA to stimulate production of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). This naturally occurring protein helps neurons survive, form connections, and adapt as the brain learns and stores memories. Levels of BDNF tend to decline with aging and are also associated with Alzheimer's disease. The results were striking. Older mice that received the nose drops performed about as well as young mice in standardized tests of working memory, spatial memory, and object recognition. These tests measure abilities such as remembering recently encountered information, navigating surroundings, and recognizing familiar objects. Untreated older animals showed the expected age-related deterioration in cognitive performance. Nose Drops Prevent Alzheimer's-Like Memory Decline in Mice To investigate whether the treatment might also protect against Alzheimer's disease, researchers turned to a widely used laboratory model known as 5XFAD mice. These animals carry genetic mutations that cause them to develop Alzheimer's-like brain changes and memory problems unusually early in life. In this model, signs of Alzheimer's-related brain pathology can typically be detected by two months of age, with measurable cognitive decline appearing around four months. The researchers began administering the BDNF-producing mRNA nose drops when the mice were two months old, around the time the disease-related changes first appeared. Treatment continued twice a week. By four months of age, the treated mice showed no further cognitive decline, unlike the severe deterioration normally seen in this model. The findings suggest that supporting brain cells early in the disease process may help preserve cognitive abilities. One of the most intriguing results was what the treatment did not do. Although memory and learning were protected, the researchers found no reduction in amyloid plaques, the abnormal protein deposits that accumulate in the brains of people with Alzheimer's disease. That distinction could be important because many existing Alzheimer's treatments aim to remove amyloid. The new approach instead appears to help neurons remain healthy and functional despite the presence of these deposits. If the same effect can eventually be demonstrated in people, the two treatment strategies might be used together. "Current Alzheimer's treatments largely focus on clearing these amyloid plaques," Professor Neely said. "However, just under a third of Alzheimer's patients have serious plaques but do not suffer from dementia, and these patients show naturally higher BDNF levels, suggesting if we raise BDNF, it's possible to live with Alzheimer's disease pathology." "Our approach seems to work not by clearing plaques but by supporting the health, survival and connectivity of neurons themselves. It raises the possibility these therapies could eventually work alongside each other." Bypassing the Blood-Brain Barrier Through the Nose Getting medicines into the brain is one of the biggest obstacles in developing treatments for neurological diseases. The blood-brain barrier acts as a protective filter, preventing many potentially harmful substances in the bloodstream from entering delicate brain tissue. Unfortunately, it also blocks numerous drugs that scientists would like to use to treat brain disorders. The nose offers a possible alternative route. Connections between the nasal cavity and the central nervous system may allow certain therapeutic substances to reach the brain without requiring surgery or injections directly into the brain or spinal cord. "This is a very exciting proof of concept for a method to deliver mRNA-encoded cargo to the nervous system directly through the nose," Professor Neely said. The team adapted mRNA technology built on the same clinically validated BioNTech platform used for COVID-19 vaccination. Instead of delivering instructions that prompt an immune response, the modified system provides instructions for producing proteins intended to benefit the brain. First author Dr. Maria Bergamasco, from the University of Sydney's School of Life and Environmental Sciences, explained that the findings illustrate how mRNA technology could be used for purposes far beyond preventing infectious diseases. "The success of mRNA vaccines showed these systems can safely deliver genetic instructions to cells," Dr. Bergamasco said. "We wanted to see whether we could adapt that technology to deliver beneficial proteins to the brain in a completely non-invasive way." Treatment Stimulates New Brain Cell Growth The researchers also detected increased neurogenesis, the process through which new neurons are formed, in the hippocampus. This part of the brain plays an essential role in learning and memory and is particularly vulnerable to damage associated with Alzheimer's disease. The increase in new neurons provides another possible explanation for the improvements in cognitive performance. By promoting the health and adaptability of brain cells, BDNF may help maintain the neural networks needed for learning and remembering information. The nose drops also appeared to be well tolerated. Researchers reported no major side effects in the mice during the study, although further testing will be needed to establish long-term safety. Could mRNA Nose Drops Work in Humans? Despite the encouraging results, Professor Neely emphasized that the treatment remains experimental.
