On Aug. 2, 2027, that clock will bring the moon's dark central shadow — a path of totality — back to Egypt. A t otal solar eclipse will sweep across the country , passing directly over Luxor and plunging some of ancient Egypt's greatest surviving monuments into darkness. Totality will begin in Luxor at approximately 1:02 p.m. EEST and last 6 minutes 22 seconds, according to Time and Date . We know when it will happen because we can calculate the motions of Earth and the moon centuries in advance. But run the same celestial clock backward and eclipses become one of nature's most precise timestamps. Astronomers can reconstruct where the moon's shadow fell thousands of years ago, even when nobody left a record of it. When an ancient text reliably describes an eclipse, it can anchor a moment in history to a specific place and time. Did an eclipse rewrite the Pyramid Age? Pharaoh Shepseskaf's huge rectangular tomb at South Saqqara (Image credit: Jon Bodsworth - www.egyptarchive.co.uk) We know from astronomical calculations that eclipses crossed Egypt long before anyone left an identifiable written record of one. On April 1, 2471 B.C., a total solar eclipse swept across northern Egypt during the age of the great pyramid builders. In research published in 2025, archaeoastronomer Giulio Magli at the Polytechnic University of Milan proposed that the eclipse may help explain why Pharaoh Shepseskaf broke with tradition and built a huge rectangular tomb at South Saqqara instead of a pyramid.
