NASA Earth Observatory/Lauren Dauphin More than 60 years ago, scientists at NASA’s Goddard Space Flight Center aimed a laser skyward and fired a pulse of light at an orbiting satellite equipped with special reflectors. By measuring the time it took for the light to return, they demonstrated a new, more precise method for tracking satellites. Since then, satellite laser ranging (SLR) has grown more precise and more widely used. The technique now underpins many aspects of Earth observation, such as validating satellite orbits and mapping subtle changes to Earth’s surface. Pioneered at the Goddard Optical Research Facility—now the Goddard Geophysical and Astronomical Observatory —laser ranging is performed at more than 40 stations worldwide. In 2026, the next generation of SLR, developed by NASA scientists and tested at Goddard, began to come online. On October 12, a faster, even more precise SLR system officially debuted at the Ny-Ålesund Geodetic Observatory in Svalbard, Norway, operated by the Norwegian Mapping Authority, a NASA partner. The observatory’s remote location in the Arctic archipelago is shown in this image, acquired with the OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 in July 2026. It is the first NASA next-generation SLR system in operation and the northernmost SLR facility on Earth. While the systems developed decades ago have been “workhorses” of the global laser tracking network, their age and the growing number of satellites have necessitated updates, said Stephen Merkowitz, project manager for NASA’s Space Geodesy Project . “We wanted newer systems that could track more objects, and track them more accurately and precisely,” he said. The Space Geodesy Satellite Laser Ranging system fires at the Ny-Ålesund Geodetic Observatory in Svalbard, Norway, against a backdrop of aurora borealis on October 7, 2026.
