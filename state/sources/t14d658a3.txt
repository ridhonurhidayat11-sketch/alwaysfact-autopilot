Photograph: Esa/PA Space Spacecraft reaches Mercury on second-ever mission to enter its orbit BepiColombo double probe will map planet’s surface and study its interior to shed light on its unusually large core Ian Sample Science editor Thu 19 Nov 2026 19.01 EST Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 19.03 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google After a voyage of more than eight years and 6bn miles, a joint European-Japanese space mission has arrived at Mercury and is preparing to swing into orbit around the tiny, barely explored world. The BepiColombo mission will become only the second in history to circle the first rock from the sun and the smallest planet in the solar system when flight controllers fire the spacecraft’s thrusters on Saturday and send it into orbit. For researchers, the €1.6bn (£1.4bn) mission is the best hope yet of understanding how Mercury formed so close to the sun, why it has a magnetic field, and what makes up the frozen ice that lurks in the planet’s permanently shadowed craters. “We’re very excited, it’s been a long wait,” said Prof Emma Bunce at the University of Leicester, who has worked on the project for 20 years. Getting to Mercury poses unusual difficulties. Because the planet is near the centre of the solar system, spacecraft accelerate under the sun’s gravity and must spend much of their time braking to avoid shooting straight past. “It’s a very complex and challenging mission,” said Bunce, the principal investigator on an instrument called the Mercury Imaging X-ray Spectrometer, or MIXS, which will analyse the geochemical composition of the planet’s surface. View image in fullscreen A photo made available by the European Space Agency in October 2021 shows a view of Mercury captured by the BepiColombo mission. Photograph: European Space Agency/AFP/Getty Images It will be some months before scientists can start observing Mercury. In early December, the spacecraft will separate into two orbiters, one European, the other Japanese, which will manoeuvre to different polar orbits around the planet. Science operations are due to start in April next year. The European probe, named the Mercury Planetary Orbiter or MPO, will map the planet’s surface and composition at high resolution, and study Mercury’s interior to shed light on its unusually large core. Its Japanese partner will measure the planet’s magnetic field and its sodium exosphere, the extremely thin layer of gas and atoms that surrounds the planet. Mercury is only 50% larger than the moon but has four times its mass. One question the mission will explore is why the rock is so dense. Prof Geraint Jones, the lead project scientist on BepiColombo at the European Space Agency, said anticipation of the scientific results had kept everyone enthused during the spacecraft’s eight-year journey . “We’re expecting to learn a huge amount, bringing more powerful instruments on two spacecraft to study all aspects of the planet – its interior, surface, and the space around it,” he said. Mercury is a planet of extremes. The tiny world takes 58 Earth days to rotate on its axis, meaning any given spot on the surface can experience daylight for three months and then night-time for three months. It is little more than a third of Earth’s distance from the sun, at only 36m miles. In the day, ground temperatures reach 450C while at night they drop to -180C. Jones said: “As well as cameras covering a wide range of wavelengths to study the surface, we’ll be ‘tasting’ the gases around the planet, sensing the magnetic field, probing the interior with ultrasensitive instruments, and mapping the shape of the whole planet with a laser.” The spacecraft are expected to orbit Mercury for about three years, after which they will run out of fuel, lose altitude and ultimately slam into the surface. “Sadly, they are not coming back,” said Bunce.
