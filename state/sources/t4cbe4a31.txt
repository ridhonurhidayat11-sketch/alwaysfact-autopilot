The future looks bright as well, with the foundation of the Space Games Federation , the self-described "first governing and sanctioning body for competitive sports played in zero or micro-gravity," co-founded by a NASA astronaut. Not to be outdone, science fiction has expanded the idea of zero-g and low-gravity sports to their logical extent, imagining everything from Doctor Who's zero-g squash to the advanced, gamified combat simulations of Ender's Game. There was even Space Jam 's alien basketball game for the fate of the planet, though the gravity-defying antics there come from cartoon shenanigans rather than a zero-g playing field. As humanity edges closer to colonizing our neighbors through projects like the Artemis Program and its ambition to establish habitats on the moon, our imaginations have to expand to encompass not only how we'll live in space but also how we'll play. It defines how balls arc, how quickly players can move, and even how we orient ourselves in play spaces. Remove the gravity, and all of those assumptions and mechanics evaporate as well. "First of all, in zero g, Isaac Newton will win every time," Les Johnson, former Chief Technologist for NASA, told Space. Les is a physicist with over 35 years of experience working at Marshall Space Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama, as well as a science fiction author.
