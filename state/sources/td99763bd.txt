Billings: You know, it’s hard not to go with NASA’s Voyager 1 probe, which is now, uh, on its way out of the solar system into interstellar space, reaching the one light-day milestone. So people talk about light-years a lot, which, you know, “Oh, a light year, how far is that?” Well, really, really, really far. But it turns out even a light-day is very, very far because Voyager 1 was launched almost a half century ago, in September 1977, on a tour of the outer solar system. It swung by Jupiter and Saturn. Here we are, almost a half-century later. It’s been traveling at about 38,000 miles per hour, and just last week, Voyager 1 finally hit that milestone of one light-day , the point at which a signal from Earth to the spacecraft or a signal from the spacecraft to Earth takes a full 24 hours traveling through space to get to its destination. And that, to me, is just wild. It’s a relatively small thing, but it’s still so cool. Feltman: Yeah. No, it really puts the scale of it all into perspective. You know, I feel like Voyager is such a fun mission, and we don’t talk about it that much. I feel like it comes up when we hit a big milestone for how far out it is. So could you remind our listeners what the point of Voyager is and what it’s doing out there? Billings: So Voyager 1 was one of two probes. There was a sister probe called Voyager 2 that was actually, somewhat counterintuitively, launched earlier than Voyager 1. But the pair of them went out into the outer solar system on what some people called a grand tour. And Voyager 1 looked at Jupiter and at Saturn, and Voyager 2 looked at those as well and also looked at Uranus and Neptune. And now they’re both on their way out of the solar system. They, in fact, have already breached the so-called heliopause, this boundary in space where the sun’s influence wanes, and instead they’re essentially in interstellar space. And today it seems kind of basic. Oh, they went and saw these outer planets. Well, back then we didn’t really know much about these planets, and even now some of the observations from Voyager 1 or Voyager 2 are the best we’ve got from some of these locations, like Neptune or Uranus, for instance. Feltman: Well, and speaking of, you know, exactly how far out they are, it’s kind of a semantic discussion of if and when they’ve reached the solar system, right? Billings: That’s right. It depends on, kind of, what you define as the end of the solar system. For instance, beyond this heliopause I mentioned, this boundary where the sun’s influence wanes in terms of its solar wind and stuff like that, there’s also something called the Oort Cloud, which is much, much farther out, and that’s what we think is a big spherical shell of comets that are very, very, very far out around the solar system, but they’re still gravitationally bound to the sun. They’re still influenced by the sun’s gravity and held in thrall by it. And so some people would say that that is the true boundary of the solar system. So you’re right, it is a somewhat semantic consideration. But the whole point is that this is the farthest anything that we’ve ever sent out there has gotten in terms of a physical artifact, and it may stay that way for quite some time. Feltman: Also, just as an aside: [I] love the Oort cloud. In my opinion, it’s one of the most Star Trek names of a thing that we have in our immediate cosmic vicinity. It’s just like, “That cloud full of Oorts,” you know? Billings: Yeah, and the weirdest thing is, you know, we’ve never actually seen it directly. The only way we know it’s there, basically, is that we can look at the trajectories of these long-period comets and surmise that they must be coming from this reservoir that’s very, very far away, with a boundary that’s perhaps around one light-year away. And unfortunately, it will take Voyager 1 much longer to get past that. Feltman: Well, great segue to my last question about Voyager, which is just: What can we expect next from this, you know, very long-lived mission? What would you say are some of the milestones or pings back that we’re most excited about? Billings: So I don’t wanna be a downer. I do think that what’s happened with Voyager 1 and also Voyager 2 is amazing and beautiful, and they’re phenomenal achievements of humanity that will go down in history. At the same time, nothing lasts forever, and Voyager 1, and I think also Voyager 2, are both slated to essentially go dark no later than about 2036, about 10 years from now. And that’s because their radioactive, nuclear power sources that give them heat and electricity way out there, where things like solar power won’t work ’cause they’re so far away, uh, will diminish to essentially nothing due to radioactive decay. They will no longer be able to send signals back. They’ll no longer be able to stay warm, and so they’ll freeze and go dark. Obviously that’s at most 10 years away. I think perhaps it’s optimistic to think that they will reach that point because they’re already hanging on by a thread. Some people at NASA were very concerned that Voyager 1 wouldn’t even be awake, so to speak, to see this light-day milestone because they have been having a lot of trouble with keeping its instruments on. They have been shutting down instruments and components on these spacecraft to continue to use them on the small trickle of electricity, of power that still comes from these radioactive power sources. And so we do have some, some time still left in their life, so enjoy them while you can. Feltman: Always good advice. I guess you could say that if they’re chopsticks, then the Starship upper stage that did this is like the world’s biggest egg roll, and it plucked it right out of midair. And the reason why this is a big deal is because that’s never, ever, ever been done before. This is something out of science fiction. The engineering behind this is staggering. Only SpaceX has managed to do it, and it matters because if they can catch these things routinely and then fly them again very quickly—that’s the whole point of catching them like this—then you are in a new era of spaceflight.
