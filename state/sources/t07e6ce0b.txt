Illustration: Image courtesy NLA Archaeology ‘Remarkable luck’: wreck of first Australian-built warship discovered exactly 128 years after sinking HMCS Spitfire was commissioned by colonial government to protect against Russian warships during the Crimean war Petra Stock Thu 19 Nov 2026 00.22 EST Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 00.37 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google The wreck of Australia’s first locally built warship has been discovered 128 years after sinking off the coast of far north Queensland. Marine archaeologists found the remains of Ex HMCS Spitfire on 21 August, the anniversary of its sinking in 1898. Celeste Jordan, the principal heritage officer at Queensland’s Department of the Environment, Tourism, Science and Innovation, said it was “remarkable luck”. Rediscovering lost shipwrecks would “help ensure the stories of our past are preserved, celebrated and protected for future generations”, she said. Archaeology against the clock: the race to salvage fragments of early Brisbane Read more The 19-metre wooden gunboat, designed and built in Sydney in 1855, was commissioned by the Australian colonial government to protect against Russian warships based at Vladivostok during the Crimean war and amid concern about the French military presence in New Caledonia. It was designed to carry a 32-pounder cannon, although evidence suggests the armament may never have been fitted. Photograph: Jay Cronan/Department of Defence The clipper later served Queensland as a pilot vessel, before it was sold for £300 into private hands as a sea cucumber fishing vessel. A 1981 article in the Naval Historical Review described the boat as “a sturdy little vessel”. Constructed from ironbark and blackwood, and sheathed in copper, it was designed and built by the Sydney shipbuilder John Cuthbert, and launched by his wife Susan Cuthbert from Darling Harbour in 1855. On 21 August 1898, Spitfire developed a leak and foundered on a voyage off the coast of Queensland’s Cape York peninsula, according to the Australian National Maritime Museum. Its crew were rescued by the Japanese steamship Omi Maru. A crew from the Australian National Maritime Museum, the Queensland government and the Royal Australian Navy found the wreck while aboard HMAS Diamantina, a minehunter vessel. The month-long expedition searched an area based on archival records, with sonar picking up the Spitfire as an acoustic anomaly. The chief of navy, Vice Admiral Matthew Buckley, said the Spitfire occupied a unique place in Australia’s naval history. “For sailors today, this vessel represents the beginning of a story that continues in the Royal Australian Navy.” The wreck is protected under the Underwater Cultural Heritage Act, and is one of more than 8,000 shipwrecks, sunken aircraft and other heritage sites in Australian waters.
