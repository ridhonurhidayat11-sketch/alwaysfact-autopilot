Credit: Jacques Descloitres/ MODIS Land Rapid Response Team at NASA GSFC , Public Domain The Alpine Fault cuts across New Zealand’s South Island for hundreds of kilometers, a feature so distinct that it is visible from space. The jagged, snow-capped peaks of the Southern Alps are separated from the lush green coastal lowlands to the west by a prominent escarpment that forms a naturally straight line. The fault, marking the boundary where the Australian and Pacific plates slide past each other with a slight uplift component , last ruptured in 1717, when it produced an estimated magnitude 8.1 earthquake. Three centuries later, scientists know another rupture will occur eventually. The question is how big that quake will be. Over decades of paleoseismic research , scientists have reconstructed 8,000 years of the Alpine Fault’s history, revealing that earthquakes of about magnitude 8 occur every 300 or so years. How much shaking is unleashed by the next big one depends in part on how far a rupture might travel along the fault. But though the Alpine Fault seems relatively simple from space, it’s poorly understood below the surface. “We know that for continental strike slips, the fault geometry has control on how the earthquake ruptures.” This newsletter rocks. Now, Lai and her colleagues are using an innovative technology to gain a clearer picture of that geometry. The results were published 25 November in Science Advances , and Lai will present the research on 10 December at AGU’s Annual Meeting 2026 in San Francisco. For the new study, the researchers turned to an unlikely tool: underground fiber-optic cables. A 30-kilometer array of cables, part of a national telecommunications network, crosses the Alpine Fault. And fortunately for the scientists, there are extra fibers within the cables that were installed for future use but remain inactive. “We can use those for scientific purposes,” said coauthor Meghan Miller , a structural seismologist at Australian National University in Canberra. The technique, called distributed acoustic sensing, or DAS, takes advantage of these “dark fibers,” enabling the scientists to collect data without having to deploy hundreds of individual seismic sensors across the rugged landscape. Mapping the Fault DAS works by sending pulses of laser light through the cable and measuring how long it takes light that is backscattered by imperfections in the fiber cable’s glass to return. When seismic waves from small, nearby earthquakes cause the fiber to compress or stretch, these changes alter the light’s return time. This timing is used to measure how much the cable has been strained at points every few meters along the fiber during an earthquake, effectively transforming it into thousands of closely spaced seismometers. (left to right) Voon Hui Lai, an observational seismologist at Nanyang Technological University; John Townend, a geophysicist at Victoria University of Wellington; and Meghan Miller, a structural seismologist at Australian National University discuss the Alpine Fault during their fieldwork in New Zealand. Credit: Voon Hui Lai Seismic waves pass through different types of rock at varying speeds. They are slowed down by the highly fractured rock found at the core of a fault, where most of the movement takes place during an earthquake. By tracking the differences in arrival times between trapped seismic waves and direct seismic waves from small nearby earthquakes, scientists can build a high-resolution image of Earth’s subsurface. Although often referred to as a single fault, the Alpine is divided into several segments that differ in orientation and geometry. Lai’s team focused on a section near the town of Haast, at the boundary between the South and Central Westland segments. “Previous work has suggested that when earthquakes nucleate or start in one section, they may or may not jump across this boundary,” Miller said. “If they do jump or rupture across this boundary, then the earthquakes can be much larger.” The high-resolution images from DAS revealed that at depth, the fault is more complex—and continuous—than previously thought. The team found six 10-meter-wide fault cores, or areas where most of the intense deformation takes place, within a less intensely fractured zone of rock about 200 meters wide. The principal fault cores stretch for 180 kilometers and persist across the boundary separating the two segments. This assessment could refine simulations of how future earthquakes might unfold. “We can be informed about how big the earthquakes could possibly be,” Miller said. “And that, of course, can help people prepare for what may come in the future.” Preparing for the Next Big One Scientists estimate there is a 75% chance the Alpine Fault will rupture in a major earthquake over the next 50 years. And while DAS technology won’t allow scientists to predict when the next quake will occur on the Alpine Fault, what it’s revealing can help people be more prepared when it does. “Something we’ve been advocating for a lot is having a well-prepared science response in the event that this earthquake happens.” “Something we’ve been advocating for a lot is having a well-prepared science response in the event that this earthquake happens,” said Camilla Penney , a lecturer of geodynamics and tectonics at the University of Canterbury in Christchurch, New Zealand, who was not involved in the study. Today, scientists and emergency managers on the South Island are working to foster more interest in community readiness and civil defense planning through programs such as AF8 (Alpine Fault magnitude 8) . The team’s success is one example of a growing effort to use DAS to probe faults around the world. In the United States, the U.S. Geological Survey is actively deploying DAS to learn more about similar dangerous faults, including California’s San Andreas Fault. “The more we understand of big faults, the better off we are,” Lai said. —Gabrielle Dela Cruz, Science Writer Citation: Dela Cruz, G.
