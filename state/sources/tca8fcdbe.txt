Manage your subscription Your newsletters Subscriber benefits Sign out Subscribe now Leader and Technology AI’s stunning progress in maths shows how little we understand it The biggest problem in maths has been cracked by AI, but it remains impossible to predict how its capabilities will advance in other fields By New Scientist 25 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print MARK SYKES/SPL/Alamy There is a story, possibly apocryphal, that when the mathematician G. H. Hardy was about to undertake a dangerous sea voyage, he sent a postcard to a colleague declaring “I have solved the Riemann hypothesis”. Hardy, a staunch atheist, joked that this would prevent his ship from sinking, as God would never let him claim the credit for such a major result without revealing the details of his proof. Hardy would probably be both astonished and aghast that we now have what seems to be a proof of the Riemann hypothesis , a problem that has vexed mathematicians since the 19th century, but that no one yet understands it. The consensus among mathematicians is that the proof, produced with the aid of AI, will hold, but that it will take much more work to make it comprehensible to humans. However you feel about AI, this is a watershed moment, and one that demonstrates just how little we still understand about the capabilities of the technology. As recently as last month, leading mathematician Terence Tao speculated that AI would eventually solve the Riemann hypothesis, but that it may not happen before 2050. Now, some mathematicians are considering whether their hardest and most infamous remaining problems could be settled within months. In a year that has left mathematicians reeling, this may be the knock-out blow. In the past year, AI has seen incredible advances in both maths and coding, but it is perhaps no coincidence that in these arenas, with nothing more than computer code, answers can swiftly be checked and iterated upon, allowing for rapid improvement. When it comes to other human endeavours though, from art to zoology, such verification is far harder, involving either the messy physical world, or unknowable human taste – or both. Whether this verification gap can be closed may become the biggest question of our age. Answers on a postcard, please. What is the Riemann hypothesis and why is its solution so monumental?
