Photograph: Prime Minister's Prizes for Science Australia news Researcher who helped improve melanoma survival rates among winners of top Australian science prize Georgina Long and subatomic physicist Anthony Thomas recognised in Prime Minister’s Prizes for Science Get our daily political email , free app or daily news podcast Donna Lu Assistant editor, climate, environment and science Tue 24 Nov 2026 06.00 EST Last modified on Tue 24 Nov 2026 06.01 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google When Prof Georgina Long was starting out in melanoma treatment and research, “my waiting room would change so rapidly”. Referring to her patients with stage 4 melanoma, the oncologist and medical director at the Melanoma Institute Australia said she wouldn’t “see the same face for more than nine months, 12 months maximum”. “We’ve flipped that … now over 50% of people are cured with melanoma that once was almost universally terminal,” Long said. ‘Bespoke’ mRNA vaccine significantly reduces risk of melanoma recurrence or death, clinical trial shows Read more Her research, which has led to effective drug therapies for the cancer, has improved five-year survival rates 10-fold for people with stage 4 melanoma. In recognition of her pioneering immunotherapy work, Long was one of two researchers awarded the top gong at the Prime Minister’s Prizes for Science on Tuesday night. The prize was also awarded to Prof Anthony Thomas, a subatomic physicist at the University of Adelaide whose work has “redefined humanity’s understanding of the proton, dark matter and neutron stars, helping unlock the mysteries of the cosmos”. The predictions of his “ cloudy bag model ” have led to experiments around the world, he said in a statement, including “to understand the results of supercomputer calculations of quantum chromodynamics”. ‘Huge honour’ Long said receiving the $250,000 prize was “a huge honour”. “Although it’s awarded to me, it really does belong to a lot of people,” she said, crediting her colleagues, international research collaborators and patients. “What we do in melanoma has impacted other cancers,” said Long, who was named Australian of the Year in 2024 alongside the late Prof Richard Scolyer . “Immunotherapy started in melanoma – that’s the cancer that set the bar.” Last month, Long announced the results of a clinical trial which found that intismeran, a personalised mRNA-based melanoma vaccine , significantly reduced the risk of cancer recurrence or death within two years. The research – which did not form part of her prime minister’s prize nomination – was the first time an individualised cancer vaccine showed positive results in a phase 3 clinical trial . “It’s taken decades to try to outsmart cancer,” she said. “It’s still difficult – we’re not curing the other 50% [of melanoma]. The co-founder of six biotechnology companies, Smythe has turned Australian research into approved medicines for psoriasis and polycythaemia vera, a slow-growing blood cancer. Among the other award recipients was associate professor Fengwang Li of the University of Sydney, who won the Malcolm McIntosh prize for physical scientist of the year. Li was recognised for his expertise in converting carbon emissions into clean energy and sustainable manufacturing materials. Li “solved a longstanding international roadblock in carbon capture known as the ‘carbonate paradox’, where captured carbon dioxide dissolves into carbonate salts that escape into the atmosphere”. Photograph: Prime Minister's Prizes for Science The prizes, now in their 27th year, are Australia’s most prestigious awards for achievements in scientific research, innovation and teaching, and the practice and preservation of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander knowledge systems. The science minister, Tim Ayres, said the prizes recognise “Australians from different backgrounds, disciplines and perspectives whose work is strengthening Australia’s scientific capability”. “This year’s recipients demonstrate the extraordinary breadth and depth of Australian science, and I congratulate each of them,” Ayres said in a statement.
