As the New Scientist Book Club reads it, author Kit Chapman makes a case for the transformative powers of urine By Kit Chapman 27 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print A detail from Joseph Wright’s 1771 painting The Alchemist Discovering Phosphorus, showing Henning Brand in 1669 Science History Images/Alamy Imagine you could boil the development of science down to a single ingredient. A magical, mystical compound that transformed human history. Of course, science doesn’t work that way; even if breakthroughs shift the paradigm of our understanding, discovery doesn’t progress like a tech tree in a computer game, but as an interweaving tapestry of complementary and competing ideas and experimentation. Theories can bubble under the surface for centuries, overlooked because of politics or failure to realise their implications, while innovations can emerge from taking an idea in one industry and transplanting it to another. But, for a moment, try and picture what just one such ingredient could be. What would you pick? For centuries, in medieval Europe, alchemists quested for the “Philosopher’s Stone” that would finally help them achieve their dreams of transmutation. It was a search doomed to failure from the very beginning. And yet, a far better idea was under their noses – or, rather, their underpants – the entire time. My pick? Urine has a strong case for being the most transformative substance in human history. It required the first chemical reaction we mastered – fire – to keep us warm, cook our food and smelt ores. But leatherwork played an equally key role. Urine is a source of urea, which bacteria can break down into ammonia, granting early humans easy access to a base that could soften organic matter and tan hides. Across the world, leatherwork became the cornerstone of survival for every civilisation, from the Americas to Europe, Africa and Asia: even cultures that didn’t develop a bronze age, such as the Aztec, or skipped it entirely, as seen in Sub-Saharan Africa, relied on leatherwork for clothes, structures, industry and commerce. In ancient Rome, urine was so useful for laundries to keep togas whiter-than-white that Emperor Vespasian levied a tax on draining urinals. When his son complained, Vespasian’s response was blunt: pecunia non olet , money doesn’t stink. Part of the funds were used to build the Colosseum in Rome, today recognised as one of the wonders of the world. The magic of urine filtered into what would become chemical science, too. Cheimeia , the artisanal trade secrets and recipes found in Alexandria, Egypt, that would later give rise to alchemy, recognised the value of urine. In the Leiden Papyrus X , part of a cluster of the only original manuscripts on alchemy’s origins we still have, you can find a recipe for faking gold using polysulfides. In it, the recipe suggests using the “urine of a small child”, although today our understanding of the chemical reaction that creates the effect means we know water is a better alternative. It was still used for laundry and leatherwork, but was now also part of the burgeoning gunpowder industry. One of the key ingredients of this game-changing superweapon, the origins of which lie in Chinese herbal medicine, is saltpetre, or potassium nitrate. This was gathered from nitre beds, stinking manure heaps mixed with potash and mortar, all moistened with urine to claim the nitrates. This method, used from Europe to Japan, continued well into the 19th century; during the US Civil War, the Confederacy was using urine to keep its guns firing. But perhaps urine’s greatest contribution came from alchemy. By the 17th century, just as the printing press and improvements in lenses and glassware had finally begun to usher in the scientific method, the old practices and beliefs that blended experiment, philosophy, religion and misunderstanding of the ancient world had their greatest triumph. In 1669, an alchemist in Hamburg, Hennig Brand, was searching for the philosopher’s stone using 1500 gallons of fermented urine he had collected in his basement. To Brand’s astonishment, the concentrated urine at the heart of his experiment began to glow. Brand kept his accomplishment secret for years, but unable to take the next step and turn metals into gold, he eventually sold the recipe to a friend and the discovery was shared with the world. Brand hadn’t performed a magic trick, but isolated phosphorus – the first chemical element to be discovered in modern times. Today, urine still plays a critical role around the world, particularly in medicine, where it is a source of biomarkers used in diagnostic tests, and was even the original source of drugs such as human chorionic gonadotropin and follicle-stimulating hormone for fertility treatment. As Emperor Vespasian said almost 2000 years ago, money doesn’t stink – and nor do the benefits of the amber liquid we all produce every day.
