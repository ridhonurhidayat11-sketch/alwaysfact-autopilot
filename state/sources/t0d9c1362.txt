Credit: Igor Andreoni/Peter Shawhan To err is human, science endeavours included. Accounts of missteps in the laboratory or field abound among early-career researchers, as do tales of what they learnt from them. But senior scientists make mistakes, too — and not just early in their careers. Sometimes, reaching a dead end was the only way around a problem. Other times, graduate students saved their advisers from throwing away ‘wrong’ data. Senior scientists also know the dangers of overthinking and how it can lead to experimental paralysis. “Every year, I devote a meeting with students and postdocs to discuss failures and how errors of judgement or methods are part of academic life,” says Catherine Badgley, a palaeontologist at the University of Michigan in Ann Arbor. “I hope this helps them to see they can learn from mistakes rather than be devastated by them.” Nature ’s careers team spoke to Badgley and four other senior scientists to hear about their ‘failures’ and the lessons they took from them. PETER SHAWHAN: Learning to find nothing is a skill Physicist at the University of Maryland in College Park and a deputy spokesperson for the Laser Interferometer Gravitational Wave Observatory (LIGO) scientific collaboration. For the past 26 years, I have been searching for gravitational waves with LIGO, which has a gravitational-wave observatory in Livingston, Louisiana. These waves are echoes of massive events such as the merging of black holes or the collision of neutron stars. They produce ripples in space-time and, by the time these tiny ripples reach Earth, they are so faint that scientists need extra help to ‘hear’ them, because they are extremely hard to detect by just using light, or telescopes, to see them. For example, observations of a collision of two neutron stars with telescopes has revealed the presence of gold and platinum atoms, solving a long-standing question: what is the origin of about half of all elements heavier than iron? The detection of such an event with LIGO would get us even more information. In the first tests of LIGO, the team found nothing. Later, with improved detectors, we still found nothing. In fact, we got very good at reporting nothing. We became experienced with knowing what we could rule out. By the time we made our first wave detection, in 2015, a finding that earned LIGO founders a physics Nobel in 2017, we had published about 100 papers reporting null results. One such null case was in 2010. We were completing a science run, in which data are collected for a set period of time, and had a candidate for a striking event — it looked like we’d finally got there. But we knew it might be a ‘blind injection’, which is when a false signal is inserted into a data set by authorized colleagues to test our analyses. We finished the analysis, drafted the paper and then asked the LIGO director to open a sealed envelope to tell us whether this was a real event or not. It wasn’t, but the exercise showed a real problem: the sky location our analysis recovered didn’t match the signal’s known location. Two of the detector’s mirrors had moved in the wrong direction. We found the glitch in about a day. This showed we could efficiently self-correct when detecting a real sign — which happened five years later. I tell my students that scientists make lots of mistakes. We’re careful with cross-checks and analyse results carefully, so we catch almost all of them. Some are caught in minutes, some in days and some take more work. This one, because we’d built a test designed to assess itself, took about a day. That’s really the goal: not avoiding mistakes, but rather building enough ways to catch them quickly. Knowing what it means to find nothing is important; it’s a real scientific skill. Chemist Omar Yaghi (second row, fifth from right), with his team at Tsinghua University in Beijing, says that professors can learn important lessons from their students if they listen to them. Credit: Yaghi Group Photo OMAR YAGHI: A fixed mindset is the enemy of discovery Chemist at Tsinghua University in Beijing and a co-recipient of the 2025 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work with metal–organic frameworks. Back in the 1990s, I was an assistant professor at Arizona State University in Tempe. My team had begun research trying to make inorganic porous materials. These are solid materials with a network of tiny, connected pores that act like molecular sponges to trap molecules, such as wastewater contaminants and toxic gases, on their surfaces. We were successful: we had a paper 1 published in the Journal of the American Chemical Society and, later, in Science 2 . Then a doctoral student came looking to join my lab. She didn’t sound very confident, her English was not so good and it wasn’t the right time of year to look for a PhD adviser. I made up some reason not to take her. But she kept coming back and was adamant about working with me. So I accepted her into my lab group, and she started working on metal–organic compounds, another type of porous material. I felt that this was a waste of time: researchers had known about these structures since the 1950s, but nothing useful had resulted from them. Once you emptied their solvent-filled pores to trap other molecules, such as gases, they collapsed and became useless. I used to call them sculptures: they had beauty, but no function. She kept at it, and I held onto my scepticism. After a year, we sat down to discuss her results. That was when I realized that there was an opportunity there: we could make robust metal–organic compounds, after all. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are also materials full of tiny pores. But what makes them particularly interesting is that scientists can adjust the size and shape of those pores, as well as the chemical properties inside them. This makes MOFs more tunable than inorganic porous materials such as zeolites and much better at trapping some gases. Some MOFs can drive chemical reactions, some conduct electricity and some are stable enough to be reused. My student found strong chemical bindings that gave stability to early versions of these structures and, within a very short period, our findings were published in Nature 3 . It had worked. Decades later, I met with my former student, and she told me that she had been struggling back then and wanted to try her luck with me, a new professor at the time, because no one else would take her in. I think science is the greatest equalizer. One discovery can take you from humble beginnings, as it did for us, and propel you to the top of your profession. I had an opinion about an area of research and didn’t listen to my student, and, to that student’s credit, she persisted. To my credit, I changed my thinking. Neither the student nor the professor can afford to do science without the other.
