The extensive damage was caused when a Nasa spacecraft hit the asteroid Dimorphos in 2022. The asteroid posed no threat, but this was a test of our planetary defences to see if it was possible to knock a space rock off course. Four years on, a European Space Agency (ESA) spacecraft called Hera is scouring the scene to fully assess the aftermath of the collision. "The crater is almost as large as the body itself, which is really, really spectacular," said Dr Sabina Raducan, a planetary scientist, who is on the Hera science team. "It's hard to describe in words all the excitement to actually finally see what happened. And as a crater person, I can also say it's beautiful." The original 2022 crash test was carried out by a Nasa spacecraft called Dart which hurtled into Dimorphos at 14,000mph (22,000km/h). It was such a big smash-up that the trail of debris, measuring about 6,200 miles long (10,000km), was spotted by telescopes on Earth and in space. Dimorphos is about 150m (500ft) wide and was partly chosen as the target because it is one of an asteroid pair - it orbits around a larger space rock called Didymos, which is about 730m (2,400ft) wide. The crash showed it was possible to change the path of the smaller asteroid. Before the collision, it took Dimorphos 11 hours and 55 minutes to orbit around Didymos. After the crash, the duration of its orbit had shrunk by about 33 minutes. Figure caption, Watch: Moment Nasa spacecraft leaves stadium-sized crater in asteroid A key objective of the Hera mission is to measure the mass of Dimorphos, in order to work out how effective the spacecraft's shove was. This information would be vital if we ever spotted a space rock that was on a collision course with Earth. "It would help us to predict what would happen if we had to hit another asteroid," said Prof Simon Green, a planetary scientist who is on Hera's science team. "We [could] measure its properties and say: 'Well, we need this mass of spacecraft, travelling at this speed, this early, in order to deflect it enough so that it misses the Earth'." Hera's arrival on the scene is helping scientists answer many more questions too. Not only did the collision alter the shape of Dimorphos, it also changed the way it moves. "Before the Dart impact, Dimorphos was more like our own Moon - so it was always showing the same face to Didymos, and it was behaving quite nicely," explained Raducan. "Now it's very, very chaotic - it's tumbling. Imagine a barrel that you throw down the stairs, and the barrel is rolling and it's moving, and basically that's what Dimorphos does right now around Didymos." Hera has also taken detailed images of Didymos, revealing its surface in detail. "The gravity at the surface of Didymos is about 40,000 times less than the gravity on Earth," said Naomi Murdoch, who is on the Hera mission team. "We can already see some fascinating and complex geology. We can see regions of boulders. "The smallest details that we can make out in these images are about 3m in size. So that means that all the boulders that you can see with your eyes are at least 3m big, and some as big as 10m. So what we're seeing here is boulders the size of cars and buses sitting on a world with almost no gravity." Image source, ESA Image caption, The mission has sent back new pictures of the smaller Dimorphos orbiting around Didymos The Hera mission will spend the next six months studying Dimorphos and Didymos, sending back more images taken from closer up in higher resolution. "Hera is a sort of Inspector Columbo going back to the crash scene to investigate what is the outcome of this impact," said Patrick Michel, principal investigator of ESA's Hera mission. The spacecraft is packed with instruments and also has two shoe-boxed sized satellites onboard, which can study the impact site up close, and then later land on the asteroid. But the first images taken by Hera have also revealed a new challenge. Dimorphos and Didymos are travelling through space surrounded by hundreds of boulders ranging in size from tens of centimetres to metre-size rocks. This will make it especially challenging to operate the spacecraft and cube satellites through this boulder field. Image source, NASA/Hubble Space Telescope Image caption, A long trail of debris from the collision was spotted by the Hubble Space Telescope Image source, NASA/Johns Hopkins APL Image caption, This was the last image from Dart before it crashed into Dimorphos in 2022 Asteroids pose a significant threat to our planet. The biggest, which measures more than a kilometre across, could cause global devastation. But medium-sized asteroids around the same dimensions as Dimorphos could wipe out a city. "We all know what happened to the dinosaurs, right? And we don't want that to happen to us," said Carolyn Ernst at Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory, who has worked on both the Dart and Hera missions. "The good news for us is that there are far fewer potential threats out there in the Solar System than there were millions of years ago. But there are objects out there." She said the biggest asteroids have been spotted, and none are heading to Earth. "The smallest stuff isn't too much of a threat because our atmosphere protects us," she added. "But there's a size range in the middle where we don't know where all of them are, and it could, if impacted, cause problems for humanity. And if we can find out about that and know about that, well, we sure as heck would want to try to stop that." Image source, ESA Image caption, Artist's impression showing Hera. It has two small satellites on board to study the asteroid up close Scientists want to gather as much intelligence as they can about space rocks to make us as well equipped as possible to deal with whatever the Universe throws at us. A new mission is launching in 2027 to track potentially hazardous asteroids - a space telescope called the Near-Earth Object (NEO) Surveyor. There are also more visits planned to asteroids, including to one called Apophis, which is about 450m (1,480ft) long. Apophis was discovered in 2004 and it was initially thought it might hit the Earth as early as 13 April 2029. But now scientists understand its orbit better, and say it won't hit us for at least the next 100 years. Instead of a crash in 2029, it will fly just 32,000km (20,000 miles) above our surface. This will give scientists the rare chance to study a space rock up-close. Two spacecraft will head to Apophis, including Nasa's Osiris-Apex (which previously grabbed a sample from another asteroid called Bennu) and Ramses, a joint mission between ESA and the Japanese Space Agency (JAXA).
