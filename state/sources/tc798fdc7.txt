Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Rapidly warming sea temperatures and marine heat waves could eliminate South Korea’s domestic catch of the Pacific flying squid by the 2040s, according to a recent study, reports contributor Kelly Kasulis Cho for Mongabay. The Pacific flying squid ( Todarodes pacificus ) has long been a culturally vital and affordable dietary staple across the Korean peninsula. However, data from South Korea’s national statistics service reveals that current domestic harvest volumes have decreased to just one-sixteenth of levels recorded in 2002. Between 2023 and 2025 , prominent national fisheries failed to produce 10% of the annual yield recorded in the peak harvest year of 1999, when the catch reached 225,122 metric tons. Between 2022 and 2026, fresh squid prices surged by more than 40% , according to the Korea Agro-Fisheries & Food Trade Corporation. Over the past decade, sea surface temperatures surrounding the Korean Peninsula rose by 0.7° Celsius (1.26° Fahrenheit), more than double the global average. The study by researchers at Pukyong National University (PKNU) in Busan predicted that South Korea could reach a “zero-catch state” between 2043 and 2048, depending on future global greenhouse gas emissions scenarios. “Long-term warming is making traditional fishing grounds less suitable, while marine heat waves — periods of unusually warm seawater lasting at least five consecutive days — add episodes of intense heat stress,” said Kim Kyung-hoi, corresponding author of the study and ocean engineering professor at PKNU. Kim added that shifts in ocean currents and wind patterns carry additional warm waters into the East Sea, exacerbated periodically by climate variability during La Niña events. The thermal disruption is driving northward migrations of squid and ecological shifts in fish species occupying the East Sea. Kim Tae-won, a marine science professor at Inha University in Incheon, who wasn’t involved in the PKNU study, said that squid are highly mobile organisms whose migration routes and spawning grounds respond rapidly to water temperature shifts. As Pacific flying squid stocks shift northward toward Siberian and Arctic waters, warming conditions in the East Sea have simultaneously fueled an influx of predatory fish species such as the Japanese amberjack ( Seriola quinqueradiata ). Local fishing communities along South Korea’s eastern coast are experiencing economic distress. Jung Suk-geun, a marine life science professor at Jeju National University, said cold-water marine species including walleye pollock ( Gadus chalcogrammus ) and sailfin sandfish ( Arctoscopus japonicus ) have already virtually vanished from the East Sea. Jung pointed out that local squid fisheries around Ulleung Island have suffered widespread bankruptcy, forcing operators to abandon vessels and harbor infrastructure despite government relief subsidies.
