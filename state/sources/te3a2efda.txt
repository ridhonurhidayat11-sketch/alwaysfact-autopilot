Rohingya refugees are using traditional agroecology techniques on agroforestry plots, such as multi-layered intercropping, organic composting, and seed saving, to heal the degraded landscape and grow food amid deep cuts to international food aid. Supported by NGOs like UNHCR and FAO, large-scale reforestation projects have planted nearly one million tree seedlings and restored 200 hectares of camp land, using a mix of fast-growing species and deep-rooted native trees, including fruit trees, to stabilize the sandy slopes and prevent landslides. While reforestation efforts in the camp are showing measurable success, the long-term survival of forests is impacted by funding shortages and a lack of secure land rights for refugees. See All Key Ideas KUTUPALONG REFUGEE CAMP, Bangladesh —”It is like a jail, but it isn’t a jail,” says Roshid Ahamed Bin, a 47-year-old Rohingya farmer, living in what is now a refugee camp in Bangladesh. It is far from the farmland where he used to live in Myanmar, before he and thousands of Rohingya people like him fled persecution eight years ago. Here, amid the wo rld’s largest refugee settlement w ith over 1.1 million refugees , space is tight and evergreen forests have paid the price. But families are combining NGO-funded farming projects with traditional Rohingya agroecology techniques to create agroforestry plots that are restoring land while helping refugees become self-sufficient in response to deep cuts in international food aid. “I feel so bad here as I have no land, but I am keeping my ancestors’ heritage alive. Growing these crops reminds me of the life we had in Myanmar. Here, life is difficult,” said Amina Khatun, a 58-year-old refugee living in Camp 1W, a sector of the sprawling settlement. Before the 2017 influx, Kutupalong refugee camp in Cox’s Bazar was a tropical evergreen forest, dominated by Dipterocarpus species, and sanctuary for endangered Asian elephants ( Elephas maximus ). To make way for the massive refugee settlements, the Bangladeshi government allocated roughly 2,500 hectares (6,100 acres) of protected forest land, and refugees and aid agencies cleared the vegetati on to build shelters and provide firewood . Over 1,500 hectares of forest were cleared in total. The rain, with nowhere to soak in, ran straight down the slopes. Landslides became a seasonal terror, sources told Mongabay. But in the past several years, on the hillsides where there was once bare, sandy earth and the blue-black glare of tarpaulin, there are now nurseries and gardens. Across the camps, more than 159,000 refugees are engaged in homestead gardening. Space is severely limited, and not all use agroecology techniques. Some farmers rely on chemical sprays and paddy fields to make ends meet. But among them, there are traditional food gardens and permaculture designs that avoid synthetic pesticides entirely, relying instead on intercropping, a mix of trees and organic soil management. Knowledge that crossed the border Mongabay sat with Roshid Ahamed Bin inside Camp 27. He remembers the farmland in Rakhine state, Myanmar, before 2017. Despite the confinement of the camp, Ahamed Bin has found a way to reconnect with the life he left behind. Like many Rohingya refugees here, he was a farmer in Myanmar. Now, he is among a growing number of refugees using agroforestry to restore the camp’s degraded landscape and feed their families. “Our land may be far away, but the farming passion of our ancestors remains part of our lives,” he told Mongabay. Back in Myanmar, farming was not just a livelihood for his family, but a way of life rooted in the land. His family grew rice, vegetables, and fruit, using layered intercropping and agroforestry. They planted local varieties of banana, papaya, and mango trees directly beside their vegetable gardens to maximize space and provide shade. They removed insects by hand, added animal manure and composted leaves to keep the soil alive, and saved seeds from healthy crops. Several U.N. agencies and NGOs are providing support to these efforts and they actively integrate Rohingya traditional knowledge into their programs. The UNHCR SuLMER project has rehabilitated the Shoa Ma’r Jhiri stre am, stabilized slopes, and introduced kitchen gardens, relying on community input to decide what to plant. CARE’s training in ‘sack and vertical gardening’ builds on the Rohingya tradition of maximizing tiny spaces. Similarly, when FAO and WFP distribute seeds, they rely on the refugees’ traditional knowledge of seed-saving and intercropping. Together, these programs have engaged tens of thousands of refugees. “They have skills in planting, fertilizer, pest control, harvesting, timing,” said Jahir Ahamed, a staffer at the UNHCR SuLMER prokect. “We use suitable practices in gardening activities.” Because families like his fled so quickly, they didn’t bring their own seeds from Myanmar and rely on those from distributed. They grow many leafy vegetables and gourds, like amaranth and Indian spinach. Mabia Khatun, 38, from Camp 17, recalls her own farm practices. “We saved seeds from healthy plants,” she said. A practice, they still engage in with seeds provided by NGOs. “Sometimes we exchanged seeds with neighbors. Our elders taught us to care for plants and protect the land.” Accordin g to FAO an d UNHCR reports , the restoration involves a mix of fast-growing tree species for quick ground cover and slower native species for long-term slope stability. On the ground, this looks like multi-layered intercropping. This method is highly strategic for the camp’s harsh environment. By stacking plants of different heights together, families can grow food in tiny spaces while the deep roots hold the sandy soil in place. “Every seed I plant reminds me of my homeland and gives me hope for tomorrow,” said Roshid Ahamed. Refugees plant tall fruit trees like mango and papaya to create canopy shade, then underplant them with climbing vegetables such as bottle gourd and pumpkin that use the trees and shelter walls for support. At the soil level, families replace chemical inputs with composted household waste and animal manure to rebuild fertility. The impacts, so far, are measurable, according to the organizations. The FAO reports that reforestation and land stabilization efforts have helped restore degraded forest land and improve ecosystem services. The use of native species protects local biodiversity while stabilizing the sandy, erosion-prone slopes that threaten shelters during the monsoon. Beyond just stabilizing the hillsides, these new trees are actively rebuilding the local ecosystem. As the roots spread and the canopy grows, the forest slowly returns, bringing back shade and moisture to an area that was stripped bare. The FAO has also piloted dry-season planting techniques inside the camps, achievin g a 100% seedlin g survival rate in trial sites by using water-efficient methods. An uphill battle But keeping agroecology traditions alive in Bangladesh required adapting to a new reality. While families brought generations of farming knowledge with them, they had to merge it with modern, large-scale restoration efforts to heal the devastated landscape.
