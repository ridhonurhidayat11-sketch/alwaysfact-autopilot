Chapter 44!’ By Alison Flood 25 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print The New Scientist Book Club read Chris Beckett’s Dark Eden in November Since starting the New Scientist Book Club in May 2023, I have aimed to vary our books as much as possible, moving between science fiction and popular science, older classics and the hottest new titles. Occasionally, though, I’m afraid I let the power go to my head and just choose one of my own personal favourites. That’s what I did in November, with Chris Beckett’s novel Dark Eden , a book I’ve loved ever since it won the Arthur C. Clarke award in 2013. Set on the planet Eden, a sunless world that drifts alone far outside the Milky Way, this follows the descendants of two astronauts who were marooned there over a century earlier. Now numbering 532 people, they tell each other stories of the world their ancestors came from, and struggle to survive on this dark, cold planet as they await the return of “one of those sky-boats with lights on them”. I think it is all wildly imaginative and original and exciting. So it was with some trepidation that I awaited the verdict of my fellow book club members on our Discord channel. I really wanted everyone else to love it, too! Free newsletter Sign up to Book Club Sign up to newsletter It started well. “I managed not to stay up all night reading, but the temptation was definitely there – I am hooked,” wrote Dee55. Les was similarly gripped. “Started it? I’ve only gone and finished it, haven’t I? It really got me in, and I found it a fascinating exploration of social anthropology and linguistics in a very small, isolated population. And the world-building was fascinating, too – very different flora and fauna.” Advertisement Several other members also cited the strength of the world-building in Dark Eden . I also love the lanternflowers and woollybucks of this planet, and was fascinated to learn, in an essay Chris wrote for book club members, that, when creating a world for a story, he doesn’t “usually seek to make the whole thing scientifically accurate, but I try to make it hang together in a way that feels coherent”. I think he definitely succeeds in that here. In Dark Eden, I imagined how life might exist on a sunless planet Dark Eden, the November read for the New Scientist Book Club, takes place on a rogue planet outside the Milky Way. Award-winning author Chris Beckett explains how he thought through the science behind this night world There were some excellent debates about the language these people have developed – they double up on words for emphasis, for example (“big big”; “cold cold”), use bastardised forms of our words (“Landing Veekle”; “leckytrickity”), and talk about Starry Swirl – the Milky Way they can see in their skies, a wonderfully evocative image. “I think Beckett has done something really clever here. He’s created a language that feels familiar enough to read naturally, but is sufficiently different to remind us that this is an entirely separate culture that has evolved in isolation,” wrote Maria. “I enjoyed some of the language. Tom’s dick and Harry’s for instance,” wrote Matthew – referring to an exclamation the characters use for emphasis. “It’s contagious… yesterday I found myself saying Wow, it’s hot hot!” added leasabp. Dee55 also admitted to adopting some Edenisms: “My favourite bit of the language is ‘newhairs’! I am already using this for teenagers, but only to myself at present.” The story’s protagonist is John Redlantern, a young man who decides there must be more to life than waiting for rescue around the Circle of Stones that marks the original astronauts’ landing point on Eden, and tries to spark an exodus from the Family’s small valley to explore the wider planet. John definitely drew some mixed opinions from readers. “Problem is, I don’t much like our ‘hero’, he’s not a very sympathetic character, more Sméagol than Bilbo,” wrote Matthew. I chatted to Chris in an interview for book club members, and he was certainly aware of this: “He’s an awkward cuss, John, isn’t he? He’s not a particularly nice person,” he told me – before embarking on an enlightening discussion about why he chose to write from various viewpoints, and how hard it was describing the world of Eden from the perspectives of people who have never been anywhere else. “The discipline of just writing it from within the story is if I’m trying to describe the world to readers, I can’t use metaphors and analogies that refer to anything that’s outside of Eden. And that was quite tricky to do.” Book club member Laura Akers, like others, said she “definitely enjoyed the story more when I realized that John, despite his own intentions, is not intended to be our ‘hero’”. Overall, I think the book club mostly really enjoyed Dark Eden – I particularly appreciated leasapb’s comment: “Holy cow. Chapter 44!!!”, as I think the ending is fabulous – but one issue that was raised by a few people was the amount the characters have sex with each other. Incestuous sex. “Certainly one of the odd things I found was the casual attitude to sex,” wrote Alan_P. Maria agreed. “I found the casual attitude towards sex quite unsettling too, particularly because the characters often seem almost childlike in their behaviour and understanding of the world, yet discuss sex so matter-of-factly. I honestly couldn’t bring myself to think too much about the incest!” And while Barbara Howe “loved the worldbuilding, and the story of John’s breakaway family’s trek to find a better place”, she found the “constant and highly unerotic sex… offputting”. But given that at least some members have gone on to read the sequels, I think this was largely a popular choice – and a welcome change from the Earthly dystopias which seem to be dominating the big new sci-fi novels out this autumn (I’m looking at you, Audrey Niffenegger and Emily St. John Mandel ).
