Science News from research organizations Even scientists can't agree on what science actually is Date: November 21, 2026 Source: Santa Fe Institute Summary: Scientists cannot agree on what science actually is, and a new study finds their views are surprisingly more divided than those of the general public. The findings could help explain public frustration with scientific uncertainty and raise questions about how artificial intelligence might reshape future discoveries. Share: Facebook Twitter Pinterest LinkedIN Email FULL STORY A surprising new study reveals that scientists disagree about the very nature of science more than the general public does. Credit: Shutterstock Scientists dedicate their careers to uncovering how the world works, yet they have surprisingly different opinions about what science actually means. New research reveals that scientists disagree about the nature and purpose of science even more than the general public does. The study, published in Royal Society Open Science , examined 18 different descriptions of science and found that researchers rarely embraced any of them without reservations. Even when scientists generally supported a particular definition, many questioned some aspect of it. "What was surprising to me was that there was quite a bit of disagreement between scientists, including scientists who are in the same subfield," says Marina Dubova, who led the research while working as a postdoctoral researcher at the Santa Fe Institute. Members of the public, by comparison, expressed more consistent views. They were more likely to regard science as a dependable way to discover objective truths about the world, placing less importance on the uncertainty and creativity involved in scientific research. These contrasting perspectives may have important consequences. They could affect the questions scientists choose to investigate and influence how people evaluate scientific findings. They may also help explain why the public sometimes becomes frustrated when researchers cannot offer clear answers or change their conclusions in response to new evidence. What Scientists and the Public Believe About Science Dubova, now an assistant professor of psychology at the University of California, Berkeley, worked with Princeton University researcher Qiawen E. Liu to investigate how people understand science. The researchers surveyed 91 scientists and 99 members of the public. Participants were asked to assess 18 descriptions of science drawn from historical perspectives, including comparisons of scientific research to detective work, a pursuit of truth, and a mirror that reflects reality. Both groups generally agreed that curiosity, exploration, and the desire to solve mysteries are central to science. Beyond those shared ideas, however, the scientists expressed strikingly different perspectives. Some researchers viewed science primarily as a means of discovering objective truths. Others placed greater importance on imagination, personal judgment, and the creative decisions involved in research. Scientists also differed over whether scientific progress depends mainly on following established procedures or adapting and improvising as new challenges arise. Dubova believes these disagreements may reflect the individual experiences that shape how researchers approach their work. Just as chefs can have very different philosophies about preparing food, scientists can develop distinct ideas about the best ways to investigate the world. Why the Public Sees Science Differently Another intriguing finding involved differences among scientific disciplines. The general public's understanding of science more closely resembled the views expressed by physicists and biologists than those of psychologists and other social scientists. One possible reason is that physics and biology receive greater attention in science education, potentially shaping how people imagine scientific research. Dubova emphasizes, however, that this explanation remains tentative and will require additional investigation. The differences between scientists and the public could also help explain misunderstandings about scientific uncertainty. People who expect science to produce firm, objective answers may have difficulty understanding why findings sometimes conflict or why scientific conclusions must be updated when new evidence becomes available. What the Findings Could Mean for AI and Scientific Discovery The research also points to a potential challenge as artificial intelligence becomes increasingly involved in scientific discovery. With researchers developing AI systems that can carry out scientific investigations, Dubova is concerned that automation could favor a limited conception of how science should work. If those systems are built around one particular approach to research, other valuable methods of discovery could receive less attention. The study received support from the Santa Fe Institute's Lou Schuyler Seed Grant Fund, which was created in 2022 to encourage exploratory projects by early career scientists. Dubova used the funding to pay participants for taking part in the research. For Dubova, the findings represent an initial effort to understand how scientists' underlying beliefs about their profession might influence the research they conduct and the discoveries they make. "Don't take the common understanding of science for granted," she says. "Always ask. Always be curious about what someone has in mind."
