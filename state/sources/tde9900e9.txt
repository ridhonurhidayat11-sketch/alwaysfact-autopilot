Got feedback? Take our survey . (See our AI policy here .) The February sun was setting outside her family’s West Virginia home when Rebecca Lindsey found out she was being fired in 75 minutes. Lindsey, who until early 2025 managed the federal government’s climate.gov website, had just begun to think her job might be safe during the second Trump administration. “Every week, it was like, ‘This is the week we’re all going to get fired,’ ” says Lindsey, who had worked as a contractor and later a civil servant at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration for nearly 15 years. “Every time you would hear the rumor and then it wouldn’t happen, you would think … maybe this is all just talk.” But at 3:45 p.m. on February 27, 2025, Lindsey and around 800 of her NOAA colleagues received a mass termination email saying they no longer “fit the Agency’s current needs.” In June, Lindsey’s sister and the data visualization lead for the project, Mary Lindsey, was fired alongside the remaining climate.gov contractors. The website URL was redirected, hiding the site — and its decades’ worth of climate visualizations, educational tools and extreme weather trackers — from the public. Hundreds of demonstrators protested outside NOAA headquarters in Silver Spring, Md., one week after the agency laid off some 800 staff. Chip Somodevilla/Getty Images The Lindsey sisters decided to fight back. Along with another terminated NOAA employee, they split a $65,000 grant from the nonprofit Surge Climate Talent and launched a crowdfunding campaign to rebuild and update climate.gov. The site, now called climate.us , notched one million views in its first month. But as proud as they are of their work, the project is a patch, not a fix. Rebecca Lindsey predicts it would take at least seven employees and $1.8 million annually to resume 80 percent of the old site’s work. “Climate.gov was more than just a website; it was a climate literacy program,” she says. To rescue the full mission, they still have a long way to go. Mary Lindsey (left) and her sister Rebecca Lindsey (right) have rebuilt a shuttered NOAA climate website. Courtesy of M. Lindsey and R. Lindsey The Lindsey sisters are far from the only tale of makeshift resilience. Over the last year, untold numbers of U.S. scientists and their supporters have rebuilt data dashboards, formed recommendation groups for childhood vaccines and screenings, and doled out research funds for hundreds of stranded early-career scientists. They’ve reconstituted committees to advise space missions, rehomed key reports on climate and Arctic science, and even turned to social media to spread public health alerts in the wake of a massive realignment of the federal science enterprise. “I don’t think anyone could have foreseen the amount of destruction and deliberate breaking down of norms and infrastructure” over the last two years, says Christina Madison, a clinical pharmacist and public health communicator known on social media as The Public Health Pharmacist. Since January 2025, the second Trump administration has frozen or rescinded billions of dollars in research funding on topics from climate change to vaccine hesitancy and is trying to redirect it — often without congressional approval — to administration priorities such as artificial intelligence and quantum computing. During deliberations for the FY2026 and FY2027 budgets, the White House proposed slashing future science funding by one-third. “When this was first starting, it really, truly felt like an alternate universe,” says Sean O’Leary, a pediatric infectious disease researcher at the University of Colorado Anschutz. “The science was always what drove the decision making, and now we’re at a place where it’s not.” In 2025 alone, the federal government shed some 20 percent of its science workforce. And in May 2026, the Office of Management and Budget proposed a sweeping set of rules to expand the authority of federal political appointees in selecting science grants. 20 percent Approximate proportion of the scientific workforce who left federal service in 2025 Yet some researchers see the destruction as an opportunity to rebuild their corners of U.S. science — or even reimagine it, says Michael Osterholm, an epidemiologist at the University of Minnesota in Minneapolis. “If there’s any good that can come out of what has been a horrible situation, it’s that it’s given us time and provided a pause to think through clearly: Is this the way we want to continue?” Show me the money University of Ottawa ecologist Julien Martin spent a decade building a respected lab that probed how social behaviors affect the evolution of bushy-tailed, cat-sized rodents called marmots. So when the National Science Foundation began its sweeping grant cuts, Martin found himself doing something unexpected: uploading cute marmot videos to OnlyFans, an online video subscription platform better known for hosting pornography.
