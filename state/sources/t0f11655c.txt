The court's decision follows a similar ruling over a proposed lease sale off American Samoa. Opposition groups who lodged the Northern Mariana Islands lawsuit said they were concerned deep-sea mining could cause long-term damage to the ocean ecosystem. The US government has said it wants to start deep-sea mining to enable access to critical minerals and to counter China's growing influence over crucial components needed for electronics. The administration had planned to put up for sale leases for the Northern Mariana Islands on 16 December 2026. It is not clear what will happen next as the administration chose not to recognise the first court ruling and went ahead with the sale of two leases covering 13 million hectares (more than 30 million acres) off American Samoa. Opponents have vowed to challenge that sale. Ruling on the Northern Mariana Islands case, District judge Jill Aiko Otake said the defendants, the National Marine Fisheries Service (NMFS), which provided the expert opinion behind the lease sales, and Howard Lutnick, who was named in his capacity as head of the department that oversees the agency, had failed to consider the potential effects that could follow once the leases were issued. The ruling explained that while these leases would only authorise the leaseholder to conduct preliminary surveying activity, they might eventually allow the leaseholder to actually mine the ocean's floor. The potential impact of that mining had not been addressed in the biodiversity assessment which was used to sign off the lease sales, the ruling said. Using machines to mine for minerals in the deep ocean is highly controversial. Some scientists have raised concerns that deep-sea mining could damage little-understood deep-ocean ecosystems, though industry supporters argue the environmental effects remain uncertain and require further study. Deep-sea mining uses vacuum technology to suck up nodules from the muddy sediment. , external However, this process also removes organisms that grow on them, including corals and sponges, many of which cannot grow anywhere else. In one study of the impact of deep-sea mining, researchers found more than 4,000 animals, 90% of which were new species, living on the seafloor in a remote area of the Pacific Ocean. Some critics of the industry fear that before we have the chance to explore the full nature of life in the undiscovered deep ocean, it could be endangered. Image source, Getty Images Image caption, The leatherback sea turtle is listed as endangered under the ESA Norway was the first country in the world to approve commercial-scale deep-sea mining in 2024 . Over 2,000 of the world's scientists met in 2025 to review the data on ocean health and they recommended governments halt deep sea exploration while further research on the impacts was carried out. More than 30 countries supported this position and called for a moratorium. Shortly before that meeting, President Trump published executive orders to push ahead with mining and "unleash America's offshore critical minerals and resources". He said "these resources are key to strengthening our economy, securing our energy future, and reducing dependence on foreign suppliers for critical minerals". Part of the plan aims to "counter China's growing influence over seabed mineral resources". Deep-sea mining targets polymetallic nodules , external – potato-sized rocks found over 4,000 metres below the ocean surface. These contain minerals including nickel, cobalt, and manganese which are used in manufactruring electronics and batteries. They are also important for renewable energy technologies to tackle climate change, including solar panels, wind turbines and electric vehicles.
