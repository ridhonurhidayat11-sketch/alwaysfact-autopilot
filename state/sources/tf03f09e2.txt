Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Cite article Timor-Leste’s booming swim-with-whales tourism industry is seeing growing numbers of operators and increasingly aggressive practices, raising concerns about its impact on migrating pygmy blue whales. New official guidelines aim to protect whales, but repeated breaches have exposed the lack of enforcement in an industry with no formal regulatory framework —the government is aiming for regulations to be legally reviewed by next year. Disagreements between operators and scientists over how closely tourists can approach whales highlight the challenges of balancing commercial interests with animal welfare. Conservationists and operators want licensing, monitoring and limits on boat numbers, and greater participation from Timorese in an industry dominated by foreign firms. See All Key Ideas DILI, Timor-Leste — As the annual whale migration gets into full swing in Timor-Leste, conservationists are raising concerns about increasingly aggressive tourist encounters with the world’s largest animals, despite new guidelines designed to protect them. Whale tourism is booming in a country whose waters are known as a “superhighway” for marine mammals . Migrating pygmy blue whales ( Balaenoptera musculus brevicauda ) are the star attraction for tourists seeking the experience of a lifetime. Though haze from wildfires in neighboring Indonesia made spotting whale spouts difficult in the earlier part of the season, industry-watchers have noticed a marked increase in the number of operators on the water this year. By the end of October, however, fewer whales had passed through the Ombai-Wetar Strait, which lies off the northern coast of Timor-Leste. This is likely because El Niño and the positive Indian Ocean Dipole have brought cooler waters in the region, delaying the migration of the whales, says cetacean ecologist Professor Karen Edyvane, who, in the past decade, has monitored migrating pygmy blue whales in Timor-Leste’s waters. This has meant more intense competition among whale tour operators to deliver experiences for customers, who pay $250-350 a day for the chance to swim with one of the few hundred pygmy blue whales that pass through each year. Industry-watchers have noticed cowboyish practices in the busiest season yet. Boats cutting across whales’ paths, operators pursuing mothers with calves, too many swimmers in the water at once — one tourist was taken out to swim with a whale on a jet ski — are among the reported violations. “We’ve seen the same bad behavior as in the last few years — just more of it with the new boats,” Tom Quinney, a photographer who on Nov. 12 documented nine boats chasing what was believed to be a pregnant whale off the coast of Dili, told Mongabay in a written message. Quinney’s drone footage captures tourists free-diving with the whale, swimming within a few meters of it, and another drone being flown just above its head. These practices breach guidelines released on Sept. 30 by the Timorese government and nonprofit Conservation International, with assistance from the Australian government and technical input from whale experts, including Edyvane. Swimming with whales can be conducted responsibly if science and evidence-based guidelines are followed, but poorly managed encounters can have serious consequences for the cetaceans, Edyvane says. Disruptions from boats and swimmers posing for Instagram photographs can cause pygmy blue whales to expend energy that they need to complete their annual migration from Indonesia’s Banda Sea, through Timor-Leste, to Australia. “Critically, many of the animals we see in Timor-Leste’s waters are nursing mothers and calves – animals which need to nurse and rest, without ongoing disturbance from tour boats,” Edyvane told Mongabay in a written message. From her whale monitoring field research station in Subaun on Timor-Leste’s northern coast, Edyvane has documented pygmy blue whales that are underweight, with their ribs and vertebrae visible. She suspects ocean warming is disrupting the upwellings that sustain their food supply. Whale tourism could provide a significant boost to Timor-Leste’s nascent tourism economy. But the industry will have no future if it disrupts the migration on which it depends, says Delio da Costa, project coordinator for the Solutions for Marine and Coastal Resilience in the Coral Triangle (SOMACORE) at Conservation International. Da Costa was part of the team that developed the new guidelines, which are an expanded version of recommendations published in 2020. They establish rules for approaching whales, including minimum distances and speed limits, and prohibit operators from chasing animals or blocking their paths. Monitoring the ‘sinners’ Despite repeated reports of breaches, there have been no apparent consequences for rogue operators. Timor-Leste lacks a formal regulatory framework for whale tourism, and no operator has been apprehended for misconduct in the decade since the industry emerged. It also lacks a clear avenue for complaints, which are directed instead to a general tourism information phone number and email. The government says it has yet to receive any whale-related complaints through these channels. But observers are increasingly calling out malpractice. Last December, toward the end of the 2025 season, photographer Ollie Clarke wrote an open letter to the industry after witnessing multiple operators flouting internationally recognized rules. He described a “race to the bottom” as new players flood the market. Also in December 2025, Quinney documented six boats pursuing a mother pygmy blue whale and her calf off the coast of the city of Manatuto. One of the most serious breaches this year involved a boat speeding into the path of a female pygmy blue whale and her calf. Drone footage taken by marine biologist Ywen Mieybégué, seen by Mongabay, identified the vessel as belonging to Timor Sunshine Divers, one of the country’s largest operators. Mongabay has approached Timor Sunshine Divers for comment, with no response. Pursuing mothers with calves can risk separating the young animals from their mothers, which occurred in the incident documented by Quinney in 2025. Separated calves are more vulnerable to predation and injury and may experience impaired growth, with potential consequences for whale populations over time. Quinney says his social media posts documenting poor practices last season prompted several overseas tour companies to reconsider their relationships with local operators. “Tour companies are starting to demand a consistent, ethical, guideline-adhering approach,” he told Mongabay. But an increasing number of tourists are avoiding operators altogether, paying fishermen as little as $50 to see whales, usually without government maritime safety inspections, equipment or briefings on responsible interactions. On Oct. 29, Edyvane said, she observed three local fishing boats — unregistered for carrying passengers — directly in front of the Subaun whale research field station, carrying nine fee-paying international tourists who were hoping to free dive with whales — another breach of the guidelines. From guidelines to regulation Simon Jeffrey, co-owner of Dive Timor Lorosae, says he operates ethically and wants to see formal regulation before the industry “eats itself.” The Australian national is working with authorities to provide an operator’s perspective on draft regulations for Timor-Leste’s whale tourism industry. Having conducted hundreds of tours during five years as an operator, Jeffrey told Mongabay by phone that aggressive maneuvering to guarantee encounters for clients can make whales harder to swim with.
