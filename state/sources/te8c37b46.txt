In a new study published in Earth’s Future on Tuesday, researchers find “cloud-to-ground” lightning strikes—bolts that hit Earth’s surface—could more than double by 2100 compared with historical levels. And the reason is our warming world. Lightning is already becoming more common: between 1995 and 2014, the researchers found, lightning across the world increased by 11 percent compared with the early 20th century. In the coming years, it could jump by between 76 percent and 140 percent over the 1995–2014 period, much more than previous estimates suggest. “I was quite surprised that the projections were so high,” said the study’s lead author Andreas Krause, who studies Earth sciences modeling at the Technical University of Munich, in a statement . “I expected smaller changes.” On supporting science journalism If you're enjoying this article, consider supporting our award-winning journalism by subscribing . The reason has to do with how lightning forms: as thunderstorm clouds develop, positively charged particles collect near the top of the cloud while negatively charged particles amass near the bottom. According to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, most lightning occurs inside these clouds. But sometimes negatively charged particles in the cloud can connect with positively charged particles on the ground, generating a thunderbolt—or “cloud-to-ground” lightning. How dangerous lightning is depends somewhat on where you live. In the U.S. lightning strikes kill an average of 21 people per year and cause an estimated 24,600 fires per year. Countries such as India see a disproportionately high number of lightning strikes each year. As the climate warms, the study authors found, heat rising from Earth’s surface is more prone to shake up the atmosphere, creating more potential for electric fields—and cloud-to-ground lightning—to form. Places with cooler climates, such as Alaska, stand to see the greatest rate of increase in lightning from previous levels.
