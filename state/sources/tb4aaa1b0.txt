Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Suncor, Canada’s second-largest oil company, wants to release treated wastewater from mining oil in Alberta’s tar sands into a drinking water source for the community of Fort Chipewyan. Investigative reporter Leana Hosea visited the community to learn more. She joins the Mongabay Newscast this week to detail what she found . “This story is not about pro-oil and anti-oil. It’s about waste, not wanting toxic waste, which they don’t know how well it’s going to be treated,” Hosea says. “It’s never happened before. Being released into the Athabasca River, which flows into Lake Athabasca, which is where [the community] all drink from. And because treatment is difficult, it’s uncertain whether they’re going to be able to clean the water to those standards.” While Suncor never responded to Hosea’s request for an interview, Chief Allan Adam of the Athabasca Chipewyan First Nation was told the treated wastewater, or tailings, was “too corrosive” to be used on the oil refinery machinery. While the provincial government of Alberta backs the strategy to release the tailings without an environmental impact assessment, the federal government of Canada will have the final say. In August 2026, Chief Adam wrote to the federal government, urging it to intervene. “When I ran for chief, I was given a mandate of what I needed to get accomplished. I was not given the mandate to agree to a treat and release of a tailings pond, which is deadly toxic. No, we don’t want it,” he told Hosea. Independently, the federal government is currently investigating the link between potential health impacts of pollution from oil extraction on the community of Fort Chipewyan, which was previously noted by local physician John O’Connor, who noticed high rates of cancer in the community, including the rare bile duct cancer. “The Alberta Cancer Board did a study, found 30% higher rates of cancer, including this rare bile duct cancer,” Hosea says. “In 2014, they did another follow-up study and found higher rates of cervical cancer than the rest of the province. But what they didn’t do and what they refused to do was look at the possible sources of these high rates of cancer.” While the Alberta government said there are no links between oil extraction and the health impacts that the community of Fort Chipewyan is facing, a 10-year study backed by C$12 million ($8.4 million) in federal funds is set to commence. “In September this year, it had been announced that the … federally funded health study would start to try and look at the connections between the environment and people’s health in Fort Chipewyan,” Hosea says. “But of course, 10 years is a long time, and they’ve been waiting for decades.” The Alberta government recently voted on a referendum on whether to secede from Canada, in part due to public sentiment over wanting looser environmental regulations for oil extraction; voters ultimately rejected the measure. Still, the federal government under Prime Minister Mark Carney has so far shown support for Alberta’s oil industry, Hosea says. “[Y]ou hear of the Alberta government making changes and making laws to accommodate oil and gas industry, and Canada trying to appease them in regards to making Canada’s rules lighter,” Chief Adam says. “[It] don ’ t help us in any way, form, or fashion whatsoever. And it’s unfortunate that our people have always been in the line of fire.” Please take a minute to let us know what you think of our podcast here .
