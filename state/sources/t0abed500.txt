It may have just been settled with the help of AI By Matthew Sparkes 20 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print The Riemann hypothesis describes a pattern within the prime numbers ROBERT BROOK/SCIENCE PHOTO LIBRARY/Alamy A mathematician at AI company Anthropic claims to have discovered, and verified with formalisation, a proof of the Riemann hypothesis – one of the most infamous and thorny problems in all of mathematics. The Riemann hypothesis was first put forward in 1859 by German mathematician Bernhard Riemann. It proposes an origin for the prime numbers, namely that they follow a certain pattern. The question is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute Millennium Prize Problems, which will award a $1 million prize for an accepted solution. Read more OpenAI has dumped 722 maths papers – now it must clean up the mess Levent Alpöge at Anthropic published a statement today claiming to have solved the problem with the aid of Anthropic’s Claude AI. Advertisement “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann hypothesis,” writes Alpöge. “It is natural to feel that this is an end, in my case a loss of a principal guiding light, but what I saw during the course of progress so amazed me that I believe it is one dawn across science of something far greater.” In August, Anthropic announced previous work on the Riemann hypothesis , but that stopped short of a full proof. Alpöge hasn’t published a human-readable version of the proof, saying it would be “obviously inappropriate to attempt to post some massive unreadable travesty of ‘slop'”, though he says an unpublished version ran to more than 250,000 pages. Instead, Alpöge has released a version of the proof written in Lean, a computer code mathematicians use to mechanically verify proofs as a series of logical steps in a process called formalisation. This process is so thorough that if a theorem comes through intact, meaning that the code compiles, it is considered to have been proved correct beyond all reasonable doubt. However, the process isn’t without vulnerabilities, and the code and mathematics will need to be thoroughly checked by humans. “Let’s see if it compiles first,” says Kevin Buzzard at Imperial College London, reacting to the news. “Might be a while before we find the relevant experts who are able to tell us the thrust of the argument, assuming it’s all true, of course, which it probably will be because of course Alpöge will have [a] huge amount of egg on [his] face if the Lean code doesn’t compile or is deficient in some way.” A golden age of maths is dawning and mathematicians are freaking out Mathematicians are stunned at the progress AI is making in solving advanced problems, leaving some questioning whether there will still be room for humans Alpöge says the proof may also help solve the generalised Riemann hypothesis (GRH), which widens out the concept to different properties of prime numbers, and could expand our understanding of a broader family of mathematical structures. “There are numerous results that are conditional on the generalised Riemann hypothesis, and now those are theorems!” says James McKee at Royal Holloway University of London. “I suppose most mathematicians believed, or the more cautious ones merely suspected, that GRH was true, so its proof will not be unsettling. What is both unsettling and tremendously exciting, is the rapid progress in recent months towards automated proofs of significant mathematical results. It is both a wonderful time and a terrible time to be a research mathematician.” Alpöge has been involved in a number of recent discoveries involving AI. In July, he announced that the Jacobian conjecture – which academics have spent decades trying to prove was true – is actually false, giving a tiny, 216-character counterexample as proof . He and Tristan Buckmaster at New York University also announced that they had cracked three significant problems considered “stepping stones” to the Navier-Stokes problem, hours before OpenAI controversially revealed a full solution . Anthropic listed on the stock market on 19 November, and Alpöge appears to suggest that he waited to announce the proof until after. “The precise timing is horrible. If I could have, I’d have finished this all much earlier,” he writes, saying that he spoke to Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei and determined that “there was a constraint to respect” before the announcement could be made.
