The practice of using trained elephants is controversial and poses safety risks to both the elephants and the mahouts who work with them. Nepal’s working elephant populations are maintained through a breeding center near Chitwan National Park, but there’s a risk of population decline due to factors such as disease. New conservation techniques like genetic ID-based monitoring may come to rival the direct, elephant-borne approach for work such as the five-yearly rhino census. See All Key Ideas CHITWAN NATIONAL PARK, Nepal — Perched some 3 meters off the ground, or about 10 feet, on the back of Asian elephants, workers traverse Nepal’s dense jungles and grasslands to complete wildlife counts, rescue distressed animals, and manage wildlife such as tigers ( Panthera tigris ) and greater one-horned rhinos ( Rhinoceros unicornis ). Working elephants have long played an important role in the conservation of Nepal’s wildlife and wildlands. Their height and sharp sense of their surroundings keep researchers safe from tigers and other predators, and they’re also used on patrols. But dwindling captive elephant populations and opposition to using the highly intelligent animals as beasts of burden are changing the long-term outlook for working elephants and the men known as mahouts who work with them. Dr. Amir Sadaula works closely with elephants and mahouts as a veterinary officer at the National Trust for Nature Conservation (NTNC), which operates a biodiversity conservation center on the edge of Chitwan National Park. There, NTNC employs eight trained elephants and teams of mahouts to aid in wild animal rescue and research work. “Without [the] elephant, we could not do the rescue,” Sadaula says. Mahouts Promod Chaudhary, left, and Upendra Kachadiya enter a river for their elephants to bathe while returning from grazing in Chitwan National Park, Nepal, on May 19, 2026. Image by Henry Doellinger for Mongabay. In addition to elephants that aid researchers, privately owned elephants also walk the streets of Sauraha, a community adjacent to Chitwan. Tourism companies in the gateway community often own one or two elephants and offer elephant rides to tourists as a way to experience wildlife up close. Captive elephants around Chitwan typically work with one to three mahouts. Nepal’s Elephant Conservation Action Plan (2025-2035) recognizes the importance of maintaining the captive elephant population. It also acknowledges that elephants are vulnerable to diseases like tuberculosis and elephant endotheliotropic herpes virus, and that their treatment is dependent on limited funding and resources. “Captive elephants in Nepal do not appear to have a future in itself unless strategic management intervention is made,” the plan says. “Captive and trained elephant population is crucial for the management of Tarai national parks and wildlife reserves and for special works such as wildlife population monitoring, rescue and translocations, and patrolling.” The Tarai, or Terai Arc, is the lowland expanse that straddles southern Nepal and part of India, where iconic species like tigers and rhinos roam. In the field On a typical day, a couple of mahouts will take an elephant to harvest bundles of tall grass at the crack of dawn. Back at the stables, others bundle the grass with grain to feed the elephants. This structure can be hierarchical: senior mahouts ride while others work bundling food and cleaning stalls. Mahouts eat and change into fresh clothing before the bulk of their day’s work begins around 9:30 a.m. They heat water over a fire and sharpen sickles on a wet block. When they’re ready, they unchain the elephants, and command them to kneel on the grass, ready to accept a rider. The first and last stop of their day is in a river to bathe. The mahouts perch barefoot on the back of their elephant, balancing to avoid the water. At their command, the elephants roll and shower in the swirling stream. Clean and satisfied, the elephants exit the river and walk into the brush, grazing on the banks of tall grass until the late afternoon. During conservation work, the elephants’ days are busy. The NTNC facilitates a rhino census every five years across the country’s rhino habitat. The last was in 2021, employing 57 elephants and 350 people, 250 of whom conducted a count across of Chitwan National Park, according to the 2021 report by the NTNC. The next census was due this year, but has been pushed back to next year over budget delays. Mahouts pile bundles of grass in a rainstorm after a morning harvest. From the back of the elephants, census takers can look down and across the tall grasses to directly observe individual rhinos — something they wouldn’t be able to do if they were walking on the ground or driving in a jeep. Researchers say they prefer the direct observation method of counting rhinos, describing it as more accurate and effective. However, new technologies make it possible to determine the population size without actually laying eyes on each individual animal. ID-based monitoring uses field observations to create a genetic profile for each animal and allows the count to be made by analyzing dung samples or distinct markings. During direct observation, the elephant-borne census teams sweep through the forest until each rhino is found, recorded, and allowed to pass through their phalanx. This practice, according to researchers, mitigates the risk of inaccurate data. To ensure the reliability of the data, the process is intense. The elephants and staff may encounter the park’s wild elephants, such as Dhurbe, an adult bull notorious for aggressive encounters with humans. Dhurbe attacked and injured several census workers in 2021. Tigers, too, can pose a threat: one killed a mahout during the 2021 census in Bardia National Park, northwest of Chitwan. “We can’t go near elephants or tigers without elephants,” says Harindra Chaudhary, a mahout for more than three decades. “We can only dart an animal if it is within 30-foot [9-m] range only.” He adds this is important as the national parks don’t have much in the way of drivable roads. During tiger captures, the backs of elephants provide a necessary perch to locate, move, and dart the big cats. The hefty pachyderms also confer an aspect of security to the task that would not be possible on foot. “There are other animals also which can harm humans. So for the safety reason, especially, the elephant is for the safety,” Sadaula says. Welfare concerns Elephants and mahouts working for the NTNC live near the biodiversity conservation center. The elephants are kept chained to posts on mounds of dirt and sheltered by metal roofs. Their living conditions are similar to those of privately owned elephants or the elephants kept separately by the military, which uses the animals in antipoaching operations.
