While their appearances may be unusual, these formations offer scientists something far more valuable than a collection of curious snapshots: clues to the geological processes that shaped Mars over billions of years. In particular, the rocks and mineral deposits could help researchers piece together the planet's watery past and identify environments that may once have been capable of supporting microbial life. NASA's Curiosity rover recently wrapped up a drilling campaign at a location nicknamed "Nazca," where scientists have been examining unusual disk-shaped crystal formations embedded in the surrounding bedrock. Some of the features are so thin and flat that the mission team likened them to potato chips, according to a statement from the space agency. NASA's Curiosity rover captured a close-up view of unusual, disk-shaped crystal formations embedded in Martian bedrock using its Mars Hand Lens Imager (MAHLI) on Nov. 7, 2026 (Sol 5067). The thin, flattened formations resemble tiny potato chips. (Image credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech/MSSS) Curiosity used its Mars Hand Lens Imager (MAHLI), mounted on the end of its robotic arm, to capture detailed close-up images of the formations on Nov. 7 — the rover's 5,067th Martian day , or Sol 5067. The rover also used its ChemCam instrument to study the rocks' chemical composition, while samples from the Nazca drill site were delivered to its onboard Sample Analysis at Mars (SAM) instrument for further analysis.
