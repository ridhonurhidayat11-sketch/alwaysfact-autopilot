The blaze engulfed several barns at Hudson Valley Farms in upstate New York before firefighters could get inside. No information has been provided as to what caused the fire. Crews managed to rescue hundreds of young birds and hatchlings, and no workers or first responders were injured, according to local news outlets. Foie gras, made from the fattened liver of a duck or goose, has long drawn protests from animal rights groups because the birds are force-fed. Voters in both Washington DC and Denver backed bans on foie gras sales in this month's midterm elections. Image source, Getty Images Image caption, Hudson Valley Farms is in a legal battle with the state of New York over its foie gras ban Marcus Henley, manager of Hudson Valley Foie Gras in Ferndale, New York, told local news outlet Radio Catskill that the fire destroyed two buildings housing ducks and geese. "The building isn't there anymore," Henley said, adding that he suspected an electrical malfunction may have started the blaze, although this has not been confirmed. The company does not disclose how many birds it hosts at one time. Commercial poultry farms can typically house thousands of birds. Westchester News 12 reported that the emergency response to the fire in the early hours of Thursday morning involved 37 fire departments and 200 first responders. The fire spread rapidly, and the metal-clad, wood-frame structure of the building made it difficult for firefighters to stop the flames from spreading inside the barn. Hudson Valley Foie Gras and the local fire department did not respond to a request for comment. Foie gras producers have long been criticised by animal rights groups due to the production process, which involves the force feeding of birds through plastic tubes, which allows them to gain over a third of their body weight in a short period of time. New York moved to ban the product in 2019, and Hudson Valley farms have been in litigation with the state since. At a city council hearing in 2019, Henley argued against the ban, saying that journalists who visited the farm found "nothing inhumane" about their practices. Animal rights activist group PETA told the BBC that they will erect a billboard nearby in memory of the birds who perished, and encouraged Hudson Valley farms to donate surviving birds to local sanctuaries.
