While massive stars die in supernova explosions, the sun and stars of similar mass die quieter deaths. But that doesn't mean these stars completely go out with a whimper. New observations collected using the Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA), the world's largest radio telescope comprised of 66 antennas in northern Chile, reveal that the sun's death throes may be different from what scientists had initially believed. NASA Jet Propulsion Lab (JPL) researcher Raghvendra Sahai, who led the investigation, explained the shape the ALMA observations took: "It's essentially a physical snapshot of an event that, until now, we've mostly inferred from theoretical models. We've caught, for the first time, a star's final nuclear heartbeat, frozen in gas." Sun-like stars "die" when they run out of hydrogen in their cores. This means that the fusion of hydrogen to helium ceases in the stellar core, and because this nuclear process is a star's main energy source, the star no longer has the outward push to support itself against the inward push of its own gravity. As the star's core collapses to create a stellar remnant called a white dwarf , the outer layers where hydrogen is still fusing to form helium swell out. This expansion leads to a relatively short-lived " red giant " phase. Eventually, these outer layers will cool and disperse, leaving the central white dwarf surrounded by a so-called "planetary nebula" shell of debris.
