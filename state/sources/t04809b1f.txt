Photograph: Peter Byrne/PA Weight-loss drugs Patients may miss out on NHS weight-loss jabs in England after GP funding withdrawn Inquiry finds most integrated care boards have scrapped payments to GPs for prescribing and monitoring the drug Tobi Thomas Health and inequalities correspondent Sun 22 Nov 2026 19.01 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google Patients who are eligible for potentially life-changing weight-loss injections on the NHS risk missing out after most integrated care boards (ICBs) in England withdrew funding for GP services, an investigation has found. The inquiry, conducted by the GP magazine Pulse, found that 21 of 36 ICBs have scrapped payments to practices for prescribing and monitoring Mounjaro, also known as tirzepatide. A further nine never had a local enhanced service and only four still retain one. Although NHS England said funding was now national, with incentives for GPs to manage obesity added to the Quality and Outcomes Framework from April, the scheme is voluntary. GPs told Pulse the payments were not enough to cover the cost of providing the service. Chris McCann, the acting chief executive of Healthwatch England, said patients had reported significant differences in their ability to obtain NHS-funded weight-loss drugs. “Many people describe a postcode lottery in access to NHS-funded weight-loss drugs,” he said. “Depending on where they live, people can face very different routes to getting the medication, and in some areas access remains unclear while local commissioning arrangements are still being decided.” In some areas of England, NHS bosses have already warned patients that the prescribing of Mounjaro cannot be guaranteed. Cheshire and Merseyside ICB, for example, states that GP participation is optional and that meeting the eligibility criteria does not guarantee access. Since GPs were able to begin prescribing Mounjaro in 2025 , access to the weight-loss medication has been reported to be a postcode lottery across England. The Guardian reported last year that thousands of patients in England were unable to access weight-loss jabs through their GP, with only 18 of 42 commissioning bodies having confirmed they had begun prescribing the drug. The British Medical Association said the new payments were unlikely to cover the extra work involved in prescribing and monitoring the drug and that eligible patients were missing out as a result. It said the arrangements could become increasingly unsustainable as the NHS widens eligibility to more people over the coming years. Prof Victoria Tzortziou Brown, the president of the Royal College of GPs, also raised concerns, saying the college recognised the worry that the new arrangements “may not adequately support the clinical work involved in assessing patients, prescribing safely and providing ongoing monitoring and follow-up”. She added: “It is vital that general practice is appropriately supported to deliver safe, high-quality care as part of any additional responsibilities associated with the rollout of weight-loss drugs in the NHS.” Black Friday deals for liquid Brazilian butt lifts criticised over safety fears Read more Prof Andy Brooks, the clinical chair of the National Association of Primary Care, said people eligible for tirzepatide needed “a clear, properly resourced route to treatment and follow-up”, including continuity of care for those already taking it. He added: “This illustrates the limits of paying for individual activities for complex problems, which require a far more coordinated approach. We should start with the needs of our populations and the outcomes that matter to people, bringing together neighbourhood partners to plan and properly resource prevention, weight management support and treatment. “The test is whether people can get the support they need when they need it, whether inequities in access narrow and whether that support can be sustained.” NHS England said: “The NHS has moved funding for tirzepatide support into the GP contract, replacing varied local arrangements with a clear, nationally funded approach.
