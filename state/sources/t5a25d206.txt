Lack of protection leaves them in a hard place Shreya Dasgupta 20 Nov 2026 Global Comments Share article Share this article If you liked this story, share it with other people. Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link More than a quarter of the world’s land reptiles depend on rocky habitats, yet these areas are often overlooked in conservation efforts, according to a recent study . For cold-blooded lizards, snakes, and worm lizards (legless lizards), rocks often provide warmth for basking, shelter from predators, and places to hunt, socialize, and breed. However, the number of reptile species globally that use rocky habitats like cliffs, outcrops, or karst systems, and how threatened they are, still remains uncertain, researchers write in the study. To fill the gap, the researchers analyzed 9,729 terrestrial species belonging to the order Squamata, which includes all lizards, snakes, and worm lizards. For each species, they consulted the IUCN Red List, which documents its conservation status, habitat requirements, and threats. Their analysis found that 2,479 species, or more than 25% of those studied, use rocky habitats. Of these, 1,022 species, or 10.5%, were entirely dependent on rocks — that is, they lived almost exclusively on rocks and used rocks for breeding, shelter, and to regulate their temperature. Of the studied species that use rocks, nearly 30%, or 676 species, are classified as threatened on the IUCN Red List, the study found. Species that depend entirely on rocky habitats were 51% more likely to be critically endangered than those that use them only occasionally. Some of these rock-dependent critically endangered reptiles include the rough-bellied round-eyed gecko ( Cnemaspis tropidogaster ), native to India’s Western Ghats and Sri Lanka, Emirati leaf-toed gecko ( Asaccus caudivolvulus ), the United Arab Emirates’s only endemic vertebrate, and Homonota rupicola gecko in Paraguay. “Rocky habitats may seem relatively indestructible compared to forests or other living habitats,” the researchers wrote in an article in The Conversation . “In reality, they are easily damaged or destroyed and, once lost, may take centuries or even [millenia] to reform.” The researchers note in the paper that some rocky habitats are incidentally protected because they fall within rugged terrain covered by protected areas. However, rocky habitats in and of themselves are “rarely formally protected or managed in their own right.” Mining, quarrying, and the collection and crushing of rocks directly damage rocky areas, while indirect threats such as invasive species and inappropriate patterns of burning also affect the species that depend on them, the researchers add. For example, the Delhi rock gecko ( Cyrtopodion aravallensis ) in India, listed as vulnerable, is threatened by mining and quarrying, while rock collection for housing construction threatens the critically endangered Homonota rupicola gecko in Paraguay. In the UAE, the critically endangered Emirati leaf-toed gecko lives on a single isolated rocky outcrop threatened by development, the researchers write. “[R]ocky areas are often undervalued as habitat and are rarely protected or managed,” the researchers write in The Conversation. “As a result, scientists don’t know how quickly these habitats are disappearing. Without greater recognition and action, we risk losing both these habitats and the species that depend on them.” Banner image: The critically endangered rough-bellied round-eyed gecko (Cnemaspis tropidogaster).
