Smoke was seen rising into the sky shortly after 09:00 local time (15:00 GMT) on Wednesday following the detonation around the ammunition plant, as the Army declared the risk "as close to zero as is absolutely humanly possible". An all-clear was issued. Local soil, air and water continue to be tested. Plans to detonate the lead azide, a volatile compound reportedly at risk of exploding on its own, were paused several times before the blast following public outcry and legal challenges. In an update late on Wednesday, Environmental Protection Agency Administrator Lee Zeldin said air monitors were detecting zero airborne lead after the controlled explosion. He wrote on X that the agency would "continue to be radically transparent with the public as sample results come in from the laboratory", and that these were "projected to start arriving back over the next 24 to 48 hours". His post added: "Our goal is to complete ALL sampling by tomorrow and will remain on the ground to complete any remediation if at all necessary." More than 1,000 post-detonation samples had been taken from homes, parks, farms and drinking water taps among other locations, Zeldin said. On Thursday, sites including churches, schools and playgrounds would be sampled as well. President Donald Trump said shortly after the blast that reports showed mitigation efforts "far exceeded expectations". The explosives had been in storage for around 20 years, and degraded as part of a process known as recrystallisation. This made them more responsive to accidental detonation from friction or other movement. No level of lead exposure is considered safe, with children and pregnant women particularly vulnerable to harmful effects. Despite widespread concern from residents of Middletown, Iowa, ahead of the blast, officials have said that lead readings in the area so far have been promising. In a Wednesday evening update on X, the Army said that the Federal Emergency Management Agency (Fema) had issued the all-clear around the plant, adding: "Post-detonation surface sampling is underway, and results will be released once complete." Image source, Sandy Mabeus Middletown Mayor Rodney Johnson told the BBC he felt "relieved" after the detonation. He watched the blast from his job at a manufacturing plant eight miles (12km) away from the blast - and monitored a camera at his two-bedroom home just one mile from the detonation site. "We did not feel anything. We did not see anything," Johnson said. Sandy Mabeus was also eight miles from Middletown. She closed the windows and the garage at her home, brought their outdoor cat inside and hunkered down. The family watched three separate live streams on iPads and televisions of the explosion as it happened. "We did not feel or hear a thing here," she told the BBC, adding that she checked the videos to confirm it had actually happened. "The waiting and wondering has not been a good thing for anybody in the area," she said. "Hopefully they had a very good result and we can just get on with things." Iowa farmer David Vandenberg lives about 20 miles from the blast site and said he first learned of the detonation around two weeks ago. The Army Corps of Engineers took samples from his yard and pond on Tuesday. "I was listening this morning, and I did hear a rumble about nine o'clock," he told the BBC on Wednesday, but didn't see or smell anything. Vandenberg expects officials to return to take more samples but "was happy to see that the blast was relatively contained. "I think the effects will be minimal" Illinois Governor JB Pritzker said on X that his state was "seeing little to no impact" from the blast. In a press release, he said he was directing state agencies to remain on standby for any "unanticipated impact". The Iowa site is only 10 miles from the Illinois border. But there is also caution from some. Iowa's Governor-elect Rob Sand said there was "more testing to do" and called for "immediate transparency" to explain why the lead-up was "so chaotic and unclear". Sand said that any Iowans who have been impacted in any way "must be taken care of fully, right away". Cameron Hamilton, the Fema administrator, said there would be additional resources and testing in the coming days and weeks. In recent weeks, the US Army scheduled and rescheduled the detonation - all the while hoping to carry out the task before the onset of colder winter temperatures, which were deemed to raise the risk of an uncontrolled explosion. Ahead of the blast, it warned locals of "potential exposure to toxic lead dust", which led to some panic. It instituted measures including an optional evacuation zone and a shelter-in-place advisory ahead of the detonation. The Army faced pushback from residents and politicians alike, and there were legal challenges on environmental and health grounds that were aimed at preventing the detonation from going ahead. But on Monday, the appeals court overturned a decision to halt the blast. It said that - while the concerns were valid - the risk posed by a potential uncontrolled explosion was greater.
