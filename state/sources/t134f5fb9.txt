[ CLIP: “I Like to Move It,” in Madagascar ] Feltman: In fairness, of the more than 100 estimated species of lemurs found on the aforementioned island, the Indri indri is the only true singer. That’s not to say that other lemurs can’t make lovely sounds, and in fact, I’m not sure I would die on the hill of saying that the indris are making particularly lovely sounds. But indris are the only lemurs that turn their vocalizations into what researchers feel comfortable calling songs . A song, in this case, is a long vocalization built from notes that are organized into phrases and follow a rhythm, with changes in pitch along the way. The question is why indris evolved to do this—and why other lemurs didn’t. Lemurs, by the way, have a fascinating evolutionary history. Like 90 percent of the living things on Madagascar, they aren’t found anywhere else. But based on the fossil record, it does not seem like lemurs evolved from a primate that was on Madagascar when it separated from Africa some 165 million years ago or when it broke away from India 80 million years ago and became an island. Instead, as wild as it might sound, lemur ancestors from Africa made their way to Madagascar by sea. According to DNA analysis published last year in Nature Communications , that happened about 53 million years ago. However it was that those little guys got swept onto shore, they found themselves without many natural predators or even any other primates to compete with. That’s probably why there are so many different species of lemur: There were lots of different niches for them to evolve into . And for the indri, one of those niches could be musicality. Besides humans and indris, the primates known to create songs are gibbons, tarsiers and titi monkeys—none of which are closely related to lemurs or to each other. That means this skill evolved more than once.
