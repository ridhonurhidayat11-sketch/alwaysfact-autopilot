Its discovery suggests orcas evolved a diet of marine mammals earlier than scientists thought By Jake Buehler 19 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print A reconstruction of the North Sea orca (with white heads) hunting dolphins (with bulbous heads) Remie Bakker Orcas may have been top predators in the ocean for longer than we had thought . Long ago in the North Sea, massive ancestors of modern orcas may have terrorised other whales. Though the fossil remains are too fragmented to conclusively categorise the orca as a new species, the findings are transforming researchers’ understanding of the animals’ evolution. “At least 2.5 million years ago, orcas were already very large and capable of hunting large game,” says Hidde Bakker at the Natural History Museum Rotterdam in the Netherlands. Advertisement Read more Orcas filmed making sunfish explode by ramming them at high speed In 1994, fishing vessels trawling the bottom of the southern North Sea between the UK and the coast of the Netherlands pulled up truckloads of fossils and mammoth tusks. Dozens of these fossils, now housed at the museum in Rotterdam, appeared to belong to some large but unknown species of orca ( Orcinus ) that lived 2.1 million to 2.6 million years ago. But no one had systematically studied them. Bakker and his colleagues took the five most information-rich specimens – two skull chunks, a jawbone and some vertebrae – and compared them with living and extinct members of the dolphin family, of which orcas are the largest living members. The only other fossil orca species known, says Bakker, is a 3.5-metre-long species that swam in the Mediterranean Sea around Italy at around the same time as the North Sea orcas were around. Analysis of its fossils suggests that the Mediterranean orca probably predominantly ate fish. But based on certain skeletal and dental clues, the North Sea orca seemed equipped to hunt larger fare. Bakker and his colleagues think it could have been 7.7 metres long, which puts it on par with some of the largest male orcas today. It also had features commonly seen in the modern orca populations – called “ecotypes” – that specialise in hunting marine mammals. In particular, its teeth were set in very deep sockets that were reinforced with bone. “In most dolphins, that’s not the case,” Bakker says. “It’s just more like a groove with teeth placed in it.” The orca – which still hasn’t been given a formal new species name – also had a relatively mobile head, which would have allowed it to grip and tear large prey. These traits all hint that this predator could attack other big, powerful animals such as other dolphins or baleen whales. Californian orcas used specialised tactics for hunting whale calves A group of orcas in Monterey Bay, California, use a submarine canyon to isolate whale calves, and launch coordinated attacks on prey in the open ocean It is possible that these orcas evolved in the ecological vacuum left behind by the extinction of rapacious, whale-eating sperm whales . “We have some evidence now that [dolphins] took over their top predator niche,” Bakker says. Figuring out more precisely when those sperm whales went extinct could help researchers better test that hypothesis, says Olivier Lambert at the Royal Belgian Institute of Natural Sciences in Brussels, who wasn’t involved in the analysis. He’s also curious to know whether chemical signatures in the North Sea orcas’ teeth and bones could further confirm their diet and their place in the marine food web. “Did they already feed on large marine mammals, fish, or a combination?” Lambert says. “This [research] may provide some insights about the emergence of some social skills [in orcas], like the highly coordinated hunting of giant whales.” Journal Reference: PeerJ DOI: 10.7717/peerj.21890 Topics: Palaeontology / whales and dolphins Advertisement Sign up to our weekly newsletter Receive a weekly dose of discovery in your inbox.
