Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link In 1974, the Mauritius kestrel was down to four birds in the wild. In 1979, Norman Myers, a prominent environmentalist, argued that the money might be better spent protecting birds with better prospects. Nevertheless, conservationists pursued captive breeding, predator control, and releases of the Mauritius kestrel ( Falco punctatus ). Its numbers eventually recovered into the hundreds, though the population has since declined again. The Mauritius kestrel is one of 141 species examined in a study published in the Proceedings of the Royal Society B . The researchers, led by Donal Smith of Monash University in Australia, looked at birds and mammals whose wild populations had fallen below 50 individuals at some point since 1950, and for which enough time had passed to assess the outcome. Of the 51 species that received direct conservation help, 31 (61%) recovered to at least 100 individuals. Six went extinct. Among the 90 species without direct intervention, just two (2%) reached a population of 100, while 33 (37%) went extinct. Recoveries were most common when conservationists addressed the causes of decline, such as introduced predators or habitat loss, while also increasing animal numbers through captive breeding or translocation. Legal protection and other indirect measures, on their own, showed no clear association with recovery. The researchers say they can’t establish how much of the recovery was caused by conservation intervention. Some species may have received help because their problems were easier to solve. Reaching 100 animals is hardly a guarantee of survival, since many of the recovered species still require intensive management. Among species that recovered after direct intervention, the median time to reach 100 individuals was 18 years. Australia’s Lord Howe woodhen ( Hypotaenidia sylvestris ) had dwindled to just 23 birds by the late 1970s. Conservationists removed feral pigs and cats, and bred the birds in captivity and released them. The population hovered around 200 for decades. After rats were eradicated in 2019, it climbed to 1,638 by 2024. Banner image : A Mauritius kestrel. Image by MauriceBer via Wikimedia Commons ( CC BY-SA 4.0 ).
