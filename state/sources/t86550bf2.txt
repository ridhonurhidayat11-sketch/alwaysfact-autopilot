See All Key Ideas Nearly 120 residents of Vindoulou, a neighborhood in Pointe-Noire, Republic of Congo, have filed a civil claim before the city’s High Court in relation to pollution allegedly caused by a foundry. “We want tests, but also compensation for damages and losses,” Cyrille Traoré Ndembi, a plaintiff and president of the Vindoulou Residents’ Collective, which represents people living in the working-class neighborhood, told Mongabay on the phone. The civil case, filed in October, is the latest in a series of actions taken against Metssa Congo, a foundry that opened in the neighborhood in 2013 .The facility recycled, among other things, car batteries, extracting lead and turning it into lead bars for export. The plant is part of the Metssa Group, an industrial metals trading company based in the United Arab Emirates. The battery recycling process can pose serious health risks. According to the WHO, lead can be released in the form of fumes, particles and dust at every stage of the process, from draining and dismantling to smelting and refining. Environmental contamination from recycling can be substantial, exposing workers and nearby communities to significant levels of lead, the health agency says on its website. According to Traore Ndembi, residents of Vindoulou soon experienced this reality first-hand, complaining about toxic fumes coming from the plant. In 2019, they decided to register their discontent over pollution. “We wrote to the public authorities, to the departmental director of health,” Traoré Ndembi said. “We also wrote to the local environmental authorities but they said there was no pollution. The health authorities in the Kouilou department then carried out medical tests like chest X-rays to prove that the area was not polluted. Their findings said we were not sick, that there was nothing wrong. So we decided to take matters into our own hands.” To provide scientific evidence to support their complaint, in 2023 the communities, with the help of NGO Amnesty International, conducted blood tests on a small group of residents, including both adults and children. The results were unequivocal: The people tested had blood lead levels far above the threshold recommended by the WHO, namely 5 micrograms per deciliter (µg/dL) or one-tenth of a liter. While there is no known level of safe exposure to lead, the health agency recommends taking action when levels cross this threshold. In some cases, the levels detected were 10 times higher. The situation was alarming, according to the human rights organization, and the findings proved decisive for the communities. The residents’ collective won its first legal battle: The administrative court of Pointe-Noire ordered the company to suspend its operations and the government instructed the operator to fund site decontamination and medical care. Image by Cyrille Traoré Ndembi. ‘I can’t keep [the children] locked indoors’ Yet, two years later, Traoré Ndembi, who still lives in Vindoulou with his wife and children, remains worried. “The authorities are not telling us anything,” he said. “It is true that the plant has closed, but nothing has changed on the ground since then. Nothing has been cleaned up. We are still living in an area that has been polluted for years.” “Closing the plant is not enough. The medical test results confirmed that we had been poisoned by lead, so we need to receive medical care and treatment! And the plant must clean up and decontaminate the site,” he said. “Because what guarantees that, even though it has stopped operating, it is no longer polluting? I don’t feel at ease when my children play outside, but I can’t keep them locked indoors either.” Alexander Van Geen, a research professor specialized in environmental contaminants and member of the Earth Institute faculty at Columbia University, told Mongabay in a phone interview for a previous article about possible contamination from the Metssa plant that he had been shocked by the blood test results obtained by Amnesty International. “I have to admit that when I first saw the data, I thought, ‘This can’t be possible.’,” he said. “I thought that at levels this high, children would be dying or suffering from terrible tremors. I was horrified.” According to the WHO, exposure to lead is highly toxic and can cause lead poisoning, a serious condition that affects the brain, liver, kidneys and bones. The U.N. agency estimates that lead exposure caused more than 1.5 million deaths worldwide in 2021, mainly through cardiovascular effects. Aware of the scale of the problem, Van Geen told Mongabay in early 2026 that “something needs to be done.” A new round of testing His words were followed by action. After Mongabay published its article, he contacted Cyrille Traoré Ndembi and the Vindoulou Residents’ Collective to carry out another round of tests, this time on the soil. “It was important to me that the affected communities carry out their own measurements,” he said after the new round of tests. “I thought that if blood lead levels were high, the lead had to be coming from somewhere. So, we began looking at the soil.” Using his own money, Van Geen sent soil sampling and analysis kits to the community members by post. The colorimetric kits were based on the method described by Samantha S. Moura and Daphne van der Molen in a study in 2025 and are designed to measure the presence of lead in soil. There is no need to dig: The tests focus on the upper layers of soil, the surfaces people walk on and where children play, which are therefore particularly important for human health. Once the kits had been sent, Van Geen conducted remote training sessions to teach residents how to use them. “We were in regular contact,” Traoré Ndembi said. “He gave me precise instructions, and I followed them to the letter.” Van Geen asked him to download an app, SurveyCTO, onto his smartphone to record the geographical coordinates of each sampling location and take photographs of every sample and of the on-site test results, depending on the equipment we had. In May 2026, Traoré Ndembi installed the data collection app and collected 49 soil samples considered valid by Van Geen, starting immediately around the former plant and extending to a distance of approximately 300 meters (984 feet). “I then sent the samples to the professor in the United States so that he could analyze them as well,” he said. At Columbia University in New York, the samples were re-analyzed using X-ray fluorescence, a more precise analytical method. Using the samples and the geographical data collected, the Van Geen produced a map showing lead contamination around the plant. “The soil immediately surrounding the former METSSA Congo SARLU plant is heavily contaminated with lead,” the analysis noted. Of the 49 samples almost half exceed 200 milligrams per kilogram (mg/kg), the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) threshold for safety for residential soils where children play. “Seven samples (14%) exceed that threshold by more than tenfold.
