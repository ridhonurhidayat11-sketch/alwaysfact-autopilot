Manage your subscription Your newsletters Subscriber benefits Sign out Subscribe now Health The overlooked longevity nutrients that may add years to your life An intriguing hypothesis proposes that certain nutrients in our food don’t just enable our survival, but have age-delaying functions, too – and efforts are under way to pin them down By David Cox 23 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Jonathan Utracik Bruce Ames was remarkable in many ways. A renowned biochemist who continued working into his nineties, he is best known for developing a low-cost screening test for cancer-causing substances. But he devoted the latter stages of his long career to an intriguing idea: that a hitherto-unrecognised class of nutrients plays a key role in promoting healthy ageing. “Many as-yet-unknown longevity vitamins and proteins likely remain to be discovered,” he wrote in one of his last papers, in 2022, just a couple of years before he passed away, aged 95. But could such nutrients have been hiding in plain sight all along? A number of promising candidates are under investigation, including some found in mushrooms and seaweed. Proving the long-term health impacts of dietary factors in rigorous trials is a huge challenge, and the field of anti-ageing supplements has been plagued by ideas that failed to live up to their promise, but we could still be on the cusp of finding out if Ames was right. If so, what might we one day be dosing up with for the benefit of our future selves? Read more Rapid bursts of ageing are causing a total rethink of how we grow old A healthy diet requires various vitamins and minerals for basic metabolic reactions in the human body – that is, to keep us alive in the immediate future. There are around 30 of these essential micronutrients, including the vitamins from A to K, and minerals such as calcium and iron. A deficiency in these can cause conditions such as scurvy, rickets and beriberi. Advertisement But Ames had a compelling idea that inadequate intake of micronutrients also causes insidious, long-term damage. Building on his many years of research investigating metabolism and ageing, in 2006 he proposed that when vitamins and minerals are in short supply, our bodies operate a triage system , allocating them to systems involved in immediate survival and sacrificing other functions that are more important for long-term health, such as DNA repair and maintenance of mitochondria. This could explain why moderate deficiencies in vitamin D, for instance, are associated with certain cancers and cardiovascular disease. His subsequent studies, working with Joyce McCann at the Children’s Hospital Oakland Research Institute in California, demonstrated that when selenium and vitamin K are scarce, they are indeed preferentially allocated to the biological systems that are most important for short-term survival. Renowned biochemist Bruce Ames continued working into his 90s, investigating dietary substances that might promote healthy ageing mct/ZUMA Press Wire/Shutterstock This led Ames to the idea that some dietary compounds are particularly adept at supporting long-term health , and that there may be an entire other class of unrecognised nutrients that might be capable of activating various protective pathways in our bodies. If we consume enough of them, he suggested, they can fine-tune our metabolism and help shield us from insidious, long-term damage. In that 2022 paper, Ames produced a list of around 40 nutrients – key ingredients for thousands of chemical reactions throughout the body – which he believed to be critical for preventing chronic conditions and promoting longevity. “I propose that many of the known vitamins and minerals have, besides a ‘survival’ function, aging-delaying (‘longevity’) functions as well. I also propose that other dietary biochemicals, not officially recognized as vitamins or as having age-delaying functions, have a positive age-delaying effect,” he wrote. Perhaps it shouldn’t be a surprise that some lesser-known substances in our diet may have this impact. There are myriad biochemicals in our food, and we know surprising little about this “nutritional dark matter” or its impacts on the body. But proving the efficacy of any purported longevity nutrients – substances whose effects gradually accumulate over the long term – was always going to be difficult, not least because they would sail under the radar of standard tests for deficiencies. Free newsletter Sign up to Health Check Sign up to newsletter A recent academic book, Supplements and Drugs for Healthy Longevity , describes more than 250 chemicals that are listed in various databases as potentially being able to alter the ageing process. However, it notes that the evidence mainly comes from experiments on organisms such as yeasts, worms or other animals. “[Strong evidence] on the human species is absolutely missing,” says Suresh Rattan , a biogerontologist at Aarhus University in Denmark, who co-edited the book. This could change, however, as the results of robust clinical trials begin to roll in, and with new research efforts under way to identify nutrients that may slow the clock. One of these is a 2025 analysis of 777 generally healthy and active people aged over 70 who took part in the DO-HEALTH randomised controlled trial to investigate whether vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids and a simple home exercise programme could prevent disease in older age and thereby prolong healthy life expectancy. This measured the biological age of the participants after three years of the trial and found that omega-3 alone moderately slowed the rate of biological ageing , with extra benefits from the addition of vitamin D or exercise. Although we don’t yet have data about the ultimate impact this has on healthspan – the number of years that we live in good health – the study’s authors noted that “even small changes in biological aging, if sustained, may have relevant effects on population health”. Could longevity nutrients keep older people active and healthy for longer? REUTERS/Toru Hanai Another large-scale clinical trial that reported results earlier this year analysed the anti-ageing effect of a daily supplement of cocoa extract (containing high concentrations of substances called flavanols, thought to assist healthy ageing) and a daily multivitamin in 958 people in the US aged over 60. This found that the cocoa extract had no impact, but the multivitamin resulted in a small but significant reduction in the rate of biological ageing , which might be linked to our micronutrient requirements increasing with age . The negative flavanol finding is interesting, because Ames, too, thought that certain substances in food that aren’t classed as vitamins could have a positive long-term impact on our health. But rather than flavanols, the candidates he proposed were pigments called carotenoids, as well as certain antioxidants produced by bacteria and fungi. Now, initiatives to investigate some of these are under way, with researchers in Singapore at the forefront of these efforts. It is projected that, by 2050, a third of Singapore’s population will be over 65 , with spiralling rates of conditions such as cancer and dementia too. The urgency of the situation has led to large-scale investment in clinical trials exploring new ways to elongate healthspan – and among the nutrients in the crosshairs of researchers there is one highlighted by Ames called ergothioneine. Produced mainly by fungi and bacteria , the best dietary source of it is mushrooms, particularly oyster and shiitake varieties. A number of studies from the past few years have already shown that eating mushrooms is associated with a lower risk of various chronic conditions and longer life expectancy . Humans have also evolved adaptations that allow our bodies to absorb and retain ergothioneine. A specific transporter molecule for this nutrient in our intestines and other tissues means that when we eat foods such as mushrooms, it is distributed around the body – suggesting that it is important for us.
