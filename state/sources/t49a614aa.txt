Sign Up The holiday season is here, and many of us are planning elaborate feasts to celebrate. Whether they’re Thanksgiving turkey dinners or Hanukkah latkes or Christmas roasts, these meals often stretch for hours at time. As you plan your menu, the timing of it all becomes paramount—including when you will sit down to eat. But does eating at, say, 3 P.M. rather than 7 P.M. make a real difference? The answer is yes—and it influences your health more than you think. An entire area of science called chrononutrition is dedicated to how when we eat affects our bodies. Essentially, it is the study of how the timing, frequency and regularity of meals interact with the body’s internal biological clock to influence health. Dietary advice traditionally focuses on food quality, calories, macronutrients and portion sizes. But when we eat matters perhaps just as much as these other factors, Pereira says. Our bodies do not process food in the same way throughout the day. Metabolism, insulin sensitivity and hormonal activity fluctuate according to our internal circadian clock. And these rhythms are themselves individual and described by a characteristic known as chronotype . Most people generally fall into two broad categories: “larks,” or morning types, who naturally wake up early, have more energy in the morning and tend to eat earlier in the day, and “night owls,” or evening types, who prefer staying up late and waking up later and tend to eat most in the afternoon and evening. Which bucket you fall into may be related to levels of hormones that help regulate activity and rest, such as cortisol and melatonin . In night owls, for example, hormone release may be more “delayed” than in larks, Pereira explains. As a result, hunger signals may also ping later, meaning that these individuals may not feel the need to eat immediately after waking up and may skip breakfast. But regardless of your chronotype, there is evidence that eating large meals earlier in the day may be more beneficial for your health. “Having a festive meal in the early afternoon is the ideal choice from a biological standpoint because it is a time when the body is better able to handle carbohydrates, fats and large portions,” explains Giovanna Muscogiuri , a professor of medicine at the University of Arizona. Insulin sensitivity and glucose tolerance are typically high in the morning and decline as the day progresses. This means that consuming a lot of carbohydrates and sugars late at night could spike blood glucose more than eating the same meal at lunchtime. Even on holidays , Muscogiuri says, try not to skip breakfast—instead have a small, protein-rich meal. This helps the body take advantage of the morning cortisol peak and may prevent energy dips that could lead to overeating later in the day. If you do choose to hold an evening celebration, try to finish eating at least three hours before going to bed. What you eat when during the meal also matters, Muscogiuri says. Start with fiber-rich vegetables, followed by protein and fats. Complex carbohydrates and desserts can be saved for the end. “By eating them last, carbohydrates enter an intestinal environment where absorption has already been slowed by fiber and fats, thereby helping to prevent a so-called blood sugar spike,” Muscogiuri says. Both Pereira and Muscogiuri emphasize, however, that we do not need to worry too much about the metabolic effects of a single meal. Celebrations such as Thanksgiving are special occasions, after all. “It is habitual behavior, rather than an occasional exception, that can have harmful effects,” Muscogiuri says.
