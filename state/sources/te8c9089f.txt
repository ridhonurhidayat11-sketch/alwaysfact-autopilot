Photograph: Reuters China China sends first foreign astronaut to ISS rival as it globalises space operations Muhammad Zeeshan Ali – the first Pakistani to reach orbit – greeted with Chinese ‘space handshake’ at Tiangong station Rebecca Bailey and agencies Wed 25 Nov 2026 14.18 GMT Last modified on Wed 25 Nov 2026 22.04 GMT Share Prefer the Guardian on Google China has flown the first foreign astronaut to its Tiangong space station, starting what it says will be the globalisation of its space operations as the US-led International Space Station (ISS) enters its final years of operation. Floating through a hatch to enter Tiangong, Pakistan’s Muhammad Zeeshan Ali grinned broadly as he was embraced by the onboard crew in what China’s space agency called the country’s first foreign “space handshake”. Tiangong, completed in 2022, is the jewel in the crown of China’s space programme. The government has invested billions into its celestial ambitions in recent years to catch up with the US and Russia, two of the five partners involved in the ISS along with Europe, Japan and Canada. After 25 years of continuous occupation, the ISS is set to cease operations from 2030, according to Nasa. The tortoise and the hare: will China beat the US in the race back to the moon? Read more On Wednesday China’s president, Xi Jinping, told his Pakistani counterpart that Beijing was “willing to work with all countries … to uphold the banner of the peaceful use of outer space”. Ali was part of the crew of Shenzhou 24, the most recent iteration of the series of missions that rotate astronauts in the space station every six months. He is the first Pakistani to reach orbit. Blaine Curcio, the managing director at Orbital Gateway Consulting, told the Guardian: “Sending another country’s astronaut to space is a major undertaking. Pakistan is putting a lot of trust into Beijing by having him fly into space on a Chinese-built rocket. “There are also considerations related to security: Ali is getting detailed insights into China’s crewed space programme … It makes China better prepared to do it again, and also makes it clear to other countries that China is open to this type of collaboration.” Space cooperation between China and Pakistan dates back to the early 1990s. China has launched Pakistani satellites, and a Pakistani-built rover ‌is due to fly on a Chinese lunar mission around 2028. Shenzhou 24 blasted off from the Jiuquan launch centre in north-west China against a pink-tinged dawn sky on Wednesday and first docked with the space station three and a half hours later. Ali, a 40-year-old Pakistan air force pilot and payload specialist, will conduct experiments including one cultivating microalgae as a potential food and another testing the effects of spaceflight on quinoa seeds. To prepare for the mission, he ​trained for seven months in Beijing, including taking intensive Chinese-language classes. Photograph: Sohail Shahzad/EPA At a press conference the day before the launch, Ali recited a line from a classical Chinese poem. Commander Tang Hongbo told reporters that differences in language and culture had made learning to work together more challenging but that the effort had paid off. Ali will not stay for the six-month term usually demanded of Tiangong astronauts. Instead, he will return to Earth with two of the Shenzhou 23 crew while its commander, Zhu Yangzhu, will remain to reach another milestone – the first full-year stay in space by a Chinese astronaut. Beijing’s accelerated achievements in the skies have spooked its rivals. China is the third country to put humans in orbit and it has landed robotic rovers on Mars and the moon, where it hopes to send crewed missions within the next decade. In racing to the moon before 2030, Beijing is competing with the US’s Artemis programme . The US has excluded China from the ISS since 2011, banning Nasa from collaborating with Beijing. In 2025, Nasa went further by blocking Chinese citizens from its facilities altogether. US lawmakers have moved to try to extend the life of the ISS, citing China’s “permanent human presence in low Earth orbit” through Tiangong. Progress on turning that proposal into law has been slow. Nasa has said that after the ISS it will be “one of many customers in a robust commercial marketplace in low Earth orbit”. The ISS was heralded as a symbol of post-cold war cooperation, but renewed cooperation with Russia seems “unlikely”, said Andrew Jones, a journalist specialising in China’s space programme. While Tiangong is smaller than the ISS, China is understood to be preparing to double its size, “likely with the end of the ISS in mind”, Jones added. “These developments could allow China to expand its international engagement, and potentially space tourism. With the ISS nearing the end of its life and the future of western commercial space stations uncertain, China could for a time have the only permanently crewed outpost in low Earth orbit.” China has long signalled its interest in developing Tiangong as an international hub. China Manned Space Agency documents published in coordination with the UN outline “resources for international cooperation” onboard the station. European and Chinese astronauts previously trained together with a view to a visit to Tiangong, but that cooperation ended for political and budgetary reasons, Jones said. Curcio said he would expect countries such as Brazil and Egypt to seek out opportunities to send astronauts on Chinese missions.
