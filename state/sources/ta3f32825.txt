A small herd of rare Bagot goats has been brought in to clear a steep, overgrown slope below the Royal Observatory in Greenwich Park, in a trial run by The Royal Parks charity. Over five days the animals will chomp through tough, woody plants and thorny stems on Castle Hill Bank, a patch that is hard to reach with machinery or by hand. Visitors can watch them at work until Friday, between 11:00 and 15:30 GMT. The trial uses conservation grazing, a traditional method in which animals keep dominant vegetation in check, giving other plants room to grow and supporting wildlife. The Royal Parks is working with Mudchute Park and Farm, on the Isle of Dogs, and the Rare Breeds Survival Trust, with funding from players of People's Postcode Lottery. Bagots are Britain's oldest native breed of goat, according to the charity. They are small and light-footed, which makes them well suited to steep and uneven ground. Clare Lanes, Greenwich Park manager, said the view of Flamsteed House above the bank was part of what made the park so special as a Unesco World Heritage Site. "But it's a very steep bank, and managing vegetation to protect that view is a real challenge," she said. "We're trialling a traditional approach by bringing in goats to graze, which we hope will help us protect the landscape more sustainably." Image source, Royal Parks Image caption, Holly, brambles and thorny stems are all on the menu at Castle Hill Bank She said the approach could also help the charity in its ambition to create climate-resilient parks for future generations as well as making a few visitors smile. The herd is kept in a paddock with a lean-to shelter and fresh water, and is shepherded on and off site each day. The goats are being watched over by Tom Davis, farm manager at Mudchute, which has previously worked with The Royal Parks on similar projects in other London parks. He said the scheme was giving an "incredibly rare breed" an important purpose. Charlotte Cass, who manages The Royal Parks' Help Nature Thrive project, said the charity was "always looking for exciting ways to support nature" and new ways to manage its eight London parks. "This project helps create a better, more viable habitat for invertebrates, birds and small mammals in Greenwich Park in the longer term," she said.
