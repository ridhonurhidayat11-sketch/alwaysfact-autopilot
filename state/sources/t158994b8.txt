Photograph: Fabrice Coffrini/AFP/Getty Images AI (artificial intelligence) International talks on controlling use of autonomous weapons collapse Week-long efforts fail to establish negotiating mandate on AI warfare after Russian ‘filibustering’ Frances Mao and Patrick Wintour Sat 21 Nov 2026 08.15 EST Last modified on Sat 21 Nov 2026 08.17 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google Week-long efforts to agree a negotiating mandate for a legally binding international treaty to control on the use of autonomous weapons collapsed just before 5am on Saturday morning, after all night talks failed to overcome Russian objections. Despite an overwhelming majority of states at the Review Conference on Conventional Weapons at the UN in Geneva wanting to agree a negotiating mandate leading to a legally binding treaty, the need for consensus forced the conference to adopt a much weaker option. The mandate simply asks countries “to elaborate by consensus”, an instrument that may not carry any legal obligations. Although Russia led the objections using uniquely obstructive stalling tactics from the opening day, other military powers who have all invested heavily in AI warfare, such as the US, were reluctant to commit to a legally binding protocol protecting civilians from robot drones and other autonomous weapons. Some argued that international humanitarian law is sufficient. More than 100 states had been pushing for a new law, saying the weapons raised entirely new challenges. European and Nato states, including Ukraine, as well as China and Iran, expressed support or willingness for a new protocol. The US said the need for human control over autonomous weapons was discussed in the meetings between president Xi Jinping and Donald Trump this week , but the US was reluctant to agree a binding treaty. South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany were among the defence tech powers exploring AI-enabled weapons who also expressed resistance to being bound by a new law, instead advocating for a nonbinding agreement. The CCW is a particular UN forum that operates via consensus, meaning all 128 parties to the convention have to agree unanimously to pass a statement. The high threshold has resulted in the CCW failing to pass any new laws regulating weapons in the past 30 years and states seeking pathways elsewhere to pass treaties, such as the 2008 ban on cluster munitions. Future of Life Institute’s chair, Max Tegmark, said the world leaders had failed to meet the moment this week. “Slaughterbots are already here,” he said. “Yet states have agreed it is sufficient to just talk about this for the 14th year.” View image in fullscreen Campaign billboard driven past the UK’s Houses of Parliament calls for control of AI weapons. Photograph: Andy Rain/EPA Diplomats inside the negotiations said that Russia had been deliberately filibustering and not working in good faith, as opposed to the US delegates, who had been seeking a path to reach a consensus statement. The exclusion of several nonmember countries from negotiations also prompted a rare protest in the chamber on the final day of the conference, with the Egyptian delegation shouting at the conference chair. Speaking at a different conference, organised by Atlantic magazine, the former US national security adviser John Bolton said: “We are moving closer and closer to almost totally unmanned warfare in the battle field. I am not sure we need manned aircraft anymore if we have the right drone technology. “The boundaries western civilisation tried to draw between war and civilian life are disintegrating.” Israel and the US have also used AI-enabled targeting systems to conduct tens of thousands of strikes in Gaza and Iran, which have resulted in mass casualties. The Pentagon struck more than 1,000 targets in Iran on the first day of the war, and according to investigative media reports, the US military is aiming to carry out 1,000 AI-assisted targeting decisions per hour. Critics said this outcome was a missed opportunity, noting that the states had been discussing the issue of autonomous weapons, their dangers, and the need for international rules on how to restrict and regulate them for 13 years already, and large substantive parts of a treaty had already been settled upon. Human Rights Watch’s senior arms adviser, Bonnie Docherty, who had been directly involved in previously successful treaties banning cluster munitions and nuclear weapons, told the Guardian that autonomous weapons needed binding law. She noted that treaties could be negotiated within 15 months, and dismissed the US’s proposal for five years to sort out a mandate. A bloc of seven countries – Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama – had led the push for a binding treaty to be negotiated within one year. “These are not weapons of the future that are being discussed here, these are already in use and being further developed. We are late in developing these norms and rules to regulate these lethal autonomous weapons systems,” Brazil’s delegate told the floor on Wednesday.
