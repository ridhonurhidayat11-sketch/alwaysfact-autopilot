Photograph: Fabrice Coffrini/AFP/Getty Weapons technology International UN talks on controlling use of autonomous weapons collapse Week-long efforts fail to establish negotiating mandate on AI warfare after Russian ‘filibustering’ Frances Mao in Geneva and Patrick Wintour Sat 21 Nov 2026 11.36 EST First published on Sat 21 Nov 2026 08.15 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google Week-long efforts to agree a negotiating mandate for a legally binding international treaty to control the use of autonomous weapons collapsed just before 5am after all-night talks failed to overcome Russian objections. Despite an overwhelming majority of states at the UN’s Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons (CCW) in Geneva wanting to agree a negotiating mandate leading to a legally binding treaty, the need for consensus forced the conference to adopt a much weaker option. The mandate asked countries “to elaborate by consensus”, an instrument that may not carry any legal obligations. Although Russia led the objections using obstructive stalling tactics, other military powers invested in AI warfare, such as the US, were reluctant to commit to a legally binding protocol protecting civilians from robot drones and other autonomous weapons. View image in fullscreen Delegates attend the UN Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons in Geneva. Photograph: Fabrice Coffrini/AFP/Getty Some argued that international humanitarian law was sufficient. More than 100 states had been pushing for a new law, saying the weapons raised entirely new challenges. European and Nato states, including Ukraine, China and Iran, expressed support or willingness for a new protocol. The US said the need for human control over autonomous weapons had been discussed at the Apec summit in meetings between president Xi Jinping and Donald Trump this week, but the US was reluctant to agree a binding treaty. South Korea, Japan, Turkey, the UK, France and Germany were among the defence tech powers exploring AI-enabled weapons who also expressed resistance to being bound by a new law, instead advocating for a nonbinding agreement. The CCW is a UN forum that operates via consensus, meaning all 128 parties to the convention have to agree unanimously to pass a statement. The high threshold has resulted in the CCW failing to pass any new laws regulating weapons in the past 30 years and states seeking pathways elsewhere to pass treaties, such as the 2008 ban on cluster munitions. The Future of Life Institute’s chair, Max Tegmark, said world leaders had failed to meet the moment this week. “Slaughterbots are already here,” he said. “Yet states have agreed it is sufficient to just talk about this for the 14th year.” View image in fullscreen A campaign billboard is driven past the Houses of Parliament in London calling for control of AI weapons. Photograph: Andy Rain/EPA Diplomats inside the negotiations said Russia had been deliberately filibustering and not working in good faith, as opposed to the US delegates, who had been seeking a path to reach a consensus statement. The Pentagon struck more than 1,000 targets in Iran on the first day of the war, and according to investigative media reports, the US military is aiming to carry out 1,000 AI-assisted targeting decisions per hour. Critics said this outcome was a missed opportunity, noting that states had been discussing the issue of autonomous weapons, their dangers, and the need for international rules on how to restrict and regulate them for 13 years and large substantive parts of a treaty had already been settled upon. One European diplomat said: “There was no political will to really regulate these weapons. No will commensurate to the urgency and need.” A Human Rights Watch’s senior arms adviser, Bonnie Docherty, who had been directly involved in previously successful treaties banning cluster munitions and nuclear weapons, said autonomous weapons needed binding law. She noted that treaties could be negotiated within 15 months and dismissed the US’s proposal of five years to sort out a mandate. A bloc of seven countries – Austria, Belgium, Brazil, Ireland, Mexico, New Zealand and Panama – had led the push for a binding treaty to be negotiated within one year. On Wednesday Brazil’s delegate told the floor: “These are not weapons of the future being discussed here, these are already in use and being further developed.
