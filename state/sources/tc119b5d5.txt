Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Cite article In Uttarakhand, India, high-altitude farmers are cultivating medicinal herbs that are in decline in the wild, helping these species revive. Women perform most of the labor in this enterprise, earning money to support their families and children’s education. Obstacles remain, however, as farmers often must obtain official certification requiring proof of land ownership — and only a small percentage of women own land in the region. “Because of this, we insist on issuing the certification in the female farmer’s name,” says Shivam Pandey of Udyogini, an NGO working toward women’s empowerment. See All Key Ideas RAMNI, Uttarakhand, India — At state fairs across Uttarakhand, while most farmers set up stalls featuring local millet varieties, Guddi Devi and her fellow farmers showcase saplings of high-altitude medicinal plants. “In the past, people used to bring kutki [ Picrorhiza kurroa ] and other herbs like sugandha bala [ Valeriana jatamansi ] from the bugyals [alpine meadows]. Since everyone was going there, digging and harvesting, everything was becoming extinct in the alpine meadows . It is a cumbersome task to even reach there, so you won’t easily find [these herbs] there anymore,” Guddi said. Situated in the Indian Himalayan region, Uttarakhand’s medicinal plants, growing at 2,000 meters (6,500 feet) or more, are highly sought after. Traditionally, locals traveled up to 100 kilometers (62 miles) on foot to collect herbs like kutki — widely used to bring down fevers — when access to medical care was scarce. Globally, market demand for medicinal and aromatic plants (MAPs) has grown substantially, with $4.2 billion in exports in 2023 , and due to unsustainable harvesting practices and overexploitation, many species are at risk. With growing concern, scientists say controlled agricultural cultivation can be a sustainable method to meet the pharmaceutical demands for these plants. “Uttarakhand has diverse agro-climatic conditions, therefore, mountain villages located at about 2500 to 3000 m altitude are suitable locations for domestication or for cultivation of high altitude medicinal and aromatic plants,” known as MAPs, Chandra P. Kuniyal, a scientist at the Herbal Research and Development Institute (HRDI), said in an email. Since men of the hills often leave their homes for better financial opportunities, it is the women living in villages at high altitudes — where these MAPs thrive in the cold — who have become the cornerstone of medicinal herb conservation efforts. “The feminization of hill agriculture is there because mostly the men migrate outside for their job,” said K.S. Kanwal, scientist at G.B. Pant National Institute of Himalayan Environment . Farmers making bundles of kutki saplings under raincoat and tarp. Even in rain, the farmers stick to the planting calendar. Image courtesy of Guddi Devi. Conservation through cultivation “The goal is to conserve these species outside their natural habitats,” Kanwal said. India is home to 8,000 medicinal plant species , of which 140 have been prioritized for national cultivation support. Uttarakhand has listed 38 such species . Because rising commercial demand threatens wild populations, both in situ (in-nature) and ex situ (off-site cultivation) strategies are being deployed. In situ conservation focuses on preserving species within their natural environment by restricting harvesting or creating protected areas, providing protection to the species as well as the critical habitat. Kuniyal notes that in remote mountain terrains, limited enforcement personnel and strong illicit trade networks make in situ protection insufficient on its own. This gave way to ex situ conservation strategies, particularly the cultivation of MAPS. Scientists assess species based on market demand and threat levels defined by the IUCN and the Indian Red Data Book. “After prioritizing target species at state and regional levels, comprehensive conservation plans are drawn up, raising plants in institute nurseries and establishing new propagation sites directly in farmers’ fields,” Kanwal said. It is mostly women who are engaged in agriculture in the mountains. “Not only do they know how to cultivate but they are micro-observers too. If there is something, an issue, any disease, another thing, they immediately inform us,” Kanwal said. While women are key agricultural workers here, men help in transporting the material to nearby collection or purchasing destinations and also marketing the herbs. Ecosystem conservation Once species are selected by government scientific bodies like HRDI and the GB Pant Institute, saplings are distributed to high-altitude farmers either directly or through partner NGOs like Udyogini , which works with thousands of farmers across six regions in Uttarakhand. The central goal of Udyogini is women’s empowerment. For women in extreme remote villages, agriculture is a source of autonomy. “Udyogini provides us with 2,000 free saplings for a 1- naali plot,” 200 square meters or 2,160 square feet, Guddi explained. “We receive plants based on our land size, and the plots are officially registered. With experience, we now know exactly how many plants a field can accommodate and how to plant them properly.” Because these herbs need cooler conditions, cultivation cannot take place in lower village plots. The women farmers hike half a kilometer (0.3 miles) uphill to tend fields that were previously wastelands. Beyond farm plots, project interventions have also reintroduced rare and threatened species back into six degraded high-altitude pasture lands called bugyals . The project also addresses another major agricultural challenge: human-wildlife conflict. Traditional crops are frequently destroyed by wild boars and monkeys, but wild animals often leave medicinal herbs untouched , offering farmers a viable way to utilize abandoned land. (In a recent study , for example, it was shown that some MAPs, containing antifeedants or chemicals that are unpalatable to pests, were less attractive to elephants than rice crops.) “In terms of land conserved, we have brought over 172 acres [70 hectares] under conservation through 2022,” said Shivam Pandey of Udyogini. Role of women Harvesting begins as early as August and runs through November. During these months, nearly every woman in Ramni village can be seen hunched, working in the fields. Recently, 23 farmers from Ramni and neighboring villages shipped 150,000 kutki cuttings to another village for planting, with another batch of 200,000 ready for sale at 1 rupee ($0.01) per plant. “These days, we don’t have a single minute to spare,” Guddi said. “Some of us are harvesting, some are preparing cuttings, some are tying bundles and others are getting plants ready for sale. Everyone is fully occupied.” Because medicinal plants have a long gestation period of about three years, farmers rely on rotational harvesting. If they harvest sugandh bala one year, they harvest atees ( Aconitum heterophyllum ) the next. This year, the women in Ramni are primarily selling kutki cuttings. While kutki is typically harvested for its roots, selling saplings to neighboring villages allows new farmers to join the sector by securing an accessible local supply of planting material. Kanwal emphasizes how women have stepped into the forefront in this sector, mastering systematic cultivation, cleaning, grading and processing. Furthermore, since formal scientific data on high-altitude farming is still sparse, these women serve as crucial primary observers. “If a crop develops a disease or any other issue, they immediately notify us,” Kanwal said. “Our field team then steps in to provide solutions right away.
