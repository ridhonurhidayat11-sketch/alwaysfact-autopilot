Space reporter Leah Crane recommends the science-themed jigsaws from Nervous System Nervous System Puzzlingly beautiful: a science jigsaw If you’re looking for a quick and easy jigsaw to breeze through while watching holiday movies, Nervous System puzzles probably aren’t for you. But if you want something that’s part puzzle, part work of art, and that will keep you transfixed for hours, they’ll definitely do the trick. Nervous System makes laser-cut wooden puzzles with uniquely shaped pieces and science themes, and they are absolutely beautiful. I’m particularly a fan of their geode puzzles, featuring concentric wiggles of bright colour, including wiggly sides that defy every puzzler’s instinct to start with the edge pieces. For even more of a challenge, they make infinity puzzles that can be assembled and reassembled many different ways – those have no edge pieces at all, and some don’t even have a picture on them! Nervous System also sells absolutely stunning jewellery, lamps and sculptures, but to me the puzzles are the real show-stoppers. Nervous System puzzles , from $45 (£32) Advertisement Leah Crane The CL Companion 8×30 binoculars, as recommended by senior editor Chelsea Whyte Swarovski Optik Zoom in: splashing out on binoculars The eccentric filmmaker Werner Herzog famously declared that he travels with only a second set of underwear, a map, a toothbrush and a set of binoculars. I don’t travel quite so lightly, but on my next trip, I definitely want to add a pair of nice binos to my baggage. I tested out three sets at varying price levels, taking them down to my favourite spot on my local river and out to my backyard during a clear night with a half-moon overhead. The Nikon Prostaff P3 8×42 binoculars are extremely well balanced and were the most comfortable in my hands. They were particularly well-suited for twilight and brighter. The other Nikon set I tried was the Stabilised 12×25 S, which is much more compact. For my small hands that was fine, and the stabilisation was marvellous at reducing any shaking, both while looking at something fixed like the moon or moving quickly to track an eagle fishing for dinner. The stabilisation requires batteries, so these are probably best – suited to the more prepared adventurer. The stand – out pair was the CL Companion 8×30 from Swarovski Optik. I’ve never seen anything look better than they did through these lenses. They let in an enormous amount of light, making the world look like the brightness has been turned up. And the moon’s craters looked stunningly crisp. For the birdwatcher, wildlife lover or stargazer in your life, these would be a worthwhile, although pretty major, splurge. I have been testing that argument with my fantastic bat detector. The device picks up on the ultrasound frequencies emitted by bats as they fly overhead and converts them into clicks that we can hear. It also displays the frequency of the squeak, and that allows us to identify the species. In my London back garden I get mostly pipistrelles, our smallest bat at just 4 grams. Nagel is right – I don’t know what it is like to fly and catch moths by echolocation. But it is quite thrilling to hear the clicks as a bat swoops past in the dark, and that experience has got me a little closer to appreciating what it is like to be a bat. There are several options available, but I used the Magenta 5 bat detector from Wildcare. Magenta 5 bat detector , £105 Rowan Hooper Editor Catherine de Lange recommends the Timeshifter app Natee Meepian/Timeshifter Time for bed: a jet lag app If you have a jetsetter in your life, they are probably by definition the kind of person who is tricky to find a gift for. So why not gift them something money can’t buy (well, almost): an end to jet lag? Many moons ago, I was on a journalism fellowship about sleep at Harvard Medical School, when I visited the lab of sleep scientist Steven Lockley. I was fascinated by his experiments, including work he was doing with NASA astronauts, which looked at circadian rhythms, sleep and what happens when our bodies are out of sync with the natural rhythms of the day. Aside from astronauts, many of us who remain earthbound can still feel these effects – especially those who do regular shift work or long-distance travel. What’s more, a body clock that’s out of whack can have serious health consequences. I followed Lockley’s research closely over the years, and he was generous enough, whenever I travelled back to Boston, to make me a tailored jet-lag plan – when to sleep or nap in the days before and during my flight, as well as when to wear sunglasses and drink caffeine, all based on what time my flight was and the time difference. Fast forward about a decade, and this service is no longer a personal one – Lockley has created an app called Timeshifter, which allows users to plug in their flight details and instantly receive a step-by-step plan to minimise jet lag. There’s a version of the app for shift workers, too. I recently tried it on a trip to New York, purchasing a one-off plan rather than the subscription, and I can honestly say that I barely felt any jet lag at all – while my husband was falling asleep in his sushi at dinner. If a loved one has started talking about “Lifting PBs” and “protein-maxxing”, you could do much worse than getting them some proper footwear for their gym trips. There’s emerging evidence to suggest barefoot trainers strengthen foot muscles compared with regular trainers, including the small, stabilising muscles that improve balance. Vivobarefoot’s Motus Strength are specifically designed for weightlifting sessions – I’ve been wearing them to the gym for the last few weeks and am a total convert. You can also get barefoot shoes for hiking and trail running, for those who prefer the great outdoors to the weights floor.
