Science News from research organizations Facing extinction, the world’s largest sea turtle is making a surprising move Date: November 27, 2026 Source: Simon Fraser University Summary: The world’s largest sea turtle may be adapting to climate change by establishing new nesting grounds along Ecuador’s cooler coastline, where researchers have recorded unprecedented nesting activity and promising hatching rates. The discovery offers a rare glimmer of hope for a critically endangered population that has declined by more than 90% since the 1980s. Credit: Cristian Soledispa / Refugio de Vida Silvestre Isla Corazón y Fragatas The sandy beaches of Ecuador are emerging as a promising refuge for the world's largest sea turtle, a critically endangered species facing an uncertain future. Researchers documented an unprecedented number of eastern Pacific leatherback turtle nests across several Ecuadorian beaches during the 2025-2026 nesting season. These sites are located more than 1,000 miles from the turtles' traditional nesting grounds in Mexico and Costa Rica. According to a leading marine ecotoxicologist at Simon Fraser University, the shift in nesting activity suggests that leatherbacks may be responding to rising ocean temperatures by seeking out coastal environments with cooler conditions. "Historically, observing female Eastern Pacific leatherbacks on Ecuador's beaches was a rare or unusual event, but our findings suggest they are adapting their reproductive cycle to thrive and survive in coastal Ecuador," says Juan José Alava, adjunct professor at the School of Resource and Environmental Management and leading co-author of the study. "It signals new hope for a subspecies of leatherback on track for extinction in the eastern Pacific within 60 years. They are making their way alongside nature against all odds, representing hope for this species and other sea turtle species even as they reach tipping points." Cooler Waters May Offer a Survival Advantage For the study, published in the journal Chelonian Conservation and Biology , the research team examined more than 45 years of historical data from Ecuador's coastline. The records included sightings of reproductively mature females, nesting attempts, nest numbers, hatching success, and turtle strandings. The researchers compared these observations with El Niño and La Niña events, which produce recurring periods of warmer and cooler sea surface temperatures. Over the past decade, leatherback activity and nesting along Ecuador's coast have increased during La Niña events, when waters in the southeastern tropical Pacific become cooler. The team also discovered that the number of eggs laid per nest and the proportion of eggs that successfully hatched were similar to those recorded at established nesting sites farther north. According to Alava, some Ecuadorian nests even produced more hatchlings than typically observed. The eastern Pacific leatherback is unique among sea turtles in its ability to thrive in both warm and cold environments. Each year, these animals travel thousands of miles between tropical nesting beaches and feeding areas in subpolar Pacific waters, where jellyfish provide an abundant summer food source. Leatherbacks have even ventured into waters off Canada's west coast (British Columbia), with more than 150 documented records between 1931 and 2024. A Population Facing Extinction Since the 1980s, the eastern Pacific leatherback population has declined by more than 90%. Natural environmental pressures, including warm El Niño episodes intensified by human-caused climate change, have contributed to this dramatic decline. The turtles also face numerous threats from human activities, including accidental capture in fishing gear, plastic pollution, collisions with vessels, egg harvesting, and the destruction of nesting habitats. Alava emphasizes that securing the population's long-term survival will require continued monitoring, coordinated conservation initiatives, measures to reduce accidental capture in fisheries, and careful management of Ecuador's coastal areas to safeguard nesting beaches. "Management and conservation efforts should be championed in close consultation and participation with local and traditional ecological knowledge through coastal, community-grounded interventions." Bella's Remarkable Nesting Success On a moonlit January night in 2026, a female eastern Pacific leatherback named Bella by researchers emerged from the ocean at Ecuador's Playa de Crucita. As she searched for a suitable place to lay her eggs, artificial light from nearby streetlamps confused her. She made her way over a rocky breakwater before eventually reaching a sidewalk. Unable to find a suitable nesting location, Bella returned to the sea without laying her eggs. Two nights later, however, she emerged along a quieter stretch of beach. This time, she successfully dug a nest, laid 47 eggs, buried them beneath the sand, and returned to the water. Several weeks later, 44 baby turtles hatched and made their way toward the ocean. Researchers had fitted Bella with a tracking tag during her initial nesting attempt, allowing them to determine that she nested four to five times during 2026 alone. One nest was left undisturbed at its original location, while the remaining nests were transferred to the San Jacinto hatchery. By the end of the nesting season, 134 of Bella's 146 eggs had produced hatchlings, an impressive overall hatching success rate of 91.8%.
