By Mohana Basu Mohana Basu View author publications Search author on: PubMed Google Scholar Email Bluesky Facebook LinkedIn Reddit Whatsapp X Save article View saved research Chromosomes are held together with protein structures called cohesins, but these diminish with age. Credit: Power and Syred/Science Photo Library Researchers have engineered a ‘molecular glue’ that can stabilize chromosomes in ageing mouse eggs 1 . They say their artificial cohesion system could pave the way for improved fertility treatments for women over the age of 35. Chromosomes in animal eggs are held together by protein structures called cohesins. These help to ensure that chromosomes divide correctly during the process of cell division. But the level of cohesins declines as women age , which can cause chromosomes to separate early and be distributed incorrectly. The resulting eggs can have too many or too few chromosomes, a condition known as aneuploidy, which is a cause of infertility, failed in- vitro- fertilization (IVF) treatments and early pregnancy loss, particularly in women who are more than 35 years old. To prevent this premature separation of chromosomes in eggs, Melina Schuh, a biochemist at Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Göttingen, Germany, and her colleagues designed an artificial system called TALhesin. The system uses engineered proteins that attach to regions of the chromosome in which cohesin is usually present, these proteins then bind to one another. Many groups are trying to understand cohesin because it diminishes as women age, affecting fertility, says Bettina Mihalas, a reproductive biologist at the University of New South Wales in Sydney, Australia, but this is the first approach to replace the protein’s function with an engineered system. “It’s a very creative, well-engineered and well-thought-out approach,” she says. The findings were published in Science 1 on 26 November. Genetic instructions The researchers injected messenger RNA that encoded the engineered proteins into immature mouse eggs. The mouse eggs then produced the proteins. Using fluorescence microscopy imaging, the researchers confirmed that the TALhesin proteins attached to chromosome regions in which natural cohesin is normally lost. The artificial system reduced the frequency of early chromosome separation in older mice aged 13 months, 17 months and 20 months compared with untreated eggs. In 17-month-old mice, the proportion of eggs without any premature chromosome-separation errors increased from 46.7% to 74.1% in those treated with TALhesin, similar to what is observed in eggs from young mice . Chromatids — the two identical halves formed by a replicating chromosome — must separate at the right time during cell division. To enable this, the researchers added a ‘molecular scissor’ into their artificial system. This switched off the artificial cohesion system at the appropriate time. Human chromosomes The team has adapted the system to deliver engineered proteins that attach to regions of human chromosomes in which cohesin is usually present. Experiments in donated human eggs showed that the TALhesin proteins accumulated in these regions. The researchers also found that chromosomes with high levels of the engineered proteins tended to have smaller distances between chromatids. This demonstrates that the TALhesin system holds the chromosomes together. Enjoying our latest content?
