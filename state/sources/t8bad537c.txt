The supermassive black hole is seen as it was just 630 million years after the Big Bang. 40 million years further back in time than the previous record holder for the most ancient quasar. The discovery further deepens the mystery of how supermassive black holes grew so massive so soon into the history of the universe. That's because previously, scientists had theorized it should take 1 billion years of feeding and merging for black holes to reach supermassive status of millions or billions of times as massive as the sun. Quasars occur when supermassive black holes are surrounded by vast amounts of gas and dust on which they feed. The immense gravitational influence of the black hole causes this material, in the form of an accretion disk, to glow brightly and to blast out bright jets of plasma. That means quasars at the heart of galaxies in regions called active galactic nuclei (AGNs) like this one can often be brighter than the combined light of every star in the entire surrounding galaxy, but only briefly. The newly discovered quasar, designated J1504+6252 and nicknamed Dún Aonghasa, is in this brief phase of its existence. It was discovered among millions of objects in 18 months of data from Euclid.
