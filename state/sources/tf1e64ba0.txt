The U.S. Army’s plan to blow up 68 drums’ worth of a toxic compound could cause significant health problems for the local community, experts warn. Next Monday the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant will detonate 68 drums of lead azide, which is used in the manufacture of detonators, blasting caps and other components of explosives. As the chemical ages and degrades, it can form unstable crystals that can spontaneously explode at room temperature. The planned detonation is to prevent that from happening and must be done on-site because of the potential dangers of trying to transport it. The explosion will likely lead to large amounts of particulate matter in the air. While sodium azide, one of the two necessary components used to synthesize lead azide, poses no danger, according to the National Institutes of Health , lead is a highly dangerous substance . The greatest risk is to children, but it’s not limited to them, warns Ellen Wells, a public health professor at Purdue University. “The literature shows that they can have lifetime impacts from exposure at a young age,” she says. “Even at very low levels, it has shown that lead can influence neurological health, and that means [lower] IQ in children or cognitive development in children.” “But it can also influence cardiovascular health,” she says, including in adults. “There’s been a lot of studies in adults that even low levels of lead in adults can contribute, population-wise, to increased cardiovascular mortality.” Lead is considered a probable carcinogen by the Environmental Protection Agency, although the International Agency for Research on Cancer has found insufficient evidence as to whether lead exposure causes cancer. Local authorities have warned residents of the impending explosion. In a Facebook post on November 16, the city of Middletown, Iowa, acknowledged that the detonation will lead to lead-containing particles in the air. It warned residents in affected areas to stay inside with windows and doors closed on the day of the explosion and to have heating and cooling systems set to recirculate to avoid having outdoor air enter their homes. The post also asked residents to clean indoor and outdoor surfaces after the exposure period has ended. “If individuals take some actions to prevent lead from getting into their house, that can probably prevent longer-term exposure,” Wells says. But even if residents follow all safety procedures, Wells says the danger could remain because of lead settling into the soil, where it can stay for decades. “If people are walking around in the soil or using the soil, then they have a chance to get exposed to it again,” she says. “I would recommend trying to get your soil tested. Or, especially if you have children, try to get their blood lead tested, maybe even a few times after. Blood lead is reflective of exposures over the past 30 days or so, and blood lead tests are generally free, especially for younger children.” The Environmental Protection Agency issued an emergency permit for the detonation to the Army. In it, the EPA has tasked the Army with performing daily environmental monitoring measures to detect any residue in the area and with conducting a cleanup operation of any high-touch surfaces where lead is detected, such as parks and public facilities. A spokesperson for the Army did not respond to a request for comment. That cleanup doesn’t necessarily have to be complicated, Wells says. “With really, really highly contaminated sites, the procedure has been to remove the soil and replace it,” she says. “However, there has been some research showing that even if you put one inch of topsoil on top of soil that has been contaminated with lead, that can sort of trap the lead in the soil, so you reduce the immediate exposures.” Rights & Permissions Adam Kovac is a breaking news reporter at Scientific American .
