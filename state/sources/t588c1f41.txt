Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Cite article In Bolivia’s highlands, native Polylepis forests have declined dramatically, the result of demand for firewood, urban expansion and small-scale agriculture. Polylepis trees create microclimates that provide water far beyond the forests themselves, unlike non-native species that drain aquifers. A nonprofit-led initiative is working with Indigenous communities to plant hundreds of thousands of the slow-growing trees, though the scale and sustainability of the impact remains to be seen. The reforestation initiative prioritizes women’s empowerment, bridging gender divides through education in a bid to make the project more durable. See All Key Ideas The bare ridges of the Tunari mountain range rise high in Bolivia’s central Cochabamba department. Only the occasional stand of squat, shrubby trees and rust-red potato crops dot the slopes. It wasn’t always this way. The high-elevation landscape was once filled with native Kewiña ( Polylepis sp.) trees, a slow-growing tree belonging to the rose family that thrives well above the typical tree line, at up to around 4,450 meters (14,600 feet) above sea level. Today’s lack of vegetation reflects decades of clearing for firewood, construction and small-scale potato agriculture, as well as drier and warmer weather in the area linked to forest loss and climate change. Only 10% of the region’s original Kewiña tree coverage remains, biologists estimate . The trees were also decimated by urban expansion, intensifying wildfires and habitat fragmentation. In 2023, the IUCN listed one Polylepis species endemic to central Bolivia as endangered. Tunari National Park, covering about 309,000 hectares (763,500 acres), is home to a large, albeit much reduced, Kewiña population, which provide vital habitat for the near threatened Cochabamba mountain finch ( Poospiza garleppi ) as well as critical ecosystem services for the Cochabamba metropolitan area and beyond, to the nearby Amazon basin. Today, however, the region faces both chronic drought and increasing erosion on the mountain range’s slopes. A river bordered by Polylepis trees in Chiaraje, Bolivia, in Tunari National Park. Image by Benjamin Swift. “We used to harvest firewood from the Kewiña without replanting,” Andrea Vicente, a potato farmer in Chiaraje, a village perched at some 3,780 m (12,400 ft) in the Tunari mountains, told Mongabay in person. “But the forest was diminishing.” With support from conservation groups, Vicente’s community, along with other Indigenous Quechua villages, is reforesting endemic Kewiña forests across the Tunari mountains, rejuvenating water systems while supporting women to become financially independent. But while the initiative marks an important success in the face of growing deforestation, its impact is limited by its scale and its long-term impact remains to be seen. Reforesting the Bolivian headwaters Efforts to mitigate deforestation in the Tunari range have a complex history. In response to severe erosion in the 1960s, the Swiss Technical Cooperation financed the mass plantation of non-native pines ( Pinus radiata ) and eucalyptus ( Eucalyptus globulus) . The project ultimately degraded local ecosystems , exacerbated water scarcity and caused conflict with communities when promises to allow them to harvest the wood were broken, Jennifer Cahill, a research scientist at the Center for Biodiversity and Genetics at the Universidad Mayor de San Simón in Cochabamba, told Mongabay in a video call. In Chiaraje, Vicente and other residents are reversing that legacy. Their work is part of an initiative launched in 2022 by Bolivian nonprofit Faunagua to restore degraded watersheds in six communities in the Tunari mountains. Since the beginning, the organization has worked with communities to build shade houses where they grow Kewiña saplings before transplanting them in the surrounding hillsides. To date, they have planted nearly 500,000 seedlings, according to Rosmery Ayala, a biologist and the executive director of Faunagua. Members of the Warmi Kewiñas (Kewiña Women) tend to their Kewiña seedlings in Chiaraje, Bolivia. Image by Benjamin Swift. Community members are already noticing subtle shifts in the microclimate. “Now that we’ve been at this for four years, there’s more rain and humidity in the air,” Irma Vicente, Andrea’s sister, told Mongabay. Research shows that Kewiña trees both sow and harvest water: Their small, dense leaves intercept droplets of high-altitude fog and channel them into the soil. This is especially important in Tunari National Park, as parts of the forests sit above aquifer recharge zones. Because Kewiñas keep their stomata (microscopic pores in the leaves’ undersides) mostly closed in the harsh alpine conditions, they transpire minimal moisture, preserving groundwater rather than depleting it. The water retained by these native forests “supports both the tropical and the Andean parts of the Cochabamba department,” Ayala told Mongabay in person. Studies indicate that 80% of the water consumed in the Cochabamba metropolitan area originates directly or indirectly from catchment zones inside Tunari National Park. “What needs to be done is to plant massive numbers of Polylepis trees to contribute to the water supply,” Milton Fernández, another research scientist at the Center for Biodiversity and Genetics at the Universidad Mayor de San Simón, told Mongabay via video call. Yet reaching that scale requires long-term community organization, which involves much more than just planting trees. Reforestation as empowerment When Faunagua began reaching out to communities for its reforestation project, the team recognized a need to address local gender divides: While men often gathered for local union meetings, the women, living in homes dispersed over a vast area, had few shared social spaces. “Before, each woman lived in her own world – focused on her home, her family, and her daily work,” Norma Achocalla, the social technician at Faunagua who leads the project in Chiaraje, told Mongabay in person. Now, “solidarity has grown because there is a dedicated space where they can exchange ideas and share their experiences as women,” such as tips for natural remedies when their children are sick, she added. Polylepis seedlings growing in a shade house in Chiaraje, Bolivia. Image by Benjamin Swift. That shared space soon brought to light another need: basic literacy. “Many women had never had the opportunity to complete primary school,” Achocalla said. “[Some of them said] ‘we don’t know how to write our names, why don’t you teach us to read and write?’” In response, Faunagua integrated literacy classes into the program. For a village of around 300, the gatherings to both expand literacy skills and tend to Kewiña saplings became popular, drawing a core group of around 25 women. In 2023, they formalized their group, calling it the Warmi Kewiñas , meaning “Kewiña Women” in Quechua. While men also work on the Kewiña project, a dedicated group for women has been important to begin bridging the gender gap. “Now we women can read, write, and sign our names – we’ve all learned through the Kewiña project,” said potato farmer Andrea Vicente, who serves as the group’s president. The program has also organized environmental education and organizational strengthening classes, and Faunagua and its partners – along with Chiaraje residents – recently finished constructing a small building that will serve as a daycare center while the women work in the nearby shade house, the protective nursery for the saplings. Long-term sustainability While the Kewiña reforestation project has made substantial progress, with 110,000 seedlings grown this year, according to Ayala, questions about its long-term sustainability remain. Rosario León, a sociologist at CERES , a Cochabamba-based think tank, has worked on Indigenous political participation and communal forest management projects since the 1970s.
