We are finally gaining tantalising clues about what they are and how they came to be By Elise Cutts 24 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Christian Gralingen For almost a decade, the Kepler Space Telescope trailed Earth’s orbit, watching out for flickers in the light of distant stars. In 2011, two years after its launch, the NASA mission found what it was looking for: the signature of a roughly Earth-sized planet dimming out a tiny sliver of light as it transited across the face of its star. The identification of Kepler-10b, a planet almost 1.5 times the size of Earth that orbits a star around 600 light years away, was the first time we had spotted a rocky exoplanet – a world beyond our solar system. Since then, our rapidly expanding exoplanet catalogue has revealed plenty of surprises, but one above all: most planets in our galaxy aren’t like Earth. In fact, they are like nothing in our solar system at all. Out of the now 6451 confirmed exoplanets , by far the most common type are the “sub-Neptunes”, planets between Earth and Neptune in size that old theories of planet formation based on our solar system didn’t predict. And there are lots of them – over 3000 at the last count. “You know how evolution, like, really likes making crabs? Planet formation seems to really like making sub-Neptunes,” says astrophysicist Matthew Nixon at Arizona State University. Advertisement Read more Is our solar system a cosmic oddity? Evidence from exoplanets says yes But there is no sub-Neptune in our solar system, and that leaves researchers with a puzzle: what exactly are these mysterious planets? Now, aided by NASA’s orbiting high-tech thermal camera, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) , they are coming into sharper focus. New discoveries are answering some questions, but raising many more about what sub-Neptunes look like, how they form – and whether they might even host life. Exoplanetary science is still young. Astronomers spotted the first planet beyond our solar system, a gas giant circling a pulsar, in 1992. In 1995 came the first exoplanet around a sun-like star , a giant, close-orbiting “hot Jupiter” called 51 Pegasi b. But the hunt didn’t hit its high season until Kepler launched in 2009. It flew with just one instrument: a photometer that could precisely measure stars’ brightness and catch the telltale flickers of passing planets. By the time the mission ran out of fuel in 2018, it had discovered over half of all exoplanets we know today. Back in those early days, our ideas of how planets formed were guided by a sample of one: our solar system. It is widely agreed that our sun formed from the gravitational collapse of the solar nebula, a giant, spinning cloud of hydrogen and helium gas, plus a sprinkling of heavier elements. Once the young sun ignited, a disc of material continued circling it that would give rise to the planets. Neptune is one of two ice giant planets in the outer reaches of our solar system NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI; Image Processing: Joseph DePasquale (STScI), Naomi Rowe-Gurney (NASA-GSFC) Looking at our solar system, scientists broadly concluded that what planets look like came down to where they formed within this nebular Frisbee. Closer to the sun, it is too hot for anything but rock and metals to solidify and clump together, meaning less material to sweep up into planets. So, the planets of the inner solar system – Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars – ended up small and rocky. Meanwhile, in longer orbits beyond the “snow line”, where compounds such as water, ammonia and methane freeze out and form ices, baby planetary cores could get bigger. These bigger cores had the gravitational heft to gobble up hydrogen and helium gas from the solar nebula. They formed the outer solar system’s two gas giants, Jupiter and Saturn, and its more distant “ice giants”, Uranus and Neptune. Earth is the solar system’s largest rocky planet; Neptune, at around four times Earth’s diameter, is its smallest giant planet. The gap in size between the two seemed natural. Given how we thought planets form, worlds much bigger than Earth should be incredibly rare within the snow line. That, it turns out, isn’t true. Exoplanet astronomers have found everything from alien gas titans that would dwarf Jupiter to planetary pebbles comparable to Mercury. But among confirmed exoplanets with a known radius, about half are sub-Neptunes, many of them hewing in warm orbits very close to their stars. “They’re really the ultimate mystery,” says exoplanet astronomer Eliza Kempton at the University of Chicago. Kempton has been studying sub-Neptunes since before they were called sub-Neptunes. In 2010, even before Kepler found that first Earth-sized planet, she and her colleagues used the ground-based Very Large Telescope facility in Chile to observe the hazy atmosphere of a planet called GJ 1214 b some 48 light years away from Earth. With a radius 2.7 times that of Earth, but orbiting far closer to its star than Mercury does to the sun, that planet is now something of a poster child for the sub-Neptunes. But back then, it was a “super-Earth”. “We just called everything a super-Earth,” says Kempton. “This was before we even knew that small planets bifurcate into, like, the ‘smaller’ class and the ‘bigger’ class.” That divide refers to a striking discovery made in 2017 that deepened the mystery of sub-Neptunes further still. When astronomers used Kepler data to chart the number of exoplanets of different sizes, it revealed two bumps . One was centred on planets with radii about 1.4 times Earth’s, and another on those of about 2.4 Earth radii. In between, there was a gap with relatively few planets. The sub-Neptunes appeared to be not just one population of planets, but two. The discovery of this “radius valley” lent support to an idea that harsh radiation emitted from young stars, plus leftover heat from planet formation, blasts away the baby blankets of hydrogen and helium gas enveloping smaller planets. That leaves bare, rocky worlds that form the super-Earth peak. Bigger planets with more gravity, meanwhile, hold onto at least some hydrogen and helium. They end up as puffier, gas-cloaked worlds in the sub-Neptune peak. In this picture, terrestrial planets like Earth might be the naked cores of erstwhile sub-Neptunes. “If you’re ultimately interested in more Earth-like worlds, sub-Neptunes represent a kind of arrested stage of development. These are the things that stop short of becoming something like Earth,” says planetary scientist Raymond Pierrehumbert at the University of Oxford. “I think of them almost like fossilised atmospheres.” Unlike the ice giants of our solar system, sub-Neptunes close to their stars might form pretty dry – as “gas dwarfs” with no ice, just a rocky interior and a thick gas envelope made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. But there are other possibilities. The discovery of hot Jupiters – gas giant planets in close orbits where they couldn’t possibly have formed – helped scientists realise that planets can migrate, something we now know probably happened in our solar system, too . Sub-Neptunes could be ice worlds that form beyond their planetary system’s snow line and wander inwards. In some simulations, this migration produces a radius valley: one that divides small, rocky planets from big, watery ones. Since JWST’s launch in 2021, astronomers have been able to start differentiating between such scenarios. Its great infrared eye sees in wavelengths of light at which molecules such as water, ammonia, carbon dioxide and methane leave distinctive fingerprints on starlight passing through exoplanets’ upper atmospheres.
