See All Key Ideas JAKARTA — Environmental groups are questioning the effectiveness of a global organization that develops standards and certification for sustainable palm oil production after fires affected thousands of hectares in concessions belonging to its members in Indonesia — including areas certified under its sustainability scheme. The criticism came as the organization, the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO), held its annual conference in Jakarta, which did not include a dedicated session on the country’s months-long fire and haze crisis. The fires have raised questions about whether the RSPO is doing enough to investigate affected concessions and to ensure that members meet their obligations to prevent and respond to fires, as well as protect forests, peatlands and wildlife habitat. Analysis conducted by Indonesian NGO Satya Bumi claims to have identified indications of fires, typically hotspots not yet subject to ground truthing , across 3,753 hectares (9,274 acres) of concessions belonging to 110 RSPO members in 19 Indonesian provinces. A separate analysis by NGO the Indonesian Forum for the Environment (Walhi) identifies 14 corporate groups and one company, including Singapore and Malaysia-registered companies, that are both RSPO members and linked to fire indicators: Genting Plantations, SD Guthrie, Kuala Lumpur Kepong, United Plantations, First Resources, Golden Agri-Resources, Musim Mas, Wilmar International, Astra Agro Lestari, Bumitama Agri, Prime Agri Resources, Sumber Tani Agung Resources, Sawit Sumbermas Sarana, Triputra Agro Persada and Palmina Utama. Singapore-registered entities from four of the corporate groups — First Resources, Golden Agri-Resources, Wilmar and Bumitama Agri — are among 10 companies from which Singapore’s National Environment Agency (NEA) has sought information under the country’s Transboundary Haze Pollution Act. Executives from these four groups met Singapore’s deputy prime minister and two senior ministers on Oct. 28 to discuss the government’s assessment process. The RSPO continues to count the 15 companies or their corporate groups among its members, according to TuK Indonesia, an NGO that advocates for social justice in the agribusiness sector. RSPO membership does not mean that all plantations operated by a company or its affiliates are certified. The NGOs’ analyses have added to scrutiny of the sustainability body, whose members include growers, processors, traders and other companies involved in the palm oil supply chain. Calls for accountability The issue of fires in Indonesia prompted two protests outside the RSPO conference venue on Nov. 4, which Mongabay attended. In one protest, around 80 people gathered to demand that the certification body and its members be held accountable for forest fires and haze in Indonesia . Protesters chanted “Stop the fire, stop the haze, now!” and “The RSPO has to take responsibility!”, and carried placards reading “Sustainable on paper, burning on land” and “If we burn, you burn with us.” Protesters hold a banner calling on the Roundtable on Sustainable Palm Oil (RSPO) and its members to take responsibility for the impacts of Indonesia’s forest fires and haze outside the organization’s conference venue in Jakarta on Nov. 4, 2026. The protest was one of two demonstrations held during the RSPO’s annual meeting. Image by Hans Nicholas Jong/Mongabay. Saiful Wathoni, secretary-general of the Central Leadership of the Agrarian Reform Movement Alliance (PP AGRA), accused palm oil companies of burning forests and taking farmers’ land. He cited official data from Indonesia’s Ministry of Health on Oct. 21 that suggest there have been more than 300,000 reported cases of acute respiratory infections during the fire crisis, and said children had died, blaming the companies for the health impacts. The figure has since reportedly climbed to more than 400,000 as of Nov. 5. “It has been four months, and they have taken absolutely no responsibility,” Saiful said during the protest. “They have made no effort to help those who are sick recover, or to restore the forests they have burned. There is one thing we want to make clear: Those inside, who are holding this meeting, must take responsibility.” The protesters said the RSPO and its members should be at the sites of fires and haze, rather than gathering at a luxury hotel in Jakarta to discuss sustainability. A second protest, organized by TuK Indonesia and Walhi, accused the RSPO of greenwashing. “The RSPO and its members should not be in Jakarta; rather, they should be at the sites of the forest and land fires,” TuK Indonesia executive director Linda Rosalina said in a press release. Despite the multiple calls for the RSPO and its members to take action, the RSPO did not schedule a dedicated discussion on the haze crisis at the Jakarta meeting, according to Katie Yared, a senior associate on the forest commodities team at U.S.-based campaign group Mighty Earth. The only reference to “fire” in the conference agenda was a finance-focused “fireside chat” titled “The Next Test of Sustainability,” held on Nov. 4, she said. Mighty Earth and Satya Bumi sent the RSPO a public letter on Oct. 30 calling for an emergency session on the fires. The RSPO did not add one to the formal agenda, saying discussions about fires were integrated throughout the conference rather than confined to a single session, Yared said. Aisha Putri Utami, a project officer at industry watchdog Sawit Watch who attended the conference, said the event had not adequately addressed the crisis. “In my view, the Roundtable event did not respond to what was happening in Indonesia, even though it was being held in Indonesia,” she told Mongabay by phone. “Nor did it offer a concrete response to the impacts of companies that have already been certified. To me, it was business as usual. They were still trying to work out how RSPO could shape the image of the palm oil industry.” With no dedicated session on fires, NGOs that are RSPO members raised the issue during the organization’s General Assembly on Nov. 4, its annual governing meeting. In a statement read during the assembly and later shared with Mongabay, the NGOs, including Forest Peoples Programme (FPP), called for a special “no-fire task force” to develop guidance on preventing and responding to fires both inside concessions and in the wider landscapes around them. They also proposed a fund to rewet peatlands outside RSPO membership, and urged members to develop plans to retire plantations on peatlands that have burned and restore those areas through rewetting and the re-establishment of natural vegetation.
