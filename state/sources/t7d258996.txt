See All Key Ideas PORT LOKO, Sierra Leone — On a rainy Saturday morning, 57-year-old fisher Sheriff Kamara sat among other community members inside a wooden makeshift shelter in Sierra Leone’s Mahera Beach, waiting for the weather to improve. Along the shoreline, discarded plastic bottles, water sachets, bags and food wrappers lay scattered among old footwear and foam. For Kamara, plastic waste has become a regular problem, increasingly affecting his ability to fish and earn a living. “When I started fishing, it was rare to find anything in my net besides fish. But now, it is a weekly, sometimes a daily, problem,” he tells Mongabay. According to Kamara, a lot of single-use plastic waste frequently gets caught in his fishing nets, particularly near the shore after a heavy rain. Removing the entangled plastic waste takes considerable time and effort. “I find all sorts of plastic in my net almost every week. Removing the plastics with my apprentice’s help takes a lot of time,” he says. For fishermen like him, plastic pollution is not just an environmental concern but an additional burden on their daily work and livelihoods. To support the fishing communities, the government launched a fisheries and marine resources strategy in 2025, which provides a 10-year framework for promoting sustainable fisheries management, protecting marine ecosystems and improving livelihoods. It also calls for sustainable use of marine resources and stronger protection for the fisheries sector. Plastic waste, including discarded bottles and containers, accumulated along the shoreline beneath fishing boats. Image by Patricia Sia Ngevao. Financial cost of plastic pollution In Mahera, a coastal community in Lungi, Port Loko district, the problem extends beyond the beach’s appearance. Plastic pollution is affecting fishing activities, increasing operating expenses and creating additional financial pressure for those who depend on the fishing trade. Plastic waste continues to damage Kamara’s fishing nets, forcing him to spend money on mending and replacements. He says a recent tear cost him 80 leones (about $3.50) to repair and halted his fishing activities for two days. “On certain days, my net catches only plastic and not fish,” he says. A typical fishing trip requires approximately 20 liters (5.3 gallons) of petrol, costing around 800 leones ($34.70), according to Kamara. The additional time spent removing plastic materials and repairing damaged nets increases the fisher’s cost of fishing. For Kamara, whose daily earnings depend on the quantity of fish he brings home, these additional expenses reduce his net income, making an already demanding occupation more difficult. Across Mahera, some families depend not only on fishing but other related activities including sand mining and petty trading, and plastic pollution threatens these sources of income. Sierra Leone’s medium-term national development plan (2024-30) estimates the country’s fisheries sector employs approximately 1 million people, or around 14% of the population. The industry produces around 150,000 metric tons of fish annually and contributes to about 12% of the country’s gross domestic product (GDP). Fisherman Sheriff Kamara sitting in the makeshift shelter at Mahera. Image by Patricia Sia Ngevao. Sheku Sei, acting director of fisheries at the Ministry of Fisheries and Marine Resources, agrees there are multiple costs due to plastic pollution. “Plastic pollution poses a growing threat to Sierra Leone’s fisheries, damaging marine habitats, threatening aquatic life and potentially affecting fish breeding grounds and productivity. Combined with overfishing, illegal fishing and other environmental pressures, it increases the vulnerability of fish stocks and fishing communities,” he says. The fisheries ministry, he says, recognizes the need for stronger marine protection, improved waste management and multistakeholder collaboration to tackle pollution and ensure sustainable fisheries and food security for present and future generations, he tells Mongabay. Women bearing financial burdens The economic consequences of plastic pollution, however, are not limited to fishers. Women who buy, process and sell fish in Mahera have to bear additional expenses when fishing activities are disrupted. Some women buy fresh fish directly from fishers for resale, while others smoke the fish and sell them farther inland. Isatu Jalloh has been involved in the local fishing business for more than a decade. She says sales are delayed when fishers have to spend time removing plastic waste from their nets, impacting the women who rely on timely delivery. When boats return late, women who buy fresh fish for resale sometimes have to first purchase ice to preserve fish. Those involved in fish smoking also require firewood, adding more expense to their business. “I spend between 80 and 90 leones [$3.47-$3.90] on ice blocks, while firewood can cost between 50-60 leones [$2.17-2.60],” she says. These additional expenses reduce the seller’s earnings, particularly when the quantity of fish available for sale is limited. Jalloh tells Mongabay that delays and smaller catches can also affect fish prices. When fishermen spend more time at sea, consume additional fuel but return with less fish stock, they increase the selling price to recover their losses. “As traders, we pay more, but buyers end up paying much more,” she notes. Discarded plastic bottles, sachets and other waste litter a stretch of beach. Image by Patricia Sia Ngevao. Widespread coastal pollution The situation in Mahera points to a much wider plastic waste problem affecting coastal communities across Sierra Leone. A 2024 study recorded 72,901 pieces of litter weighing 1,246 kilograms (2,746 pounds) across four beaches: Bailor, Tintafor, Kent and Chepo over a period of 11 months. Plastics accounted for approximately 70% of the items, mainly bottles, bottle caps and water sachets. The study covered four beaches in the country with its findings providing evidence of the wider coastal pollution challenge facing Sierra Leone. Stephanie Lavelle, the study’s lead author, recommends affordable beach monitoring involving schools, universities and local organisations to reduce plastic pollution. She also advocates efforts to help fishermen bring waste caught in their nets ashore. In her study, reference is made to the harmful practice of burning plastic waste, releasing chemicals to the atmosphere. Plastic pollution is particularly difficult to address because discarded materials can travel through drains and rivers or move along coastlines through tidal waves and ocean currents, making it difficult to trace their origin, Lavelle says in a video interview. Wider consequences of plastic pollution Beyond its impact on local fishing, plastic pollution also contributes to wider environmental and public health issues in Mahera. “Plastic pollution threatens our ecosystems, biodiversity and public health. Tackling it requires stronger waste management systems and collective action,” Sierra Leone’s minister of environment and climate change, Jiwoh Emmanuel Abdulai , tells Mongabay. Improper waste disposal contributes to flooding, while marine plastic waste threatens ecosystems and fishing livelihoods, says Mohamed Bah, communications director at Sierra Leone’s National Disaster Management Agency.
