Sign Up I’m starting to really wonder just how stable our solar system really is. To be clear, I’m not worried about planets being flung away into interstellar space any time soon. But “soon” means something different to an astronomer than it does to a normal person. Millions of years is “soon.” A few billion? Now that’s a bit later. Fail to factor in deep time, though, and it’s all too easy to mistakenly think that the solar system is static, that it has always been as it is now. After all, it looks well ordered. The inner four planets are all small and rocky, while the outer four are swollen giants with thick atmospheres, and a belt of asteroids splits the two types like a demarcation zone. One clue turned up when astronomers found the first exoplanets orbiting sunlike stars in the 1990s. These were “hot Jupiters,” gas giants akin to our solar system’s biggest planet but huddled in orbits far closer to their stars than Mercury is to our own. They could not have formed there, so astronomers postulated they were instead born farther out and gradually migrated inward via gravitational interactions with the same circumstellar disk of material from which they first coalesced. The hot Jupiters were only an early wave of weird worlds. In some other systems, planets orbit on wildly tilted trajectories, and, here and there, astronomers have found planets that actually revolve around their host stars backward . These oddities imply some sort of chaotic interaction at work, such as planets perturbing each other as they pass by and getting tossed into bizarre configurations. Then, in 2005, scientists proposed what is now called the Nice model (after the French city where the scientists met and developed the idea). This radical hypothesis is that the solar system’s giant planets formed closer to the sun than their present positions, only to migrate outward to their current arrangement as a result of gravitational tussles with each other. The Nice model, and its many subsequent modifications, brought the “migratory” view of planets home, and this key insight has been a staple of solar system dynamics ever since. I consider planetary migration’s place in celestial mechanics as similar to the role of evolution in biology or plate tectonics in geology: nothing makes sense unless you include it. It’s the framework of how we understand the way planetary orbits evolve. It also strongly implies these changes are ongoing. The solar system continues to evolve. In one sense, we already knew this to be true: The sun won’t last forever. As I wrote in my September 18, 2026, Universe column , in six billion years or so, changes in our star’s core will lead to it swelling up and becoming a red giant. The puffed-up sun will slowly shed its outer layers, causing its mass to decrease and its gravity to weaken, and in response, the planets will gradually move farther out from it. Some astronomers had previously assumed that this relatively gentle process meant the outer solar system planets would orbit stably for immense periods of time, possibly as much as 100 billion years—long after the sun itself is quite dead. There’s evidence that suggests the sun’s future evolution won’t be a smooth, sedate process , though: instabilities in its core will result in episodic paroxysms in which our star’s luminosity will briefly increase drastically. These sporadic stellar spasms will cause the sun to lose mass in powerful but brief pulses instead of in a smooth flow. And there’s more: research into how the sun loses that mass shows that it won’t be blown off symmetrically in all directions . Instead each pulse will send material off asymmetrically , in a relatively random direction, like a sudden gust of wind. If this is indeed the case, it will a have profound impact on the far future of the solar system. A team of astronomers looked into this and published its results on September 21 in the Astrophysical Journal Letters . The researchers simulated how the sun will lose mass over the course of several billion years—from when it starts to become a red giant to the inevitable end of the process, in which it loses all its outer layers, exposing its core to space. At that point it will be a white dwarf, a hot, dense ball with about half the current solar mass but squeezed into a volume only about the size of Earth. In their simulations, they assumed that the solar mass loss will occur in a series of thousands of small burps, each one ejecting about 0.01 percent of the sun’s material into space. Also, these solar eructations will form a broad funnel shape instead of expanding spherically. That last bit is critical. By focusing the emitted material, the ejection acts like a rocket, pushing on the sun. The amount of force imparted is relatively small, only enough to give it a very small kick. But this does accelerate our star, each adding a few meters per second to its velocity in space. Each kick is in a random direction. Now, you might think this would average out to zero net force acting on the sun, but that’s not the case. This situation mimics that of a “ random walk ,” in which taking a step in a random direction slowly leads away from your starting point. After a finite number of steps, you’ll have moved in some direction as if pushed there. In the same way, random ejections of matter can push a star in some overall direction as well, leading to the final velocity of several kilometers per second. This may not seem like much, but it will actually have a big effect on the planets. Well, not the inner planets so much— we already know what happens to them , and it’s nothing good. Mercury and Venus will be consumed by the expanding sun. Earth may escape that fate, but both it and Mars will be broiled as our star grows enormous. But, according to the new work, the outer planets don’t fare much better. In practical terms, as these kicks push around the sun, the planets’ orbits around it become more elongated. In a disturbing 77 percent of the simulations, the outer planets migrate so much because of this mass loss that eventually one crosses the orbit of another—and this happens early on in the process. In at least one case, Saturn is lost within just a few million years of the sun becoming a red giant! These gravitational encounters lead to ejected planets or system disruptions in 40 percent of the simulations, long before the sun even becomes a white dwarf. After three billion years, total disruption occurs in a staggering 90 percent of the simulations. Previously astronomers thought that as the sun died and the orbits of the planets expanded, nearby passes of other stars would eventually disrupt the solar system after many tens of billions of years . This new work indicates that by the time these close encounters happen, there won’t be much of a solar system left to disrupt. That’s a little depressing. If there’s an upside, it’s that this won’t happen for many billions of years from now. As far as we can tell, the planets—including our own—will be fine for eons to come. But if you have plans for seven or eight billion years hence, you might want to reassess them. Rights & Permissions Phil Plait is a professional astronomer and science communicator in Virginia.
