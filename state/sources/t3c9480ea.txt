Photograph: Willy Kurniawan/Reuters Climate science IPCC report delay will threaten global effort to tackle climate crisis, says top scientist Johan Rockström, one of world’s leading climate experts, says slowing down scientific work puts cooperation at risk Fiona Harvey , Graham Readfearn and Damien Gayle in Antalya Thu 19 Nov 2026 02.16 EST Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 10.06 EST Share Prefer the Guardian on Google Climate science is “foundational” to the Paris agreement and worldwide progress on tackling global heating, and attempts to delay new scientific reports threaten global cooperation on tackling the crisis, one of the world’s leading climate scientists has warned. Johan Rockström, the director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, said the world’s leading body of climate scientists, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, must be allowed to work as quickly as possible on its next comprehensive assessment of global knowledge of the climate systems . Some countries are holding up work on the three-part report in a row over the body’s role and representation of developing countries. One group has argued that racist assumptions underpin some of the body’s recommendations. Rockström said the reports were fair. “The IPCC is objective, evidence-based science, and it’s the only thing that we can reliably stand on as a basis for our policymaking,” he said. “The IPCC is the summary of everything done voluntarily by scientists from the entire world, global south and global north, that collectively carries out a synthesis [of established research].” View image in fullscreen Rockström speaking at Cop31 this week. Photograph: Beyza Comert/Anadolu/Getty Images The IPCC’s work is used to inform the annual climate “conference of the parties” (Cop) to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. Its next assessment, called AR7, the seventh in a series dating back to 1988, under way, and it is hoped it will be completed in 2028. In the autumn of that year, nations will gather for Cop33, where the second “global stocktake” under the Paris agreement will be thrashed out. This is the mechanism by which progress towards the core Paris goal, of limiting temperature rises to 1.5C above pre-industrial levels, will be judged. The stocktake is almost certain to find that current pledges to cut greenhouse gas emissions are inadequate and must be improved. The UN has already found the world to be on track for a 2.8C temperature rise , a level previous IPCC reports have found would result in severe and, in some cases, irreversible consequences, such as the melting of ice caps and glaciers, sea level rises, coral bleaching and extreme weather. But some countries – chiefly Russia, India and the Arab group of countries including Saudi Arabia – want to delay publication of AR7 until 2029, after the global stocktake is completed. They argue, with the backing of some climate campaigners, that developing countries are disadvantaged in trying to submit science to the IPCC. As a result, some claim, the work is permeated by racist assumptions that guarantee the privilege of developed countries. Lidy Nacpil, the coordinator of the Asian Peoples’ Movement on Debt and Development, told the Guardian the IPCC’s scenarios for cutting greenhouse gas emissions – known as “mitigation” in UN jargon – were “biased against the interests of the global south”. For instance, she said, one assumption was that cutting emissions should be done where it was most cost-efficient. “And where is it cheap to do mitigation? In the global south.” Changing the assumptions would produce different scenarios, Nacpil said. Nacpil said singling out Saudi Arabia, “whose behaviour we’re not always sympathetic with”, was “also very racist”. She asked: “Who started the fossil fuel industries in this region? Who enjoyed it for now dozens of decades? Whose multinational companies really grew from the extraction of oil and gas in the Middle East? This is not us taking sides. This is us saying: ‘But the pot is calling the kettle black.’ And they’re using their economic, political, and racial power to do this.” Rockström rejected this. “The pathways to the future represent different socioeconomic realities around the world,” he told the Guardian, in an interview at Cop31 . “This is based on the best evidence we have, and this evidence has no north or south bias. It represents the best peer-reviewed science of our understanding of how to close income gaps in poor countries versus rich countries, demographic changes, health projections, and of course also therefore forecasting socioeconomic development. It’s an objective, evidence-based analysis of the reality.” He added: “We have to defend that objective science. We should not use science as the arena to fight for justice or human rights or even democracy. That’s not the role of science.” Many other developing countries, including small islands and the least developed countries, have called for the IPCC report to be completed without delay. Raju Pandit Chhetri, a negotiator from Nepal, said calling the IPCC racist was “a political line”. “What we need to do in this process is really respect the science,” he said. “Coming from Nepal, I can tell you the impacts in just a few hours’ time: losing thousands of lives, let alone billions of pounds worth of property. China, India and Saudi Arabia all supply leading scientists to the organisation, but insiders acknowledge that parts of the process are dominated by European researchers. The IPCC said that out of 664 appointed authors working on the drafts of its three working group reports, 51% came from developing countries and economies in transition – an increase of almost half, compared with the last report in 2021. Each chapter across the three reports includes at least one developing-country chapter scientist. A spokesperson for the IPCC said: “IPCC is absolutely committed to promoting diversity and inclusion and has taken multiple steps to do so during its seventh cycle. As the UN body assessing the science related to climate change, we uphold and stand by the core values of integrity, professionalism and respect for diversity and gender. In the current cycle, the IPCC introduced institutional measures that implement equity and inclusivity.” Rockström said even if AR7 was delayed, the science in AR6 would be enough to show that current pledges, made last year before Cop30 , must be vastly improved. “We have all the evidence we need. Even if you based your updates [on emissions pledges] only on AR6 , we would still be making enormous progress,” he said.
