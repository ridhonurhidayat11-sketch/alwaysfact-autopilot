Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Cite article Between 2017 and 2022, increased tequila production in Mexico drove greater demand for its raw material, blue agave, but also caused social and environmental damages. The tequila regulatory body and state authorities have failed to halt the expansion of agave into forest areas. Jalisco lost around 23,368 hectares (about 57,740 acres) of forest annually between 2019 and 2021, a period that coincided with the expansion of blue agave fields. Between 2017 and 2025, the area planted with tequila agave nationwide increased by 218%, reaching 276,043 hectares (about 682,110 acres). See All Key Ideas For decades, Manuel García Castro has grown corn, squash and beans to feed his family and livestock. In 2019, he followed in the footsteps of prosperous local farmers and dedicated a portion of his land in the highlands of Jalisco, western Mexico, to planting blue agave, used to make tequila. A friend persuaded him to plant 15,000 shoots on 3 hectares (7.41 acres) of his land and in other areas where his cattle grazed. García Castro would provide the labor; his friend, the capital. “We have to give it a shot to see if there’s some way to make money from something else,” was their thinking, he told Mongabay. Back then, raising livestock didn’t bring in much money and expenses were high. They invested 500,000 Mexican pesos (around $25,300) in plants, pesticides and fertilizers, not including payment for García Castro’s own labor and that of the farm worker who helped him. Four years later, in 2023, hopes of selling the blue agave began to fade. Sale prices per kilogram plunged by as much as 92% compared with those between 2017 and 2019. The business was no longer profitable in the short term. Manuel García and his partner abandoned the fields two years ago. The tequila boom Tequila has a protected designation of origin in the states of Guanajuato, Nayarit, Tamaulipas, Michoacán and Jalisco. The drink is considered a national brand and a symbol of Mexican identity. Over the past decade, the tequila industry has boosted its exports and developed premium and niche brands. Between 2016 and 2025, the Tequila Regulatory Council (CRT) recorded an increase in production from 273.3 million liters (72.2 million gallons) to 583.5 million l (154 million gal). Production peaked at 651.4 million liters in 2022. Entrance to the town of Tequila, birthplace of the traditional Mexican drink. Image by Fabricio Atilano. In 2020, Mexico exported 286.7 million l (75.7 million gal) of tequila, and 418.9 million l (110.6 million gal) two years later. Between 2017 and 2018, the sale price of blue agave remained between 26 and 30 pesos per kilogram (just under $2), peaking in 2021 at 32 pesos per kilogram, according to industry website agaves.pro . Those economic conditions attracted new producers, including Manuel García, who replaced corn on his plots with blue agave. Agave replacing forests In 2019, when blue agave prices boomed, Maximiano Bautista Andalón, a geospatial specialist and academic at the Western Institute of Technology and Higher Education (Iteso) in Mexico, began using satellite imagery to document the expansion of agave farms in Jalisco. That year, he counted roughly 100,000 hectares (about 247,100 acres) planted with agave only in Jalisco. Six years later, the area had reached 200,000 hectares (494,210 acres). The researcher found that this expansion affected natural ecosystems and created monocultures, as many farmers stopped growing food while others took over forest and rainforest areas. “Agave is a complex issue, especially because of how it was distributed: in areas with pine forest, oak forest, or right next to mid or high-altitude areas where other crops used to grow,” Bautista Andalón said. “It is a highly industrial model in which almost no other little plant survived among the agave fields.” Agave plantations in the Tequila Valley. Image by Fabricio Atilano. In 2023, a report by the University of Guadalajara and a coalition of 43 states and provinces from 11 countries, including Mexico, highlighted the expansion of agave production for tequila and mezcal as one of the drivers of forest loss. “The increase in demand and prices for these distilled beverages, especially tequila, has triggered a cyclical expansion of agave cultivation into these ecosystems,” the report warns. Between 2019 and 2021, a period coinciding with the tequila boom, Jalisco recorded its highest deforestation levels losing 23,368 hectares (about 57,740 acres) annually. Neither the National Forestry Commission (Conafor) nor the Jalisco Department of Environment and Territorial Development responded to Mongabay’s interview requests for this article. The agave landscape around the town of Tequila, designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in western central Mexico, has spread indiscriminately across the state. By analyzing satellite imagery, researcher Bautista Andalón found that some of the newest agave plantations were preceded by fires, land clearing or tree cutting, or were established near water sources important to local communities. Sequence showing fire damage followed by new agave crops on a property near Cajititlán Lagoon in 2019, 2021 and 2025. Images courtesy of Maximiano Bautista Andalón/Iteso, using historical satellite imagery available on Google Earth. Southern Jalisco is also experiencing the impacts of agave expansion. University of Guadalajara researcher Oscar Raúl Mancilla is part of a scientific group that has studied land-use change in the region for 20 years and found that blue agave cultivation increased by 60% in the Autlán region , in southwestern Jalisco, between 2015 and 2026. “Thousands of hectares have shifted from tropical dry forest, livestock farming or other types of crops to agave cultivation,” Mancilla told Mongabay. Crossing the boundary The area planted with tequila agave nationwide increased 218% between 2017 and 2025, according to the Ministry of Agriculture and Rural Development In 2021, the Tequila Regulatory Council (CRT) and the Jalisco government implemented the Environmentally Responsible Agave (ARA) certification to guarantee deforestation-free brands that comply with international sustainability standards. Eduardo Ibarra Arellano, the CRT’s sustainability project manager, told Mongabay that they provided factories and farmers with a map outlining more than 2.67 million hectares (about 6.6 million acres) available for plantations across Jalisco’s 125 municipalities, as well as others in Guanajuato and Michoacán. Farmers must confirm that their crops lie within the designated areas and register with the CRT under Official Mexican Standard NOM-006-SCFI-2012 , which sets the specifications that define what can be produced, certified and sold as tequila. The tequila boom drove an increase in demand for agave and the creation of new tequila companies. Image by Fabricio Atilano. “If that property was a forest or a protected area before 2016, they could not be granted authorization to plant agave there,” Ibarra Arellano said. However, producers know they can turn to coyotes, informal traders between producers and tequila manufacturers, to sell the plants illegally. Ibarra Arellano said the map took watersheds into account when delimiting planting zones and that they follow a strict zero-deforestation policy. The Jalisco Department of Environment and Territorial Development has stated the ARA certification has prevented the deforestation of 17,000 hectares (about 42,000 acres) of forest. However, the mechanism has fallen short. In November 2025, the Federal Attorney for Environmental Protection (Profepa) shut down an illegal property in the municipality of Valle de Guadalupe, Jalisco, where land-use change for agave cultivation affected 202 hectares (about 500 acres) of oak forest. Agave fields in Jalisco extend into protected areas such as La Primavera Forest , where the ARA map identifies at least nine eligible crop areas that appear to contain rows of agave.
