See All Key Ideas KAOHSIUNG, Taiwan — At dawn, just as the birds begin to sing, Chiang Huang-nu usually puts on her traditional straw hat and walks through the wooded grounds of a former sugar factory in Ciaotou district in southwest Taiwan. Along the way, the 83-year-old might greet around 30 other regular strollers, most of them retirees. Chiang has taken these morning walks among the greenery since she was a child. For the past several months, the walk has involved chatting with fellow community members about the community’s fight against the government’s plan to redevelop the area and demolish their beloved wood. For decades, Chiang and fellow residents have spent their mornings in the Ciaotou Woodland, a 154-hectare (380-acre) urban forest surrounding the historic Ciaotou Sugar Refinery. Located in the eponymous district, north of Kaohsiung city center in southwestern Taiwan, the woodland is home to 100,000 trees, according to local environmentalists, and a habitat for local wildlife. “Some of the trees were planted during the Japanese colonial period,” which lasted from 1895 to 1945, Chiang told Mongabay. “They are more than 100 years old. They protect us from the heat in summer.” For the past year, however, residents can only walk in the nearby park. The woodland has been fenced off and made inaccessible to the public, following a government order. Its trees are under threat from the urban redevelopment project. On a November morning, three elderly men in their 70s were sitting on plastic chairs in the middle of the park, sheltered from the sun by the trees. “I would rather see this place preserved, but what can we do against the government’s decisions?” said one 77-year-old man. His two companions nodded in agreement. Three regular morning walkers sitting in the Ciaotou Sugar Industry Culture Park in Ciaotou district, Kaohsiung, Taiwan, on Nov. 12, 2026. Image by Aylin Ho for Mongabay. Faced with such powerlessness, residents have found a glimmer of hope and a figure of resistance in Chiang. The octogenarian, nicknamed “Forest Grandma” for her passion for protecting the woodland, is running in Taiwan’s local elections on Nov. 28. She is contesting a city council seat in Kaohsiung, the island’s third most populous city. Her only manifesto policy is to protect the wood. Her slogan is “Vote for trees, not parties.” “Of course I’m going to vote for her,” a 70-year-old resident, who only gave her surname, Tsai, told Mongabay. “If you destroy this forest, what will we have left? We won’t have anywhere to go for a walk.” Like Chiang, other morning residents on the morning walk come from working-class families. Either they themselves, their parents, or both were employed by the factory, which began operating in 1902. After the sugar refinery ceased operations in 1999, the surrounding area was transformed into a vast woodland. For around 15 years, the old woodland became a place for many residents from Ciaotou and nearby districts to walk in the mornings or to hike or cycle on weekends. The Ciaotou Woodland in Ciaotou district, Kaohsiung, Taiwan. Image courtesy of Forest City Association, Taiwan. Forest Grandma has no formal education and is illiterate. She does not speak Mandarin, the official language in Taiwan, and communicates only in Taiwanese Hokkien, the language spoken by descendants of early immigrants from China’s Fujian province who moved to Taiwan centuries ago. Born into a poor farming family in 1943, during the Japanese colonial rule, she was given a Japanese name, Tamako. Her current name, Huang-nu, was registered after the Nationalist government retreated to Taiwan in 1949, following a civil war in mainland China. For Chiang Huang-nu, who worked at the Ciaotou Sugar Refinery for years, her political involvement did not stem from an electoral ambition but from a long-standing attachment to a place she has known since her youth. “Destroying this forest means destroying my entire life,” she said. The idea for her candidacy came from her son, Chiang Yao-hsien, also known as Jungle Chiang. The 68-year-old is a heritage and environmental activist. In 2001, two years after the Ciaotou Sugar Refinery was shut down, he turned the complex into an artists’ village, where his mother volunteers to work as a sweeper. “My mother is popular in the area,” Chiang Yao-hsien told Mongabay. “Her background as a former factory worker speaks to an entire generation. I knew she could bring people together around our cause. So I encouraged her to run.” Large-scale urban forest In 2007, APEC nations made a regional commitment to increase forest cover by 20 million hectares (50 million acres) by 2020. Taiwan pledged to contribute to this goal by increasing its reforested areas by 11,550 hectares (28,540 acres). As part of the commitment, a woodland was planted near Tienpao River in Ciaotou between 2010 and 2012. Environmentalists praise the woodland as a rare example of a large-scale urban forest in Kaohsiung. “These trees are not even 20 years old, and today, while we continue to take part in APEC meetings, the authorities are considering destroying this forest for a real estate and land development project,” said Chuang Chieh-jen, chairperson of the Forest City Association, Taiwan (FCAT). According to environmentalists, the development would threaten the habitats of fireflies and at least eight protected bird species in Taiwan, including the crested serpent eagle ( Spilornis cheela ), ring-necked pheasant ( Phasianus colchicus ), Asian emerald dove ( Chalcophaps indica ), collared scops owl ( Otus lettia ) and black-naped oriole ( Oriolus chinensis ). The crested serpent eagle ( Spilornis cheela ) is a protected species in Taiwan. Image by gwyn0429 via iNaturalist ( CC BY-NC 4.0 ). Both Chuang and Chuang Huang-nu have also raised concerns that removing the trees would weaken the district’s flood resilience. Some recall that before the woodland existed, Tienpao River would overflow and flood their homes. The Taiwanese government had plans to construct a new town in Ciaotou as early as 1994. In the first phase, it built a residential district in Ciaotou. In the second phase, it constructed a science and technology park, which opened in early 2026. The push for the third phase is related to the growth of the semiconductor industry in southern Taiwan, which received a boost with the “Southern Silicon Valley” project approved by the central government last year. The 227 hectares (561 acres) of land around the former sugar factory, including the woodland, will be converted into commercial and residential zones for high-tech businesses and their workers. It will also build roads connecting Phases 1 and 2, including a 60-meter-wide (197-feet-wide) thoroughfare that will cut through the forested area. In response to Mongabay’s query, the National Land Management Agency (NLMA), under the Ministry of the Interior, said that a significant part of the wood is considered artificial forest rather than natural forest.
