NASA/Robert Markowitz For 65 years, NASA’s Johnson Space Center in Houston has helped shape human spaceflight, from the Gemini missions that demonstrated rendezvous and docking techniques needed for Apollo to the research conducted aboard the International Space Station, which has supported a continuous human presence for more than 26 years. Today, Johnson teams are preparing crews and systems for NASA’s next chapter: returning astronauts to the lunar surface and building the capabilities needed for a long-term human presence at the Moon. Across those missions, generations of flight controllers, engineers, scientists, instructors, medical specialists, and other experts have helped crews live and work beyond Earth. Johnson is home to NASA’s Orion and Moon Base programs, and its teams continue to support space station operations, astronaut training, spacecraft development, mission operations, and human health. Mercury and Gemini: Preparing for Apollo When the Manned Spacecraft Center officially opened on Nov. 1, 1961, NASA had already flown two suborbital Mercury missions, with the first orbital flight just months away. Most Project Mercury activities remained at Cape Canaveral in Florida to maintain continuity, but the new Project Gemini took up residence in Houston, housed in temporary leased facilities across the city while construction of the new Clear Lake campus continued. The new Mission Control Center became NASA’s primary flight control center in June 1965, with the Gemini IV mission that included the first American spacewalk. Houston became the nerve center for American human spaceflight, overseeing the remaining eight Gemini missions before transitioning to the Apollo program. Following the tragic Apollo 1 fire in January 1967, managers, engineers, and astronauts at the Manned Spacecraft Center applied lessons learned to make the spacecraft safer and ensure the safety of its astronauts, resuming crewed missions in October 1968. Managers in Houston devised a daring plan to send Apollo 8 on a circumlunar flight.
