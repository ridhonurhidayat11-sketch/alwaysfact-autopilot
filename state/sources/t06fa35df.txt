What could go wrong? A nationwide ground stop offered one answer. With Thanksgiving days away, experts still aren’t satisfied with the official explanation By Ramin Skibba edited by Eric Sullivan The nation’s air traffic control towers rely on a sprawling network of aging communications equipment that the Federal Aviation Administration is trying to modernize. shocky/Shutterstock The trouble may have started with a single router. Last Friday afternoon a problem in the Federal Aviation Administration’s telecommunications network prompted the agency to halt departures at every airport in the country. The extremely rare nationwide ground stop on November 13 lasted more than four hours, and the fallout took days to clear: more than 18,000 flights canceled and more than 28,000 delayed. With Thanksgiving a week away, the agency’s engineers are still scrambling to figure out what went wrong. At a November 14 press conference , Secretary of Transportation Sean Duffy blamed the ground stop on the FAA’s “incredibly old telecommunication system.” By his account, the problem surfaced during routine work by Florida-based contractor L3Harris , which is replacing the system’s copper wire with fiber-optic cable. Whether the culprit was a faulty router or the crew that installed it, he said, is still unknown. Either way, the trouble spread through parts of the air traffic control system, disrupting radar feeds and communication with some airplanes. “When we see that—your safety is paramount—we shut down the airspace, and we do it safely,” Duffy said. Many observers view such statements with skepticism, though they agree that the FAA is contending with ongoing problems with aging infrastructure. “Like most engineers, I feel like we’re not getting the whole story here,” says Missy Cummings, a transportation engineer at George Mason University and a former Navy fighter pilot. She’s also surprised that anyone would attempt a challenging upgrade on a busy Friday afternoon. “This just doesn’t pass the smell test,” she says. The limited information released so far suggests a lack of transparency, says Ratul Mahajan, an associate professor of computer engineering at the University of Washington. “As a comparison point, Microsoft had an outage, too, and they had a detailed incident report published within three days,” he says, referring to the worldwide Microsoft 365 failure that began the day before the ground stop. He’s less bothered by the Friday-afternoon timing. “One way to make systems resilient is for changes to not be special and to be putting processes in place so that you can do updates all the time,” he says. A system that’s updated regularly, in other words, becomes better at being updated. The copper-to-fiber swap at the center of Duffy’s account is itself part of the FAA’s fix for its aging network. The agency says it has replaced 69 percent of the old copper wire with the fiber cables, which carry data at much higher speeds. Plenty of older gear sticks around. At some sites, the air traffic control system still uses floppy disks, which “contain vital weather and other critical information,” according to the FAA’s website. Of course, most people haven’t used floppies since the 2000s, and they can hold little data compared with USB sticks and cloud storage that can be quickly downloaded. Though the floppy disks aren’t running operating systems and aren’t critical infrastructure, they’re an example of aging technologies air traffic controllers are working with, says Ruth Stilwell, an experienced former air traffic controller and policy expert. Ripping out the old equipment carries its own risks. “It’s true that some things may be working, and you may not want to change them, because every time you change something, you introduce the risk of breaking it,” says Michele Polese, an electrical and computer engineering researcher at Northeastern University. But once technology gets so old that vendors no longer sell or maintain it, he argues, fixing problems as they arise becomes harder. “Like most engineers, I feel like we’re not getting the whole story here.” — Missy Cummings, transportation engineer, George Mason University The FAA is attempting that kind of swap across the whole country while keeping planes in the air, and federal auditors have their doubts about how it’s going. In September the Government Accountability Office warned that the FAA is juggling thousands of modernization projects without an integrated schedule to coordinate them, leaving costs uncertain and flights vulnerable to disruption. “The net effect could be that air traffic controllers are forced to continue to rely on severely outdated systems that are prone to failure leading to more system outages, ground stops, flight delays, or worse—safety incidents,” the report said. The recent ground stop came two months later. The administration thinks the answer is more money. Within hours of the ground stop, Duffy raised his funding request to Congress for air traffic control and airport upgrades from $30 billion to $50 billion . To Stilwell, the ballooning budgets and new programs look like “requirements creep” that leaves the underlying infrastructure problems unaddressed. She also warns that Duffy’s push to consolidate air route traffic control centers—the regional hubs that guide planes between airports—risks another “ Chicago sabotage ”: in 2014 a contract employee disabled one of those centers, outside Chicago, halting traffic at both of the city’s major airports. Meanwhile the FAA has yet to spend most of the $12.5 billion Congress already gave it in last year’s so-called One Big Beautiful Bill Act. “Infrastructure maintenance is not sexy and does not get headlines, but it is the thing that causes these problems. Rather than a new wish list, focus on this first,” Stilwell says. The agency also needs to invest in training and keeping the workforce, she says—yet in 2025 the so-called Department of Government Efficiency, or U.S. DOGE Service, fired some 400 FAA workers , and around 1,300 more took buyouts or early retirement. If you’re traveling for Thanksgiving, the FAA has offered some reassuring news on its website : its system is now stable, and the agency has paused installations of the type of router in question. Stilwell stresses how much remains unknown, starting with why the FAA stopped traffic entirely. By the agency’s own account, on November 13 its telecommunications system remained operational while experiencing “instability,” something like a fluttering Internet connection. “Planes were flying; there were no reported incidents of aircraft being in danger; controllers were still talking to planes. So why wouldn’t you go to reduced traffic instead of no traffic?” she says. So far, Stilwell says, the FAA hasn’t explained that. “This is so odd for this to blow up as big as it did and to last as long as it did.” Rights & Permissions Ramin Skibba is a science writer based in San Diego, Calif.
