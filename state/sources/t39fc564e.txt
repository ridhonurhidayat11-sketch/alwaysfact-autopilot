Credit: 3DMedisphere/SPL In the mid-2000s, a young woman in the KwaZulu-Natal province of South Africa became infected with HIV. Her story could have represented just another sobering statistic in the epidemic that was tearing through the region, but instead, she became a source of inspiration for scientists and of hope for millions. Enrolled in a study to track the course of newly acquired infections, the woman mounted a ferocious immune response to the virus over the next several years as doctors in the study treated her and took regular blood samples to monitor what was going on in her body. Although she could not control the virus without the help of drugs, the antibodies she raised against HIV are recognized as among the strongest that have ever been discovered 1 . The woman is known in the literature as CAP256. Scientists now think that by mimicking some of the key elements of an infection like hers, they might be able to train other people’s immune systems to make similarly potent antibodies that can block a diverse array of HIV strains. Ultimately, they say, they might achieve one of the most elusive goals in biomedical research: a vaccine that can stop HIV in its tracks. The world has a golden chance to improve HIV prevention: don’t squander it “We have a line of sight to an efficacious vaccine,” says Mark Feinberg, president and chief executive of the International AIDS Vaccine Initiative (IAVI) in New York City. “The scientific promise is greater than it’s ever been.” This year, several teams described vaccination strategies that — in monkeys — can trigger the production of what are known as broadly neutralizing antibodies (bnAbs), molecules that can completely shut down an infection from a diverse range of HIV strains before it begins 2 – 4 . “No one has achieved that before. It’s a very important milestone,” says Rogier Sanders, a virologist at Amsterdam University Medical Centre. Humans who have received components of similar vaccines can produce precursors to these bnAbs 5 – 7 , suggesting that with further refinement, an effective vaccine is possible. Success is by no means guaranteed, says Feinberg. HIV has proved a formidable foe for vaccine developers in the past. And there is another factor that adds complications. The availability of successful drugs, such as lenacapavir , that prevent HIV infection might make it difficult to test how well a vaccine works. Meanwhile, devastating cuts to the US funding that supported preclinical and clinical research for HIV have left some efforts on a precipice. “We’re in a better position than we’ve ever been, and just at this time, we’ve seen the funding streams cut off,” says Dennis Burton, an immunologist and virologist at Scripps Research, a research institute in La Jolla, California. Shots in the dark The latest glimmers of success follow decades of failure. Initially, scientists looking to develop an HIV vaccine followed a strategy that had been successful for many other viruses, including hepatitis B: administer a lab-made version of the proteins that viruses use to attach to host cells and the body should start producing antibodies that will block infection. In HIV’s case, it’s the surface protein known as envelope that latches onto a molecular receptor on human T cells so that the virus can infect them. “There was only one protein, and so it seemed straightforward,” says Sallie Permar, a vaccinologist and paediatrician at Weill Cornell Medicine in New York City. Starting in the 1980s, researchers made vaccines that contained the HIV envelope protein, to elicit antibodies against it, but the vaccines failed to prevent infection in clinical-trial participants. HIV mutates rapidly and most circulating strains were still able to mount an infection. “With HIV, there are millions of strains circulating simultaneously. Taking out one does nothing,” says Richard Wyatt, a viral immunologist at Scripps Research. Trump blew up the global fight against AIDS. Can it recover? Researchers tried other strategies, such as vaccines that recruited ‘killer’ T cells to wipe out infected cells, and those that delivered a wider diversity of proteins to overcome HIV’s penchant for mutation. In 2009, a clinical trial in Thailand reported modest signs of protection for a vaccine that triggered both T-cell and antibody responses 8 . But efforts to replicate those results failed, and that strategy was abandoned, too. One overarching problem, says Feinberg, was that nobody knew what the immunological signature — the measurable biological response after injection — of an effective HIV vaccine looked like, so the only way to assess a candidate was to conduct large-scale efficacy trials and hope for the best. “It was all a shot in the dark,” says Burton. That attitude began to change after the discovery of bnAbs. In the 1990s, teams led by Burton and microbiologist Hermann Katinger, then at the University of Natural Resources and Life Sciences in Vienna, identified the first of them in individuals who were living with HIV 9 , 10 . These bnAbs could protect monkeys from infection by HIV strains circulating in Europe and America, but it took large doses, which were unlikely to be reached and sustained with a vaccine. And the protection was less effective against some strains from Africa. In the 2000s, Burton’s team developed a way to sort through vast numbers of B cells — the immune cells that produce antibodies — for the rare ones that make bnAbs. After screening around 1,800 people living with HIV, they identified a pair of highly potent bnAbs, made by a donor in Africa, that blocked infection from dozens of HIV strains 11 . “That ignited the field,” says Burton. Scouting for talented B cells Researchers would have to learn how to train the immune system to produce these kinds of bnAb. The strategies to do so emerged from the recognition that bnAbs often develop gradually in HIV-infected individuals such as CAP256. It happens through a sustained “cat and mouse game” between the fast-changing virus and an immune system struggling to keep up, says Penny Moore, a virologist at South Africa’s National Institute for Communicable Diseases and the University of Witwatersrand, both in Johannesburg, who analysed CAP256’s blood. To mimic that struggle in an uninfected person, researchers first deliver a ‘priming’ immunogen — a portion or complete copy of HIV’s envelope. This shot awakens B cells, triggering them to divide and become active. Subsequent doses deliver distinct versions of the HIV envelope to first ‘shape’ and then ‘polish’ the antibodies produced. The goal is to guide the awakened B cells through a process called affinity maturation, in which the antibodies develop extensive mutations that improve their binding and breadth. The key is that the most capable B cells are recruited early and the antibodies that they produce are honed over time (see ‘Targeted vaccines’). Source: P. J. Klasse et al. Nature Rev. Microbiol. 23 , 734–752 (2025). Sanders says that this strategy, sometimes known as germline targeting, is a bit like looking for an elite footballer among pools of youth players. Rather than trying to train everyone up to an expert level, they want to identify talent early and nurture it to superstardom. “We’re scouting the talented B cells,” Sanders says. Booster shots Researchers have taken different approaches to scouting these cells. After Moore and colleagues discovered CAP256’s antibodies, they sequenced the viruses circulating in her blood to identify what might have triggered and shaped the antibodies’ production.
