Sign Up Humanity has long looked up and yearned to know more about the glowing dots in the night sky. Over the past century, advances in technology have allowed us to glean amazingly good insights into both distant stars and our closest cosmic neighbors. But while high-tech probes may tell us what the atmosphere is like on Jupiter, Saturn or Mars, we still can’t directly answer one deceptively simple question: What does it smell like on other planets? A human may not ever make it to another planet to sniff for themselves. But scientists do have some ideas of such worlds’ odor—albeit rough ones. That’s because astronomers use tools called spectroscopes, which measure how different gases affect the wavelength of light, as well as sampling probes and models of gravity and density, to work out which elements are present on the planets. Some of those elements smell in specific ways. But while we know those chemical signatures are there, few have given much thought to how they might translate to human senses . In that regard, Boyko Ranguelov may be unique. A physics professor at Bulgaria’s University of Mining and Geology, Ranguelov has long been interested in what he calls the planets’ “organoleptic properties,” or how the worlds would stimulate human senses. In a 2022 presentation to Bulgarian and Serbian astronomers, Ranguelov laid out his theories of what the other planets in the solar system smell like. The gas giants largely smell terrible, he says, like rotten eggs or human farts . That’s because of the dominant chemicals of hydrogen, helium, hydrogen sulfide, ammonia, phosphine, hydrogen cyanide and ammonium hydrosulfide. That terrible smell would only be amplified by the high pressures that exist lower in their atmospheres. “According to our human senses, the other space bodies in our solar system are not very friendly,” Ranguelov says. “We are strongly accommodated to Earth’s smells and would be badly surprised to smell other space bodies.” Rocky planets, such as Mars or Venus , are different. Because of the amount of iron present on Mars, it likely smells similar to rust, with a hint of gunpowder, Ranguelov says. While there may not be any direct knowledge of planetary smells, there are a tiny handful of humans who have indirectly smelled another celestial object. Apollo astronauts who landed on the moon reported that their equipment and samples had a distinct odor . “The samples smelled as safety matches immediately after they were collected,” according to Ranguelov. “Sometime later they lost the smell after the contact with the oxygen in the moon module cabin.” If we ever venture beyond our solar system, humans may be pleasantly surprised at some of the aromas we’d find. There are indications, for example, that the molecular cloud Sagittarius B2 “will smell like an alcoholic cocktail of vodka and sour cherry liquor and salad of pickled cucumbers,” Ranguelov says. “But to get this smell, we’ll need a nose the size of several light-years.” Rights & Permissions Adam Kovac is a breaking news reporter at Scientific American .
