(Image credit: ESA/DLR/FU Berlin) Researchers have spotted an ancient, "dead" Martian volcano from orbit around the Red Planet. What is it? The European Space Agency's Mars Express spacecraft, which has been orbiting and studying the planet since it arrived in 2003, recently captured incredible new imagery of the volcano Tharsis Tholus. This snapshot was captured by the spacecraft's High Resolution Stereo Camera. Latest Videos From Space View more Watch full video here: Even as old and worn as it is, the Martian volcano stands at around 4.97 miles (8 kilometers) tall which is comparable to some of the highest peaks in the Himalayan mountains. Despite its impressive height, it's still only about a medium-sized volcano for Mars, which is home much more impressively-sized volcanoes. For example, the planet's largest volcano Olympus Mons measures in at roughly 13.2 miles high (21.3 kilometers). (Image credit: ESA/DLR/FU Berlin) Why is it incredible? While Tharsis Tholus might not be the tallest volcano on Mars, it is incredibly steep. It is steeper than Mauna Kea in Hawaii on Earth. To fully showcase the volcano's structure and topography in 3D, researchers actually exaggerated the vertical scale in this image, ESA reported . In addition to being steep and fairly tall, the volcano also has an interesting history. You can see that it is speckled with what look like small holes which are actually craters created by impacts from objects like asteroids. The volcano's current, unique shape is a combined effort by these impacts, environmental factors causing erosion and warping and ancient lava flows that left behind built-up material.
