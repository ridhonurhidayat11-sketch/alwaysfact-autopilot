(Image credit: Gary Hershorn/Getty Images) NOAA marked an important milestone in numerical weather modeling on November 3 with the implementation of a next-generation rapid-update forecast system by the agency’s National Weather Service (NWS). The new Rapid Refresh Forecast System (RRFS) combines many of the biggest strengths of NOAA’s existing regional models to deliver the most detailed, hourly-updating, short-term forecasts over all of North America, and with longer forecast lead times than other models. RRFS replaces a patchwork of aging regional systems, advancing the evolution of the NOAA weather model system toward a unified global-to-local forecast system . In addition to more traditional forecast products, NOAA Research and NWS scientists integrated two advanced functions into the RRFS to improve forecasts of fire weather, smoke and dust plumes. “The transition of RRFS from research to operations represents a major step towards modernizing NOAA’s weather forecast modeling suite,” said NOAA Administrator Neil Jacobs, Ph.D. “RRFS extends the reach of some of our best capabilities and delivers more accurate, earlier forecasts to support Americans’ safety and security.” The Rapid Refresh Forecast System, pictured above, delivers the most detailed, short-term regional forecasts over all of North America, and with hourly updates and longer forecast lead times than other models. (Image credit: NOAA Global Systems Laboratory) Download Image The new standard: RRFS The RRFS combines the hourly updates of NOAA’s short-term High Resolution Rapid Refresh Model (HRRR) and its ability to issue forecasts for each 3-kilometer by 3-kilometer square of land surface with the larger, continental domain of the North American Mesoscale model (NAM). The RRFS also extends forecast lead time for the contiguous United States to 84 hours, compared with the HRRR’s 48 hours. RRFS covers all of North America including Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico, along with Canada, Mexico, Central America and the Caribbean with enough detail to predict high-impact, small-scale events such as thunderstorms, tornadoes and flash floods. RRFS also features seamless predictions for coastal and offshore areas that previously required forecasters to switch between different weather models. From its inception, the RRFS was designed to include the Rapid Refresh Ensemble Forecast System (REFS), a model that generates multiple weather simulations using RRFS data as its foundation. Because REFS also runs on a 3-kilometer surface grid, it can calculate the probabilities of local-scale events, such as the likelihood of severe hail or a flash flood, extremely valuable information for emergency managers, first responders and broadcasters. "RRFS is the biggest improvement to our regional weather models in years," said Ken Graham, director, NOAA’s National Weather Service. "The higher precision provided by this model helps our forecasters issue better forecasts, warnings, and decision support for local emergency managers and meet our core mission to save lives and protect property." New features: Fire, dust and smoke forecasting RRFS includes a mode that applies an even finer 1.5-kilometer grid that can be deployed on demand to forecast smaller-scale phenomena like fire weather conditions, complex winter weather over travel corridors and severe thunderstorms. Meteorologists can use this mode to focus on areas where high-impact weather is imminent or actively developing. Scientists with NOAA’s Global System’s Laboratory (GSL) also developed RRFS-SD (Smoke and Dust), NOAA’s first high-resolution smoke and dust model to transition to NWS operations. RRFS-SD, the successor to GSL’s experimental HRRR-Smoke model, uses data from satellites and weather models to predict when and where aerosol particles from fires and dust storms will travel across North America over the next 18 to 84 hours. RRFS-SD predicts both high-altitude smoke, which affects aviation and air temperatures, and ground-level particulates that most directly affect human health and surface visibility. This gives agencies data they need to issue air quality alerts. RRFS-SD also includes NOAA’s first Hourly Wildfire Potential forecast that evaluates the risk of rapid, intense wildfire spread. Sunsetting legacy models As RRFS goes operational, NOAA will discontinue outdated regional modeling systems including the North American Mesoscale (NAM), the Short Range Ensemble Forecast, the High Resolution Ensemble Forecast, the High Resolution Window and the NAM Model Output Statistics. This will unify and simplify the regional models within the NWS operational modeling suite. Tried and tested: A true collaborative effort The RRFS and REFS emerged from a coordinated development effort between NOAA GSL, the NWS Office of Modeling and Development, and a network of partners that includes other NWS entities, academic institutions, other federal agencies and international partners. “RRFS is a great example of how research supports one of NOAA’s most critical missions,” said Steve Thur, Ph.D, Assistant Administrator for NOAA Research. “Our scientists worked closely with their colleagues at NWS to improve forecasts for all of North America.
