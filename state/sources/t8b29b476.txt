Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Every year around the Hindu festival of Diwali, or Deepavali, residents in Nepal observe Kaag Tihar , an annual festival day dedicated to worshipping house crows as divine messengers. However, in the country’s capital, rapid urban sprawl, habitat degradation and changing perceptions toward the bird are testing human-crow relations, reports Mongabay’s Gobinda Pokharel. Satellite analysis shows Kathmandu’s urban build-up area expanded by 124% between 1989 and 2018, while another study found municipal green space plummeted by 54% between 1975 and 2025. Meanwhile, the removal of more than 2,000 mature trees along major thoroughfares like the Kathmandu Ring Road has forced house crows ( Corvus splendens ) to nest on utility poles and electrical wires, according to a 2024 study led by Tribhuvan University researcher Dinesh Bhusal, in Nepal. “When old and large trees are cut down, specialist birds lose their habitats,” said Hem Bahadur Katuwal, a bird ecologist at the Xishuangbanna Tropical Botanical Garden, Chinese Academy of Sciences, southwest China. “Eventually, only functional generalist species, such as crows, which can survive under almost any conditions, remain.” Compounding the population shift is Kathmandu’s unmanaged refuse. Open dumping of waste along riverbanks and uncovered trash at meat shops provide abundant forage, attracting dense concentrations of crows and black kites ( Milvus migrans ). “Large quantities of discarded food and exposed waste from meat shops have contributed to the unusual increase in crows and kites in Kathmandu,” said Kamal Raj Gosai, an ornithologist and associate professor at Tribhuvan University’s Tri-Chandra Multiple Campus. Gosai, noted that crows act as crucial scavengers by feeding on carcasses, yet unmanaged refuse accelerates their crowding. This aggregation of crows on dumped food and waste has fueled public health friction. A recent outbreak of highly pathogenic H5N1 bird influenza at Nepal’s Central Zoo — linked to wild crows moving across the city — resulted in the loss or culling of more than 60 animals. Kathmandu-based veterinarian Krishna Prasad Acharya noted that crows occupy an “unusual ecological niche” moving between unmanaged garbage dumps, poultry farms and human settlements, escalating cross-species disease risks in an unzoned urban landscape. However, Katuwal said the idea that crows must be eliminated from cities “is entirely misguided.” “Crows perform an important service by helping clean up urban waste and carcasses,” he said. “The problem is not the crows; it is our poor waste management and the destruction of habitats.” Sunita Dangol, acting mayor of Kathmandu Metropolitan City, told Mongabay that the municipality has incorporated bird conservation into its environmental framework, removing decorative tree lights and adjusting road expansion plans to preserve mature trees. “We are also developing clear guidelines for urban tree planting in consultation with botanists,” Dangol said.
