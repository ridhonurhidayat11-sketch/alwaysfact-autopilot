Sign Up Earth just saw the most anomalously hot day ever measured on the planet. According to preliminary data from the Europe-based Copernicus Climate Change Service (C3S), Friday, November 20, 2026, was 2.12 degrees Celsius higher than the preindustrial average—or the time before humans began heating up Earth by burning fossil fuels. The record stems from a combination of long-term, human-driven climate change and a blockbuster El Niño that is likely the strongest in the past 1,000 years. It comes as international negotiations at the COP31 climate summit have fallen apart, with no global agreement on how to rein in global warming. There are likely to be more days with an anomaly at or above 2 degrees C as this El Niño builds to its crescendo over roughly the next month. The next several months are also likely to be record hot. “I think we will likely see more days exceeding 2 degrees C over the next couple of months (possibly through March 2027),” says Zachary Labe, a scientist at Climate Central, a climate research nonprofit, “though I still expect this to be more a rarity than the norm when comparing every day.” It’s possible 2026 will be the hottest year on record, besting 2024, though it will be close and could end in a tie. Because it takes time for the heat released by El Niño in the Pacific to affect global temperatures, 2027 will likely see the biggest heat effect and will almost certainly become the hottest year on record. Both 2026 and 2027 are expected to be more than 1.5 degrees C above preindustrial temperatures, a level so far only ever surpassed in 2024. Under the landmark 2015 Paris climate agreement , which came out of the COP21 meeting, countries agreed to limit warming to “well below” 2 degrees C above preindustrial and to try to keep it under 1.5 degrees C. But the United Nations recently announced that the world will blow past the 1.5 degrees C mark , with global temperatures expected to hit 1.8 degrees C above preindustrial. Surpassing the 2 degrees C benchmark for a day—or even a year—doesn’t mean the Paris Agreement limit has been permanently breached, because that considers the average temperature over many years. By that measure, the world has warmed by nearly 1.4 degrees C above preindustrial, according to the U.N. The COP31 summit—to which the U.S. did not send an official delegation—ended without an agreement for a final deal. These documents nominally provide a blueprint for countries to follow in their efforts to lower greenhouse gas emissions and adapt to the changing climate. In a statement, outgoing secretary-general António Guterres, who has been a vocal supporter of phasing out fossil fuels, noted his “profound frustration” at the summit’s outcome. “Temperatures keep shattering records. Climate extremes keep intensifying. Emissions remain far too high. And every delay makes our task harder and more costly,” Guterres said. “The world is now poised to exceed the globally-agreed limit of a 1.5 degree temperature rise.
