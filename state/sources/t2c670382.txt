Facebook Linkedin Threads Whatsapp Reddit Email Page link Illegal wildlife trafficking continues to thrive across West and Central Africa despite recent increases in regional law enforcement operations, according to a recent report by the Environmental Investigation Agency (EIA). Nigeria remains the primary exit point for pangolin scales and elephant ivory bound for Asian markets, with authorities seizing more than 50 metric tons of the contraband between 2021 and 2026. As enforcement pressure has intensified in major hubs like Lagos, organized criminal syndicates have shifted their logistics to smaller port cities, while concealing contraband inside legal shipments such as moringa seeds, reports contributor Gloria Dickie for Mongabay. Out of 72 suspects involved in 58 documented wildlife crime incidents between January 2021 and February 2026 in Nigeria, only 19 were charged with a crime. Just 12 individuals were convicted across six incidents, yielding an arrest-to-conviction rate of approximately 16%. Legal experts noted that low-level poachers and couriers regularly absorb legal penalties, whereas high-level network kingpins and facilitators frequently evade prosecution. “On the one hand, there’s been progress. Nigeria has had its first prosecutions,” said Justin Gosling, who leads the EIA program on securing criminal justice. “Even five years ago, it had not had any on wildlife trafficking at that level.” Gosling added that law enforcement agencies must target high-level organizers rather than fringe operatives to disrupt syndicates permanently. “As long as the enforcement agencies are only picking off the individuals around the edge of the networks, then they will just bounce back. So they’ve got to get to the right people,” he said. Ruth Musgrave, CEO of the Elephant Protection Initiative Foundation, the secretariat of a pan-African alliance working to conserve elephants and end the ivory trade, underscored the need for multifaceted regional strategies to combat illegal trade. “This investigation shows that we must take a holistic approach to fighting the illegal wildlife trade,” Musgrave said. She added that West African countries have worked to secure and, where possible, destroy government stockpiles of confiscated ivory and pangolin scales to prevent the contraband from leaking back into black markets. Nigeria is preparing to enact its Endangered Species Conservation and Protection Bill 2024 , which will overhaul its wildlife protection laws for the first time in nearly 40 years. However, experts said that uniform regional enforcement is critical to prevent trafficking networks from simply relocating to adjacent jurisdictions. Read the full story by Gloria Dickie here .
