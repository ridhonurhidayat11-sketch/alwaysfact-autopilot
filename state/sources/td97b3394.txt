From left, technicians Chad Cook and Seth Harvey install the high-resolution microscopic imager – part of the seven-camera DragonCam science imaging suite – onto NASA’s Dragonfly rotorcraft at the Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL) in Laurel, Maryland, on Nov. 6, 2026. NASA/Johns Hopkins APL/Ed Whitman First to check in: DragonCam, which consists of seven cameras designed to characterize Titan ’s surface at multiple scales during flight and while on the ground. By mid-November, all seven cameras developed and built by Malin Space Science Systems had been installed in their insulated housings, tested, and integrated into the rotorcraft. Each camera will look out at Titan through a window in a foam enclosure that insulates the cameras and Dragonfly’s interior from Titan’s cryogenic environment, where ambient temperatures reach minus 290 degrees Fahrenheit. A configurable LED array, built at APL and spanning the light spectrum from the infrared to the ultraviolet, provides information about the color of surface materials at the sampling site and helps scientists make inferences about their composition. During Titan’s long night, which spans nearly eight Earth days, these observations also may allow DragonCam to identify organic molecules that fluoresce under ultraviolet light. The LED illumination also allows us to see Titan as human eyes would. “One of the most exciting things about Dragonfly is that we'll be able to see Titan from perspectives we've never had before,” said Elizabeth Turtle, Dragonfly principal investigator at APL. “With the capability to take images when Dragonfly is on the surface as well as while flying, DragonCam will give us both detailed views and sweeping panoramas of Titan's landscape, as well as insight into the processes that shaped this extraordinary world. Seeing these cameras become part of the Dragonfly lander is an exciting milestone that brings us another step closer to this unprecedented exploration.” Matthew Clark (left) and Michael Ravine of Malin Space Science Systems check DragonCam's seven science cameras and digital video recorder before shipping them to Johns Hopkins Applied Physics Laboratory (APL) for integration and testing onto NASA's Dragonfly rotorcraft.
