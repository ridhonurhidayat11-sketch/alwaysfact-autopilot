The calf was born at Chester Zoo in Cheshire this week. The video shows 19-year-old mother Asha with her newborn calf at their enclosure after Asha had been pregnant for 15 months. Keepers said both rhinos were in good health and the birth was an important step in the conservation of the threatened species. Tom Garland from the rhino team at Chester Zoo said the calf was the fourth born to Asha and father Beni, who died earlier this year following a short and sudden illness. "It's always exciting to welcome a new calf and Asha has been a brilliant mum to all her youngsters," he said. "Every birth is important for the future of this species and it's wonderful to see another healthy arrival." Image source, Chester Zoo Image caption, The greater one-horned rhinoceros species is under threat of extinction The species was driven to near-extinction in the early 1900s by habitat loss and trophy hunting and remains vulnerable to extinction with around 4,000 greater one-horned rhinos living in the wetlands, forests and grasslands of India and Nepal. The calf is part of a Europe-wide conservation breeding programme that helps to maintain a healthy and genetically diverse population of greater one-horned rhinos in human care. Conservation scientist John O'Hanlan said supporting Asha's 15-month pregnancy had been "really exciting". "She's one of relatively few greater one-horned rhinos in Europe actively breeding, so every successful birth is important in maintaining a healthy and genetically diverse population," he said. Image source, Chester Zoo Image caption, The birth is part of a Europe-wide conservation breeding programme Mark Brayshaw, Head of Mammals at Chester Zoo, said in addition to safeguarding the genetics of the species, Asha and her calf could also help millions of zoo visitors understand the value of conservation. "Greater one-horned rhinos are much better protected in the wild than they once were, but many of them are concentrated within one national park in India, which makes them vulnerable to illness and environmental changes," he said. "They still need people to love and protect them."
