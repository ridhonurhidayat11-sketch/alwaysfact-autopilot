Dark Energy Survey/DOE/FNAL/ DECam/CTIO/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA By Charlie Wood Senior Staff Writer November 16, 2026 View PDF/Print Mode astronomy astrophysics big data cosmology dark energy physics supernovas telescopes All topics J oshua Frieman remembers when the world found out that the universe was made of much more than just matter. For a few years, the scientist had been studying the possibility that the cosmos needed something else — a “missing energy” — to make sense. But it was a niche idea. When he and a colleague started to plan a workshop on the topic in 1997, at first only a few dozen people signed up. Then, in early 1998, two teams of researchers announced that exploding stars called supernovas seemed to be racing away from Earth under the influence of an unidentified, omnipresent repellent force. The observations implied the existence of exactly the sort of missing energy that Frieman and his colleagues planned to talk about. “Suddenly, everyone wanted to come to our workshop,” including reporters with TV cameras, said Frieman, who was then at the University of Chicago. It was “kind of a circus.” Within a few years, cosmologists had arrived at a whole new view of reality. The missing energy, given the name “dark energy” for its invisible nature, was pushing galaxies apart. The energy seemed embedded in space itself. It made up roughly two-thirds of creation (with matter — visible and dark — making up the remainder). Frieman embarked on what would become a two-decade mission to learn about dark energy by watching how it influences all that we can see: the Dark Energy Survey (DES). Now that project has come to a close. In May 2026, DES shared findings based on its full dataset of thousands of supernovas and hundreds of millions of galaxies spanning billions of years of cosmic history. Those findings, in combination with data gathered by a different project, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), suggest that our understanding of what the universe is made of — the venerable “standard model” of cosmology — is not complete. “There appear to be cracks in that model,” said Simone Ferraro , a cosmologist at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory. “There’s something that doesn’t fit.” Share this article Facebook Copied! What they can say is that after 20 years of stability, their field is returning to a state of confusion. “It’s certainly enough to make you either uncomfortable or excited,” Ferraro said. Bigger and Faster Forever Experimental cosmology is a piecemeal endeavor. It’s a bit like trying to reconstruct a person’s life from a dossier of data about them: report cards, utility bills, cholesterol tests, and so on. You come up with a story that weaves all the data together, go find new data, and use it to refine the story. Frieman and other cosmologists started to speculate about a missing form of energy in the mid-1990s because the story they were telling didn’t seem quite right. In previous decades, they had peered at the sky and inferred roughly how much stuff was out there. They saw a smattering of galaxies lighting up the void — visible matter. Based on how fast the galaxies were spinning, astronomers deduced that a preponderance of invisible matter must surround each bright speck, holding it together — dark matter. It’s certainly enough to make you either uncomfortable or excited. Simone Ferraro, University of California, Berkeley The rate at which these galaxies were receding from view implied that the universe had been expanding for some 8 billion to 13 billion years. It looked as if there was just enough matter (dark and visible combined) to tug gravitationally on the universe and continuously slow this expansion without ever bringing it to a full halt. But new observations started to clash with this accounting. Astronomers spotted stars, for instance, that seemed to be older than the universe. While trying to understand these and other anomalies, cosmologists noticed that it helped to add a new type of energy to their models. Then came the bombshell 1998 findings that the expansion of the universe was not actually slowing down; it was ever so slightly speeding up. By the end of the year, the unknown stuff driving that acceleration had a name: dark energy. Frieman charged off to measure it. In 1998, cosmologists were like detectives with a thin dossier. They had a blurry image of light from the early universe known as the cosmic microwave background (CMB) . And they had observed several million galaxies, along with dozens of supernovas. Because light takes time to travel, these observations gave cosmologists snapshots of what the universe looked like at different moments over the past few billion years. Hahn/CTIO/NOIRLab/NSF/AURA That was just enough data to show that the universe was experiencing an unexpected growth spurt. But to figure out exactly when it had started and how fast it was happening, Frieman and his colleagues would need to fatten their file. Their solution was DES. DES began in 2003, with a handful of physicists at Fermi National Accelerator Laboratory brainstorming what it would take to get a more comprehensive view of the cosmos. They went on to design and build the 570-megapixel Dark Energy Camera, the most powerful digital camera of its era, and install it on the 4-meter-wide Blanco telescope in the Chilean Andes. They then used it to conduct the Dark Energy Survey, which ran for 758 nights between 2013 and 2019. During this time, DES increased the number of recorded galaxies to hundreds of millions. DES was supposed to end in 2018, but the researchers extended it to make up for lousy observations due to clouds from a strong El Niño season. Is Dark Energy Getting Weaker? March 19, 2025 Comment Save Article Read Later Every couple of years the DES collaboration, which grew to include more than 400 scientists across seven countries, would analyze the data they had collected so far to see what it suggested about the history of our universe. Again and again, what they found was consistent with the theory that had coalesced after the 1998 supernova papers: The universe was about 30% matter (mostly an invisible and lethargically drifting “cold dark matter,” or CDM), and the remaining 70% of the universe was dark energy. Physicists noted that this energy acted like an unchanging property of the vacuum of space, referred to as “lambda.” The bigger space gets, the more of this vacuum energy there is, and the faster the cosmos expands.
