Chief Science Data Officer Nov 19, 2026 Article NASA’s Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, illustrated here, scans the entire sky every three hours as it orbits Earth. Its publicly available data helps scientists find the rapidly spinning cores of dead stars, known as pulsars. NASA's Goddard Space Flight Center/Chris Smith (USRA/GESTAR) An international team of scientists has developed a method that can find pulsars in publicly available NASA data about a million times faster than before. This opens the door to discovering many more of these rapidly spinning objects. A paper describing the results published Thursday in the Physical Review Letters. Using 15 years of data from NASA's Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope and a cryptography-based method developed by study co-authors Dotan Gazith and Barak Zackay from the Weizmann Institute of Science in Israel, the team found PSR J1622−7204, an isolated pulsar spinning 261 times per second, using the equivalent of 10 hours of computing time on a standard computer processor. By comparison, the previous best method failed to find the pulsar, despite using enough computing power that a standard processor would take 600 years to perform the calculation. A pulsar is a rapidly spinning neutron star that sweeps beams of radiation toward Earth, much like a lighthouse. Finding pulsars in gamma-ray data is extraordinarily difficult because Fermi detects only a tiny number of photons from a given pulsar, mixed in with background noise from other gamma-ray-emitting sources and the broader universe. To find the signal, researchers must accurately guess several unknowns at once: how fast the star spins, how quickly the spin is slowing down, and precisely where it sits in the sky. Searching every possible combination would require an impossible number of computations. "We don't see every rotation from the pulsar, and we can't just count the rotations to work out where the photon came from," said lead author Daniel Pook-Kolb, a scientist at the Max Planck Institute for Gravitational Physics (Albert Einstein Institute) in Germany. “We have a four-dimensional grid, and we must search each direction with millions of trials in it. There’s just not enough computing power on Earth to do it.” This artist's concept shows a pulsar, which emits light in regular pulses as it rotates, like a cosmic lighthouse. Pulsars are dense remnants of exploded stars, and are part of a class of objects called neutron stars. Openly available data from NASA's Fermi space telescope can help scientists spot pulsars in the gamma-ray spectrum. NASA/JPL-Caltech Traditionally, scientists have searched for these pulsars by breaking Fermi data into smaller chunks and analyzing them by crowdsourcing computing power from volunteers around the world. Even with these volunteers contributing their computers’ unused processing power to run searches, it typically took the equivalent of hundreds of computer-years to identify a single pulsar.
