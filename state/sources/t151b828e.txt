See All Key Ideas ZORTAPA, Liberia — Grace Kotee-Zansi is living a dream she had as a child: patrolling the East Nimba Nature Reserve (ENNR) as a biologist ranger to stop hunters from killing protected animals. Her job today brings relief from her childhood memories of her grandfather hunting and farming in the forest. The ENNR, a proposed extension to a UNESCO World Heritage Site , covers 13,500 hectares (33,400 acres). Zansi says she’s working to stop wildlife killing and tree cutting. She patrols the forest, checking cameras installed to capture animals and human activities. The Mount Nimba landscape lost roughly 9. 9% of its total forest cover between 2013 and 2020, according to a report using satellite imagery. Zansi is one of the few women serving as a ranger here, making her stand out in a field dominated by men. For decades, men have carried out forest protection in Liberia, with only a few women like Zansi now joining the race. They patrol for months, leaving their spouses and children at home, tracking poachers, documenting animal footprints and recording illegal human activity in the park. Official data from the Liberian Forestry Development Authority (FDA) state that a total of 143 rangers are deployed across Liberia’s protected areas; only 17 are women. This gap is due to the struggle of “recruiting qualified women who are willing and prepared to serve as rangers,” the FDA tells Mongabay. “Liberia’s ranger workforce continues to expand through various biodiversity conservation initiatives. However, comprehensive gender-disaggregated data remain limited, and available estimates indicate that women constitute a relatively small proportion of the ranger force,” the FDA says in an email. Grace Kotee-Zansi, a ranger biologist and a mother of two assigned to the East Nimba Nature Reserve forest in Nimba county. Image by Tina S. Mehnpaine. Lassana M. Dorley II, communication and knowledge management officer at the Society for the Conservation of Nature of Liberia (SCNL), agrees with the FDA. His organization ​​is Liberia’s oldest conservation NGO, founded in 1986. It works with the FDA to co-manage forests in Lofa, Nimba and Sinoe counties. While the FDA hires “rangers,” SCNL partners with local communities, training and hiring “ eco-brigades ” (who work more in wetlands, mangroves and urban areas with flooding) as well as “eco-guards” (who work in forested rural areas) . These stewards serve as frontline first responders who support the rangers in patrolling and protecting the forests. In its 2025 annual report, SCNL said it had trained 120 eco-guards and boosted forest protection law enforcement to protect wildlife. Dorley notes that cultural factors and a lack of education among rural women have slowed their progress toward achieving the goal of a 40% female ​​workforce. “We are still finding it difficult to have women who can read,” Dorley said, attributing the literacy rate among rural women to cultural norms. “People feel that women should be at the back,” he adds. Cultural barriers Globally, the ranger workforce is predominantly led by men, with only a small fraction being women. Experts say cultural traditions have pushed many women away from venturing into ranger jobs, especially in many parts of the continent where men are hunters and key players in forest protection governance. Women make up less than 11% of the global ranger workforce. A 2021 report by the Universal Ranger Support Alliance attributed this gap to cultural and gender social norms. This report followed the 2016 Rangers Perception Africa report, which estimated that women constituted only 19% of the ranger workforce in Africa. Zansi’s dedication to her job has made her one of the outstanding women in forest protection, working alongside NGOs like the Liberia Chimpanzee Rescue and Protection, earning her the USAID 2023 Environmentalist of the Year and the World Female Ranger awards. Trees in the ENNR forest. Image by Tina S. Mehnpaine. Breaking from a cultural bottleneck Zansi grew up in Beo Bongarlay, a town bordering Côte d’Ivoire 90 kilometers (56 miles) from the ENNR, where women working as rangers were nonexistent. In fact, women were housewives, farmers, breastfeeding mothers — or they stayed at home. Zansi says seeing her in uniform inspires women and girls, which she considers her biggest accomplishment. Her work is entirely hands-on fieldwork; she rides a motorbike to reach areas for outreach and community awareness. When other women see her driving herself instead of a male driving her, “they are surprised,” she says. “Some will even call their mother to come, and some will even make friends with me.” The ENNR, which spans Liberia, Guinea and Côte d’Ivoire, is one of the country’s largest protected landscapes, as the country races to conserve 30% of its forest. This ecosystem is home to rare and endangered species, including the Nimba viviparous toad ( Nimbaphrynoides occidentalis ) and western chimpanzees ( Pan troglodytes verus ). The rangers protecting these species are backed by 104 eco-guards. Louise Gbayee, one of the eco-guards in Zortapa within the ENNR, was inspired by Zansi. As a farmer, Gbayee joined the recruitment to help protect the forest. She’s the only female among the eco-guards, earning $110 in compensation, food and other items for patrol. On a bright Tuesday, Gbayee and her colleague are on their way to a 10-day patrol in the forest, leaving her children at home with her partner. Food, water, cameras and bags are packed for the journey. “I feel good to help protect this forest,” the mother of five says. For all 10 days, Gbayee would be disconnected from her children and boyfriend. No internet, no phone calls. Louise Gbayee, one of the female eco-guards in Zortapa, sat for an interview before departing with her colleagues for a 10-day patrol in Zortapa. Image by Tina S. Mehnpaine. The ENNR is headed by Catherine Kuku Dolo, the park warden. As the only woman park leader in Liberia currently, and mother of a 6-month-old son, Dolo says women have been a great support and inspiration to her work. “Women are nurturers of communities.” Both Zansi and Dolo are breastfeeding mothers. Their babies are left with babysitters whenever they leave for patrol or fieldwork. “My children know the work I do, so they have gotten used to me leaving them for days or hours,” says Zansi, who has two teenage daughters. Beyond field patrols, Zansi supervises bio-monitoring and analyzes data collected by eco-guards and rangers on the plant and animal species that live in the landscape and the threats they face. She ensures the equipment rangers and eco-guards carry on patrol is up-to-date. Liberia’s vast forest — 43% of the Upper Guinea rainforest — is under threat from ​​deforestation fueled by wildfires, forest degradation and agriculture. The situation is worsened by the invasion of cacao farmers from Côte d’Ivoire, who are invading protected forests and leaving communities vulnerable to the ​​effects of climate change . Frontline protectors like Zansi, ​​Gbayee and Dolo are vital to forest habitats and ecosystems, especially as the country wants to sell carbon credits . The partnership between the FDA and local communities is established through an agreement allowing the communities to recruit members like ​Gbayee.
