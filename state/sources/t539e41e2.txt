See All Key Ideas VILAMOURA, Portugal — Shark finning entails slashing fins off sharks and dumping the bodies into the sea, often while they’re still alive. In the Atlantic Ocean, the practice has been banned on paper since 2004 , yet some Japanese and Chinese vessels still do it . The fins end up in shark fin soup, a delicacy in parts of Asia, and the bodies sink to the bottom of the sea. The most effective rule to enforce finning bans, called “fins naturally attached,” prohibits vessels from landing fins unattached from bodies. At the International Commission for the Conservation of Atlantic Tunas (ICCAT), a multilateral body that governs much of the fishing in the Atlantic and adjacent seas, it’s been proposed 18 years in a row, including in 2026. It’s never passed due largely to resistance from Japan. However, at ICCAT’s recent annual meeting, held Nov. 16-23 in Vilamoura, Portugal, delegates reached a compromise. ICCAT parties, which include 56 countries and the EU, adopted a measure that could establish a fins-naturally-attached rule by 2029 or 2030, under certain conditions, but for the time being allows alternatives for sharks landed frozen, which many are. The deal drew mixed reactions from parties and NGO observers who had backed a straightforward, no-alternatives policy as the best way to end finning, a practice many people regard as cruel and wasteful. “Until we see a no-exceptions rule, we’re not going to be opening the champagne bottle,” Nicolas Blanc, executive co-director of the Portugal-based NGO Sciaena, told Mongabay. The shark finning agreement was, however, one of the few deals reached on the meeting’s most contested issues. Despite intense negotiations, the parties couldn’t agree on a new catch limit for yellowfin tuna ( Thunnus albacares ) or how to divide the catch among the parties. Nor did they reach an agreement on management objectives for blue sharks ( Prionace glauca ), failing to take a key step toward a long-term, science-based approach for the near threatened species . Shark fins drying on a Hong Kong factory rooftop in 2013. Image by Kin Cheung/AP Photo. Shark week ICCAT is one of the world’s five tuna RFMOs, or regional fisheries management organizations. It also adopts rules for sharks, which vessels both target and catch incidentally. Sharks are predators key to marine ecosystems and have been severely impacted by overfishing globally. The new measure, introduced by Japan, requires sharks landed fresh — those that are chilled rather than frozen on board — to have their fins naturally attached. For sharks landed frozen, vessels can use one of two alternatives: The first is to tie fins directly to the corresponding body with rope or wire; the second is to keep the fins and body separate, marked with “identical and uniquely numbered tags,” and stored in the same hold. These alternatives are the same as those adopted by the Indian Ocean Tuna Commission , another tuna RFMO, in 2025. The alternative options have conditional sunset clauses. They would expire on Jan. 1, 2029, if only one party or fewer elects to use them in the preceding two years — and a straightforward fins-naturally-attached policy would take their place. They’d expire on Jan. 1, 2030, if only two parties or fewer used them in the 2027-2029 period. If these conditions aren’t met — and NGO observers suspect they won’t be, given how many ICCAT parties could opt to use one of these alternatives in their fleets — then discussion of a fins-naturally-attached rule will resume at the 2029 annual meeting. The European Commission, the EU’s executive branch and a longtime backer of fins naturally attached, trumpeted the deal, calling it a “landmark” and a “breakthrough agreement.” Its oceans and fisheries commissioner called it “major step forward.” Other parties and NGO observers expressed mixed feelings about the agreement, with some calling it a necessary compromise because it was all Japan would agree to. ICCAT generally operates on consensus, meaning one party can block a measure. “There’s the glass half full sort perspective on it, and there’s the glass half empty perspective,” Mark Waddell, Canada’s head of delegation at the meeting, told Mongabay, acknowledging that it was a struggle to decide whether to support the deal. “The definition of insanity is repeating the same thing over and over again, expecting a different outcome,” he said. “And we’re 17 years on here trying to advocate for fins naturally attached.” “We are tenacious and unified on this,” he added, speaking of the attitude of Canada and other parties that support fins naturally attached. “Having said that, we are optimistically accepting the proposal by Japan with a view to seeing a trajectory that would take us to, be it 2029 or 2030, a full fins-naturally-attached [rule].” Several conservationists who focus on sharks expressed dissatisfaction with the agreement, saying it wouldn’t do enough to protect sharks. “We are concerned that the compromise on finning ban enforcement is exceedingly complicated and risk-prone with a lengthy timeframe for potential benefits and alternatives that have no scientific basis,” Sonja Fordham, president of the U.S.-based NGO Shark Advocates International, said in a joint statement with other NGOs. “Opening the door for entire fleets to start employing problematic techniques involving wires, ropes, and tags to sort detached fins needlessly risks an increase in marine debris and threatens long-fought progress toward the only fool-proof method for finning ban enforcement: ending at-sea removal of shark fins.” Iris Ziegler, the head of fisheries policy at the Germany-based foundation Deutsche Stiftung Meeresschutz, told Mongabay she was concerned that neither the term “sharks” nor “frozen” was well defined in the measure and fleets could exploit these ambiguities. She said her general takeaway was that ICCAT was “preserving the status quo and standing still instead of making progress,” with “industry interests prevailing and shark conservation sacrificed on the altar of profit maximization.” There are more than a dozen RFMOs in the world, with some overlapping geographically but managing different fish stocks. The EU put forward a proposal that would have set management objectives for blue sharks, which are heavily targeted for their meat and fins. The proposal also set the commission on track to adopt a harvest strategy for the species by 2029 at the latest. However, the EU and the U.S. couldn’t agree on how to set the management objectives, according to NGO observers, and the proposal failed.
