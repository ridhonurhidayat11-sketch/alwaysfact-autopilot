When gibbons sing less, it also complicates the work of researchers, who typically rely on monitoring songs to find and count the animals. See All Key Ideas Around dawn, a gibbon pair starts to sing. Their voices interweave, and the duet builds to a crescendo that fills the canopy. Across much of the Bornean forest, this is the sound of the morning. But since smoke from Indonesia’s worst fire crisis in over a decade has engulfed the island, forests have become noticeably quieter. On days when the haze is thick, the Borneo Nature Foundation (BNF) field team in Sebangau, Central Kalimantan, reports that gibbons sing less. Their songs sound shorter. Morning duets start later than usual, if they happen at all. Susan Cheyne, BNF’s co-director and vice-chair of the IUCN Primate Specialist Group Section on Small Apes, documented a similar pattern following forest fires in the same area in 2006. Researchers have yet to formally analyze their observations of the effects of this year’s fires, and will not be able to separate the different factors until the fires have subsided and fieldwork can resume. Cheyne believes several causes may be at play, with smoke — which irritates gibbons’ airways and reduces the light that cues morning duets — as a major driver. Why gibbon song matters Singing plays a central role in the social and reproductive lives of gibbons. Fiercely territorial, socially monogamous primates, they sing not only to attract mates, but also to defend their territory. Paired adults perform elaborate duets to demonstrate the strength of their bond and ward off neighboring groups. Their songs are remarkably sophisticated, following rhythmic patterns and note sequences in ways that primatologists have compared to human music and language. For gibbons, singing demands considerable energy and coordination — it is “an honest signal biologically,” according to Cheyne. A complex, well synchronized duet is difficult to fake, serving as a reliable sign of a healthy, strongly bonded pair. Gibbon vocal behavior is also sensitive to environmental conditions. Research has found that they appear to sing less when exposed to disturbances like rain and noise pollution. How smoke disrupts morning duets Since mid-September, air quality in Central Kalimantan, has frequently reached hazardous levels. In the capital, Palangkaraya, pollution levels in October peaked at 11,981, nearly four times the level the U.S. Air Quality Index classifies as hazardous. As of Nov. 2, the Indonesian Ministry of Health reported more than 400,000 cases of acute respiratory infections across Central Kalimantan and six other affected provinces. Borneo is home to four species of gibbons, all of which are endemic and endangered. Like humans, they have been exposed to high concentrations of smoke particles. Cheyne expects the smoke to affect gibbons in similar ways, irritating their airways and making it harder to breathe. This is likely one of the driving factors behind the drop in singing. “I’m sure an opera singer would never be able to hit the notes and range and frequency that they should be able to if they’re constantly breathing in smoke,” she says. Smoke may also affect singing by reducing visibility and dimming the light inside the forest. An analysis of more than 300 days of gibbon singing data from northern Lao PDR found gibbons tend to sing later and less often on foggy mornings. Gibbons typically perform their duets around sunrise, as light starts to filter through the canopy. “We think that light is the main cue,” Cheyne says. When haze obscures sunrise, gibbons may delay their singing or skip it altogether. According to Cheyne, there is also a visual, acrobatic component to their territorial displays, which may help explain later starts on hazy days. “They’re not just sitting; they’re swinging and singing at the same time.” North Bornean grey gibbon ( Hylobates funereus ). Alasdair Owens, a post-graduate researcher at the University of Exeter in England who uses passive acoustic data to study Bornean white-bearded gibbons ( Hylobates albibarbis ), says a factor that may become more evident in coming months is the lasting effect of the fires on the availability of fruit, which makes up the bulk of gibbon diets. Food shortages, he says, could impact their singing. Fires have burned across parts of the ranges of Borneo’s gibbons, including an estimated 13,000 hectares (32,100 acres) inside Sebangau National Park. Previous El Niño fires have led to what Owens describes as “almost famine conditions” in the forest. A 2015 study on orangutans , gibbons’ larger relatives, found that females adjusted their behavior for months following forest fires and fruit shortages. They rested more and traveled less to reduce energy expenditure. Gibbons have also been found to sing less when food availability declines. The longer-term effects of reduced singing on gibbons’ health, social relationships and reproductive success remain unclear. Cheyne predicts that if families sing less, neighbors might encroach on their territory, leading to conflict between groups, while young gibbons may struggle to find mates. Disruptions to gibbons’ daily routines caused by haze and delayed morning duets could also have far-reaching consequences. If gibbons reduce their active hours, they may have less time to feed and socialize, Cheyne says. How to count silent gibbons Less singing also complicates the work of gibbon researchers, who typically rely on acoustic data to find and count these animals. Once researchers return to the field, how will they know whether gibbons are singing less, or whether there are simply fewer gibbons? Population surveys typically estimate density by triangulating sound data from different listening posts to pinpoint the precise location of singing groups. For that purpose, Owens says, how many times a group calls matters less than whether it calls at all. To account for groups that may not be singing every day, researchers will need to survey for longer. Owens is developing an automated method to do this, using passive acoustic monitoring and deep learning techniques. Sound recorders, synchronized with GPS, are placed at strategic locations in the forest. Deep learning technology is applied to identify gibbon calls. By comparing the tiny differences in the time it takes a call to reach each device, researchers can calculate where the gibbon is singing from, with “quite high accuracy,”20 meters (66 feet) in the best cases. He hopes to test this method in 2027, in areas of unprotected and secondary forest in Central Kalimantan. In Sebangau, meanwhile, BNF plans to return to the same gibbon families it has monitored for 20 years once it is safe to resume fieldwork.
