A new musical on Broadway depicts the experience as absolutely electric—with a swelling chorus and pumped-up rock sound blaring while stars shine all around the audience. Galileo is a new Broadway musical opening on December 6. It tells the story of pioneering astronomer Galileo Galilei and the way that his public support for the heliocentric view of the universe sparked conflict with the Catholic Church . The show’s ballads depict how the Church hobbled Galileo’s efforts to advance astronomy, but it also makes it clear that he was both a man of faith and a rebellious soul. (The sometimes Christian rock–like melodies and lyrics also support this aspect of the character.) The show doesn’t shy away from the complications of Galileo’s conflict with the church, propelled by his close friendship with Bishop Maffeo Barberini, who later, as pope, played a key role in forcing Galileo to recant and sentencing him to house arrest for teaching heliocentrism. Galileo ’s music, lyrics and arrangements were created by composers Michael Weiner and Zoe Sarnak. Once it opens, Sarnak will be focusing her creative attention as composer of two more STEM (science, technology, engineering and mathematics)–themed musicals: Particle Fever , an adaptation of a 2013 documentary about the discovery of the Higgs boson with the same title, and a stage adaptation of the film A Beautiful Mind. “I’m in my science era, I like to joke,” she says. “And I’m thrilled about it.” On supporting science journalism If you're enjoying this article, consider supporting our award-winning journalism by subscribing . Scientific American spoke to Sarnak about how sound tells Galileo’s story in the new musical, how she puts science to a soundtrack and why this story is ripe for a retelling today. [ An edited transcript of the conversation follows .] You studied science before you decided to become a composer. How did your research experience fuel your music? I would say, if you asked kids from my high school what they thought I was going to be when I grew up, they would say a mathematician or a scientist. My dad is a mathematician, my mom studied philosophy... She does ethics of technology at Cornell Tech. I took music as something I did for fun..., because I’ve been composing music since I was very young. But I have a family of professors and doctors, so that’s sort of the family trade, you know? So I was doing molecular bio and music in parallel in my undergrad. I worked in a virology lab, and it was a huge part of my life. I think there’s a relationship between [molecular biology and music] in thinking in a map-based way and integrating layers of information, as opposed to just one linear thought. When you think about music and how it works, as a composer, you're thinking melody, harmony, rhythmic structures, counterpoints, how they all come together. And I think that the puzzle that is, for example, molecular bio is kind of similar in that way. Composers Zoe Sarnak (right) and Michael Weiner (left) Michaelah Reynolds What has been cool about this moment as I come into writing Galileo and Particle Fever is: I can actually explore the emotion behind what these scientists go through. The soul of a scientist sings to me because of the dreamer quality, because of the creative, thinking-outside-the-box quality. If Sunday in the Park with George makes sense as a musical, then certainly Galileo does. What drew you to Galileo’s story? He was this kind of rebellious figure speaking truth to power. And when [Weiner and I] started to talk about it, it felt useful to imagine that he’s a rock star because of that. And suddenly, you can feel how explosive he is, how brave he is, how unbridled and brash he is, which, by the way, he was . Like the actual Galileo was certainly not a meek man. And I remember thinking about the way [punk musician] Patti Smith talks about using music to speak truth to power. And it’s like, if there's a lens for this person’s story to be told, it’s through rock music. It makes sense once you start working on something like this and you see how much people have engaged with his story through the years in various art forms because the stakes are so high and it’s evergreen in its relevance, for better or worse, because we’re constantly coming up against moments where a new way of thinking hits up against the power structure. That’s part of the ebbs and flows of how civilization works. And so that got me really excited about a way in which we could tell this story that can only be told through a musical, theatrically. I heard that you wanted to have each character in Galileo’s story have a distinct musical sound. Can you describe what they are and why? Starting with Galileo, we were really interested in a version of his sound that feels sort of [like] rebellious, unbridled rock. He’s pushing against a system that doesn’t want to let a new idea come into the light, and so he has to explode against it. That felt very clear. He still has a lot of depth emotionally, and we do have moments where he’s quite quiet, but those tend to be personal, with Barberini or with his daughter Virginia. But to capture the sound of what it would mean to see the [celestial bodies] that no one had seen..., that would be like a “hits you in your core” kind of experience. So we knew we wanted that for him. With his daughter Virginia, then, we were thinking about how his real daughter had wisdom and soulfulness to her. She was born into a very difficult circumstance, and yet their correspondence shows that she was very brilliant as well, [with] an ability not to be toppled by a system that was really quite stacked against her. But she has a younger sound purposefully. We want her to start in the space of hopeful the way that teenagers are and then evolve into the version of her that is in Act II. I think with the pope, we use a lot of strings but strings in this chamber way that sounds influenced by music in mass—a chamber classical element to the church sound. Barberini has a beauty to what he sings, but he pulls it in a modern direction. Until you get to what happens in Act II, he’s a progressive thinker within the realm of the church, which helped us shape his sound. Can you preview some musical choices you made for Particle Fever? Galileo sits at the intersection of a scientist and his dreams and the political circumstance and how those explode against one another, whereas Particle Fever is zoomed in. It’s set at CERN, and we’re asking people to dive into the importance of discovering the Higgs boson. It’s a whole world of physics that we have to immerse people in. Because particle physics speaks to the tiny and also, therefore, the origins of the universe—the infinite—it has a scope of pointillism up to massive orchestral gestures. And that’s really exciting, musically, for me because it lets these two shows have very different sound worlds. I think that the shared thing is definitely the humanity that lives inside of our desire to explore and progress. For Particle Fever, there’s this question of whether or not those kinds of projects will be funded moving forward and whether we, as a civilization, commit to the importance of discovery.
