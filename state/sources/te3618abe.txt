NASA Earth Observatory Nov 24, 2026 Article View more Images of the Day: Nov 23, 2026 Instruments: Landsat 9 — OLI Topics: Topography Volcanoes October 26 November 11 NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison October 26 November 11 NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison October 26 November 11 Curtain Toggle 2-Up A volcanic eruption in the shallows near Iwo Jima has extended the Japanese island’s famed black sand beaches, at least temporarily. Signs of unrest began in late October 2026. On October 27, seismic sensors recorded a sharp uptick in activity, and Japan’s Meteorological Agency reported that the first photographs of the eruption were captured. Soon after, sensors on several satellites began to capture an array of observations. The OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 9 captured the left image on October 26, when the coast was still quiet. On November 11, Landsat 9 got its first clear view of the land the submarine volcano has built (right). A shortwave-infrared signature, a sign of the eruption’s heat, is shown in orange. A faint gray volcanic plume drifts southeast of the vent, and a streak of floating pumice stretches across the water in the same direction. A false-color backscatter radar image from the NISAR (NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar) satellite offered an even earlier look at the eruption. On October 28, despite heavy cloud cover, the radar detected a large disturbance in the water in the same area that erupted in December 2023. When the clouds cleared in early November, the MODIS (Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer) and VIIRS (Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite) sensors on NASA and NOAA satellites also captured signs of the ongoing eruption’s volcanic plumes and infrared signature . Japan’s Coast Guard released a report on November 16 confirming the formation of new land connected to the pre-existing beach. The eruption appears to have added roughly 50 hectares (125 acres) of new beach, about the area of 90 American football fields. That would enlarge the island by about 1.7 percent. The new land adjoins the southeast beach where American troops landed on February 19, 1945, beginning one of the bloodiest battles in the history of the U.S. Marines. The location of the eruption is no surprise to scientists.
