Credit: Enrico Camporeale Language is the foundation of human communication and, together with mathematics, is the medium through which science is formulated, performed, debated, and shared. A technology that transforms our relationship with language inevitably transforms how we practice and communicate science. Five years ago, we wrote in Eos about practical applications of machine learning for Earth and space scientists. Since then, the emergence of large language models (LLMs) has, indeed, been transformative , dramatically expanding the landscape of artificial intelligence (AI) capabilities and tools available to scientists. Much of scientists’ work consists of translating ideas into words—in the form of manuscripts, proposals, and reviews—as well as code and figures. Although human scientific insight and communication remain indispensable, this work is fundamentally linguistic and often largely procedural, the very domains in which LLMs can excel. Our aim here is practical: to describe concrete ways large language models (LLMs) can support everyday research in which language, code, math, and reasoning intersect. Yet this new technology has become polarizing. Many scientists use it daily, others remain skeptical, and some feel left behind . LLMs can capably automate numerous tasks and draw on vast stores of accumulated online information to tackle myriad questions. However, they also make mistakes, misunderstand context, and occasionally breach their guardrails . Scientists can, knowingly or not, use AI systems in ways that are unethical or illegal , for example, if they use LLMs to review or analyze unpublished, confidential materials in a way that exposes the writers’ intellectual property without their knowledge or consent. Amid the real and reasonable concerns about LLMs, we believe the technology nevertheless offers powerful tools that scientists can use productively to improve and dramatically accelerate their work. Our aim here is practical: to describe concrete ways LLMs can support everyday research in which language, code, math, and reasoning intersect. The Earth and space sciences are an ideal testing ground because many applications inherently combine physical theory, numerical modeling, and complex data processing. The following seven use cases are neither exhaustive nor unbiased; they reflect our direct experience and are intended to inspire readers to explore these new tools effectively and responsibly in their own work and to come up with further uses and extensions to the ideas listed below. 1. Coding and Programming Programming is a workhorse of modern science. Researchers routinely write scripts to download and process files and data, run models, produce figures, and reverse engineer legacy code. These tasks rarely constitute core scientific contributions, but they consume substantial amounts of research time. LLMs, prompted with plain-text requests, can dramatically accelerate this workflow by generating, debugging, explaining, and translating code across virtually every major programming language . As code generation becomes increasingly automated, the ability to write code from scratch may become less important than the ability to devise diagnostic tests and counterexamples that expose errors and establish whether the code behaves as intended. 2. Scientific Writing and Proofreading Scientists also spend considerable effort writing in various formats and for different audiences. Some formats, such as scientific journal papers, grant proposals, and reports from manuscript peer reviewers, tend to follow well-established structural conventions. For example, research papers typically comprise an abstract and introduction followed by sections on methods, results, and so on. This consistency in formatting makes manuscript preparation a useful and growing application for LLMs. LLMs can further democratize scientific publishing by helping researchers overcome barriers to writing in non-native languages. Indeed, LLMs can assist at many stages of the drafting process. They can turn rough notes or voice dictation into coherent prose , clarify methodological descriptions, shorten and tighten abstracts, and draft figure captions, allowing researchers to spend more time considering and communicating their core scientific claims and interpretations. LLMs can also further democratize scientific publishing by helping researchers overcome barriers to writing in non-native languages , a benefit we see among colleagues in our own labs virtually every day. Proposal writing, a high-stakes and time-consuming activity, is another task with which LLMs can be beneficial . Proposals demand a persuasive narrative combining scientific ambition, insight, expertise, and feasibility. LLMs, drawing on published literature, can act as expert sounding boards, helping researchers transform preliminary ideas into specific aims, identify weaknesses and missing components such as omitted references, and develop risk mitigation strategies (i.e., backup plans if the initially proposed research workflow does not pan out). They can also suggest ways to rewrite technical text suited for multidisciplinary review panels and maintain consistency across objectives, tasks, deliverables, and expected outcomes. Along with its upsides, however, fluent AI writing can easily conceal and even introduce incorrect or flawed reasoning, and it can invent sources and references. So every LLM-assisted statement and reference must be rigorously verified for accuracy and consistency. Scientists should also follow current publisher and institutional policies regarding disclosure of AI usage and prohibitions about uploading unpublished and confidential materials into commercial AI systems. 3. Accelerating Calculations Many scientific calculations are straightforward in principle but may require tedious multistep derivations and simplifications of equations, a process that can easily become error prone. LLMs can help derive equations step by step; suggest useful relationships, mathematical identities, or approximations; check intermediate algebra for errors; and translate physical descriptions into mathematical form .
