Sign Up Rachel Feltman: For Scientific American ’s Science Quickly , I’m Rachel Feltman. If I asked you to name the most famous scientists of the 20th century—or even of all time—I bet Stephen Hawking would end up somewhere on your list. But how much do you really know about this iconic physicist? In his new book Hawking: His Life and Work, award-winning science writer and biographer Graham Farmelo draws on years of research to explore Hawking’s complicated personal story. Scientific American ’s books and culture lead Bri Kane recently sat down with Graham to chat. Here’s their conversation. I mean, the level of detail of knowing what he had for breakfast some days, and what his mother went home and, and wrote in her journal about him that day. It’s pretty extraordinary. What was he like as a child? Because the first few hundred pages of this book, he’s a child. Like, he’s under 18. He’s in school. He’s playing with his friends. And I’d never really heard about that part of his life before. So I wanted to ask you about your research and your sources into his childhood. Graham Farmelo: Two things. First of all, the very early life of Stephen Hawking, that is illuminated in my book thanks to the wonderful work of his father in keeping a journal for 60 years, right, which started in the 1920s. One of the things he longed for was a son; he wrote diligently, sometimes almost every day, about Stephen Hawking. That and the writings of his mother give an extraordinarily rich feel for the growth of Stephen Hawking and, I would say, help illuminate why Hawking became the person he is. Well, his school days, I can’t tell you the good fortune I had there because I managed to meet with, I think it was about half a dozen of his school chums, and I actually became very good friends with his best friend at school, John McClenahan. Lovely man. I think he was very lucky to meet Stephen. I was very lucky to meet John. John died last Christmas Eve, and I was the last person to have lunch with him, right? And John read, reread, checked and facilitated a lot of the detail, right, of that amazing duo that was Hawking and McClenahan because they were seen as the kind of Lennon-McCartney of the Meccano model, and Stephen was the person that came out with the big dramatic ideas like. But John was the person who made it happen, and you needed them both, you know. So again, all I read before was that John and Stephen worked together. But with him, he was sending me dozens of e-mails of picturing the devices they made. So anyway, it was a real experience to get to know. And I’m sad to say some of those people have died since that research has been done. So future Hawking biographers are not going to have, I’m sad to say, access to those people. Kane: I did not realize he wasn’t a very diligent student. Like, young and in elementary school and, and what, you know, we Americans would refer to as, like, high school and early college years, he was not particularly interested in academics. It was boring. He was, you know, a bit of a gifted child syndrome. It was a little too easy for him, and he wasn’t really engaged, which is interesting because he was so popular. Like, he had friends. He was on the rowing team. He was busy tinkering with his friends and building helicopters and radios. It’s interesting to think about how that led to the popularized figure of Stephen Hawking that we have in our minds. Farmelo: Well, it could very easily have gone wrong. Remember, if you just move the focus along to when he was at college—he was at [the University of] Oxford—that, on the quiet, he’d registered for the Oxford Union, which trains prime ministers. And at the age of 17, he was debating in that society opposite a future prime minister at one point, right? But what he didn’t want to do was spend all the time being a swot. He knew he was good; he came very close not to getting the top grade because he was spending so much of his time partying and coxing. But one thing that I cut from the book, and I perhaps shouldn’t have done, but Derek Powney, who sadly died a couple of years ago, I showed him a telegram that he sent Stephen Hawking, and it said something like this. This is pretty accurate. He said, “Congratulations. A first-class honors degree on one term’s work.” [Laughs] Now, I think that’s a little bit of an exaggeration, but do you see what he’s saying? That basically he only got his finger out, as they say in English English, right, in the last term because he was having such fun. And that could have been disastrous. Kane: Yeah, so he was quite the mischievous child, and he was very popular and, as you said, was always going to parties, hosting parties. He had a very rich social life. And he kept a secret during this time. He was considered maybe frail. He was known to his friends as maybe a bit clumsy. But it was also during this time that he was getting his first diagnosis of motor neurone disease. Farmelo: That was just after he graduated from Oxford. He got into [the University of] Cambridge, and after one term, he got that diagnosis. Basically the upshot was that he would probably be dead within the next two or three years, at most five. Absolutely devastating for him, right? Although he didn’t show it particularly clearly, but also his parents and his sisters ... Kane: Yes, I mean, you had access to his father’s diaries at this time, and his father was very concerned, and this was very devastating news that Stephen might not have a future or what that future would look like. There’s a sentiment in the book at one point where Frank, Stephen’s father, is looking at the medical journals that he has access to at the time and looking into ... Farmelo: And hiding them from his wife ... Kane: Yes, and he hides them from his wife, and him and his wife do not tell Stephen right away. Stephen overhears a conversation. Farmelo: Nobody told him. That’s exactly right. And even then, Stephen was in—it’s a pattern throughout his life—he was in denial. His mother would say, “He looks unperturbed.” And his father, you should read his diaries, right? One of them—I don’t think this is in the book ’cause my publishers didn’t like this, although I think they’ve changed their mind now—it begins, “Damn, damn, damn, damn.” And his father was saying, that he would walk into a room and burst into tears. It was a really terrible thing for his father, and his father worked his socks off to try and find if there was any hope, and it looked like there were none. Because that was a very primitive field in medicine. And basically there was nothing to be done. Kane: Yeah, I mean, Stephen Hawking comes from two very loving parents who were very interested and very invested in his future. And then he gets this devastating diagnosis fairly early in his life, and it really throws all of their plans off track. And then as he grows—his disease is degenerative; it’s not exactly getting better with time, as they expected—and then he meets Jane [Wilde; later Jane Hawking], and him and Jane fall deeply in love.
