Displaced residents say they were driven out by fellow Kaingang from another village, exposing an internal feud over illegal leasing of Indigenous lands to local farmers. See All Key Ideas PITANGA, Brazil — Since February 2025, around 300 Indigenous Kaingang people have been living in rural Pitanga, a municipality in central Paraná, Brazil. Camping in a wooded area on private property, they survive in makeshift shelters covered with tarpaulins, without electricity or running water. The camp is not a village. Displaced residents describe it as a temporary shelter that seems to be turning into a permanent home with each passing day. Conditions at the camp are precarious. Families shelter under plastic tarpaulins battered by constant storms associated with El Niño, which further worsen their living conditions. Hunger is also part of daily life. The families’ only steady income is Bolsa Família, a federal welfare benefit, which runs out long before the end of the month. Job opportunities are scarce. Farms across the region have spent years replacing manual laborers with machinery for planting and harvesting. Finding work in downtown Pitanga is hardly an easy alternative: The daily round trip covers more than 40 kilometers (25 miles), most families have no car, and there is no public transportation serving the rural area where the camp is located. The roughly 300 people living at the Pitanga camp were forcibly driven from their village in January 2025. Unlike in other parts of Brazil, they were not expelled by agribusiness. According to the residents, they were driven out by members of their own people amid a conflict that has dragged on for nearly three years and stems from a practice prohibited by law but established in the region for at least two decades: the illegal leasing of Indigenous lands. On Jan. 4, 2025, an armed, violent raid destroyed any prospect of peaceful coexistence between the two villages of the Ivaí Indigenous Territory, Paraná’s second-largest Indigenous reserve, located between the municipalities of Pitanga and Manoel Ribas in southern Brazil and home to the Kaingang. At dawn, more than 100 Kaingang from Ivaí village, armed with shotguns, machetes and wooden clubs, marched on the village of Serrinha . They burned every house and car and injured dozens of Indigenous residents. Since then, the displaced Kaingang have been waiting for the Federal Court, the Federal Public Prosecutor’s Office (MPF) and the National Foundation for Indigenous Peoples (Funai) to grant them the right to return to their lands. A river running through the camp provides water for drinking, bathing and other needs. Image by José Pires. A conflict shaped by agribusiness Although agribusiness was not directly responsible for expelling the Indigenous residents, it plays an important role in the conflict between the two villages. Local farmers have been leasing areas inside the Ivaí Indigenous Territory to grow monocrops since the mid-2000s, according to a Funai document . Leasing Indigenous land violates Brazil’s Federal Constitution, which grants Indigenous Peoples exclusive use of their territories and bars third parties from occupying, possessing or exploiting them. The country’s Land Statute also prohibits leasing public land, including Indigenous territories. The Ivaí Indigenous Territory spans 7,306 hectares (18,053 acres). Funai records show that around 300 hectares (741 acres) are leased each year to grow soybeans, wheat, corn and other monocultures. The practice brings thousands of Brazilian reais to the Laranjal Indigenous Community Association (Acil), an organization managed by leaders from Ivaí village who oversee the lease revenues. Beyond being illegal, the practice concentrates money in the hands of a small leadership circle while most families in the reserve live in poverty. Kaingang residents say leaders protect the leasing arrangements through violence, driving out or even killing anyone who questions the system. Aware of the risks, in 2024, according to the residents, a group of Indigenous residents decided to challenge the leaders. The pushback sparked considerable tension and an election for village chief was hastily organized in an attempt to curb the power of the group that had controlled the territory for years. But the challengers lost and the result further inflamed the conflict. Threats and hostility from other community members intensified, forcing dozens of families to leave. This was how Serrinha, a new village in the southern part of the Indigenous territory, came into being. Serrinha ended up with a significant share of land within the Ivaí Indigenous Territory, leased by the Kaingang. A series of threats and violent incidents followed, culminating in the major attack of Jan. 4, 2025. At around 6 a.m. that Saturday morning, Lucélia Rodrigues says she woke to the sound of gunshots and fireworks. It was a warning that something serious was happening. “We went outside, and soon a bunch of cars and buses arrived, with the Kaingang from Ivaí village shooting at us, and we had to run,” she says. “I fled into the woods with my children. I could only grab my documents and my phone; I left everything behind.” Like every other house in the village, Lucélia’s home was also set on fire. “They burned my house,” she says. “And the other one I was building, I don’t know whether they dismantled it and took it away or burned it too. I stayed hidden all day.” Lucélia Rodrigues has been living with her children in a tarp-covered shack since her house was set on fire in early 2025. Image by José Pires. The flames destroyed all the belongings of the Serrinha residents. After the attack, they took shelter at a state school in Pitanga. As the start of the school year approached, a local farmer offered the families part of his property as a place to stay. They have been living there ever since. Farmers quickly settled on the leased land where Serrinha once stood. One tenant, speaking on condition of anonymity, says he pays Acil 3,000 Brazilian reais (around $500) per planted alqueire (a regional unit equal to 2.42 hectares or about 6 acres). According to the farmer, who grows soybeans and corn, the amount is charged per harvest, and the leased land can yield up to two harvests a year. Dealing with the Indigenous leaders is perilous, the farmer adds. “They are dangerous; the leaders make threats if they are crossed,” he says. “I know people who were killed for questioning the leases.” Farmers sign contracts with Acil and pay straight into the association’s bank account. “But if you’re late, or do something they don’t like, you risk running into trouble,” he says. Beyond the official payments, leaders pocket an extra fee of 500 reais per alqueire, he adds. “That extra money stays with the leaders, you know. I usually plant about 10 alqueires twice a year. So do the math: from my fields alone, they make 70,000 reais,” he says. The tenant says part of his soybean crop goes to Coamo, Brazil’s largest agricultural cooperative by revenue. Mongabay contacted Coamo about the allegation that it buys soybeans grown on the Indigenous reserve, but the cooperative did not respond by publication time. Agreement aimed at ending leases has yet to succeed In April 2025, the Federal Public Prosecutor’s Office, Funai, Acil and residents of Ivaí signed a consent decree (TAC) to establish a new agricultural, environmental and land-management system designed to end leasing on the reserve. The agreement was signed with the participation of then Funai president Joênia Wapichana.
