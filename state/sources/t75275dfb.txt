Science News from research organizations A galactic shock wave could rewrite the map of the Milky Way Date: November 23, 2026 Source: University of Sydney Summary: A surprising discovery suggests the Milky Way may be making stars in places where most galaxies cannot. Scientists found evidence that a massive rotating structure near the galactic center is creating powerful shock waves that trigger star formation rather than suppress it. Even more unexpectedly, part of the Norma spiral arm may not be a spiral arm at all, but a vast region where new stars are being born. Credit: Hillary Davis New research suggests the Milky Way produces stars in an unexpected location, revealing a surprising difference between our galaxy and most of its neighbors. The findings also raise questions about a familiar feature of the Milky Way, suggesting that part of what astronomers have long considered a spiral arm may actually be something else. The discovery comes from research led by a University of Sydney PhD student, who found evidence that a massive rotating structure of stars near the galactic center is generating powerful shock waves through surrounding clouds of gas. These waves squeeze the gas together, creating conditions that allow new stars to form in a region where such intense forces usually prevent their birth. Published in Nature Astronomy , the study also casts doubt on the nature of the Norma spiral arm, a feature of the Milky Way named for the constellation Norma as seen from Earth. Lead author Dr. Hillary Davis, who recently completed her PhD at the University of Sydney's School of Physics and Sydney Institute for Astronomy, said the research provides new insights into the structures near the galactic center and how gas moves around them. "At its heart, star formation is a process we understand from everyday physics. Forces set matter in motion. In this case gas is compressed, and that compression can create the conditions for new stars to be born on a massive scale," Dr. Davis said. "In most similar galaxies, star formation very commonly occurs along spiral arms. What's extraordinary is seeing this happen in a different region of our Galaxy, where shocks are usually so strong that star formation is often suppressed." A Hidden Star Factory at the Heart of the Milky Way The Milky Way belongs to a class of galaxies known as barred spiral galaxies. Unlike galaxies with only a central concentration of stars and surrounding spiral arms, these systems contain an elongated structure of stars stretching across their central region. In our galaxy, this structure is called the Galactic bar. As the bar rotates, its gravity influences nearby gas, driving it into powerful shock fronts. These fronts compress the gas in much the same way that a shock wave compresses air. Studying this structure from Earth is particularly difficult because we are located inside the Milky Way rather than viewing it from the outside. Large amounts of cosmic dust also block our view of the galactic center. "The Galactic bar cannot be easily observed because of a combination of our position in the Galaxy and dust obscuring our view," Dr. Davis said. "As a result, the bar's properties have been highly debated." Observations of other barred galaxies have shown that these shock fronts generally inhibit the formation of new stars, even in regions containing abundant gas. The intense conditions make it difficult for the gas to collapse into stars. However, the new findings indicate that the Milky Way may behave differently. Although it is not the only known example, our galaxy appears to belong to a relatively small group in which substantial star formation takes place along the shock fronts of the central bar. "We believe that the Milky Way's bar has star formation occurring along its shock front, which is uncommon for most bars in nearby galaxies," Dr. Davis said. A Milky Way Spiral Arm May Not Be What It Seems To investigate this unusual activity, the research team compared detailed computer simulations showing how galaxies change over billions of years with astronomical observations of regions where massive stars are forming. A crucial part of the evidence came from masers, naturally occurring sources that emit highly concentrated microwave radiation. These emissions are associated with the formation of massive stars and operate on a principle similar to lasers, but at microwave wavelengths. By examining the positions of these masers, the researchers uncovered a striking match between actual observations and the locations of shock fronts predicted by their simulations. "We found that masers previously associated with the Norma arm closely align with the shock front predicted by our simulations," Dr. Davis said. "This suggests that at least part of the so-called Norma arm may not be a spiral arm at all, but a region of star formation along the leading edge of the Galactic bar. "This raises questions about the Galaxy's spiral structure, and the bar region of the Milky Way, which needs further investigation," she said. The possibility is significant because it suggests that some of the star formation previously attributed to the Norma spiral arm could instead be occurring along the central bar. If confirmed, this interpretation would change how astronomers understand the arrangement of stars and gas in that part of the Milky Way. New Evidence Could Redraw the Milky Way's Map Beyond revealing an unusual location for star birth, the research also sheds light on the physical properties of the Galactic bar itself. Astronomers have debated the structure's length, orientation, and rotation speed for decades, partly because the Milky Way's dust and our viewing position make direct measurements so challenging. "Our findings place further constraints on the size, angle and rotation speed of this central bar," Dr. Davis said. The team's analysis indicates that the bar extends approximately 4.3 kiloparsecs, equivalent to about 14,000 light-years, outward from the galactic center along its long axis. This supports a picture of the Milky Way containing a relatively long bar that rotates slowly. The results illustrate how increasingly sophisticated simulations can help scientists investigate regions of our galaxy that are difficult to observe directly. By comparing models spanning billions of years with the available astronomical evidence, researchers can narrow down the possible structures and processes shaping the Milky Way. "By comparing a new generation of large-scale computerized galaxy simulations, evolving over billions of years, against a more limited set of observations, we now have a stronger understanding of the bar and star formation processes in the Milky Way," Dr. Davis said.
