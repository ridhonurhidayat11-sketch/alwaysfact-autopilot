That has important implications for innovators, investors and broader society, say experts 26 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Cpl Aaron J Stone/UK Ministry of Defence Only five years ago, defence technology start-ups had little choice but to wait for government funding to build and test their inventions – often in complex, multi-year programmes. No longer, however: Russia’s invasion of Ukraine in 2022 has prompted such a fast, furious response that military innovation timescales have shrunk dramatically. “Ukraine is becoming a real-world lab for military innovation, and it’s innovating at a pace greater than anything that we’ve seen previously,” says Ola Fashade, an air vice-marshal in the Royal Air Force and director of military digitalisation and the rapid AI delivery task force at the UK’s Ministry of Defence (MoD). “Agility and tempo of adaptation is critical. In a contested battlespace, technology that works well on day one, by day eight has become a little muted – and by day twelve it’s probably no longer relevant. And if you don’t adapt over that period, what you have is a very expensive and ineffective bag of steel.” That raises the question of how significant this accelerated innovation could become. In September 2026, New Scientist CoLab, sponsored by BAE Systems, brought together leading thinkers and investors from industry, the military and academia to discuss the factors driving this innovation and the lessons that can be transferred to other industries. Staying alive The investment for this step change is coming both from the technology incubators based within military prime contractors – like the Launchpad incubator at BAE Systems – and from a host of venture capital (VC) companies worldwide. “The idea behind Launchpad is to forge an ecosystem that gets technology from defence start-ups into the front line quicker, or into spinouts, before they run out of money and die,” says David Ewing, head of technology commercialisation at BAE Systems. In addition, VC funding is seeing a raft of new multibillion-dollar military tech players, dubbed neoprimes, that complement the work of the primes on bigger-ticket battlespace items. The poster child for the neoprimes, says Samuel Burrell, a partner at the pan-European VC firm Expeditions Fund, is the nascent British air defence start-up Cambridge Aerospace, which reached a valuation of $3.4 billion with little but a convincing tech narrative: “They had no battlefield data, no product – just a compelling team and an idea on paper.” It wasn’t all talk: Cambridge Aerospace has begun delivering its Skyhammer air defence missile for taking out Shahed-type drones and cruise missiles. A solid-rocket-motor-powered ballistic missile interceptor, Starhammer, will ship in 2027. In the maritime battlespace, meanwhile, fast-scaling neoprime Kraken Technology Group of Hampshire, UK, has raised $175 million from various VC firms, valuing it at $1 billion, to further develop its uncrewed surface vessel and drone technologies for users including the MoD, NATO and the US Special Operations Command. Key to it all succeeding, according to Amelia Gould, chief technology officer at Kraken, is a culture that actively nurtures iterative speed – to ensure battlefield systems stay ahead of emerging countermeasures: “Having really quick feedback loops from the warfightersin the field to r for high-consequence innovation,” Gould says. Forging ecosystems The shortening of feedback and iteration cycles, however, means tacticians must increasingly accept imperfect battlefield technologies. “We need to be much more open to minimum viable products, with relatively few safeguards, because, in conflict, staying alive is what matters,” says Hetti Barkworth-Nanton, CEO at Ploughshare, an MoD-owned specialist in turning technology into real-world applications, companies and growth. Ewing agrees: “We’ll definitely see a move away from having so many exquisite platforms, in programmes that take years to define, decades to deliver and decades to wind up, in favour of quicker cycles of technology validation, implementation and adoption.” What kind of spinoffs could emerge from this new way of refining technology? Westwood hopes some powerful answers on clean energy (see box) may emerge. “If we get ourselves really motivated around the fact that we’re boiling the Earth, that could be a real technology driver here,” he says. What makes Westwood so confident of a positive dividend outside the military? An example from history, he says: “We went into world war two with propeller-driven biplanes and came out six years later with jet fighter aircraft. And the subsequent innovation around the jet engine seeded the commercial air transport market that’s been such a huge influence on the way we all live now.” Ukraine’s move has militaries around the world racing to emulate its iterative techniques, which are based on the low-cost technologies behind smartphones and consumer drones: artificial intelligence, mobile connectivity, satellite positioning, cloud storage, digital design systems and 3D printing. “It’s the ability to develop, deploy and iterate these technologies at scale that have been the driver for the drone technology that we see Ukraine using,” says Mark Westwood, director of defence and security at Cranfield University in the UK. Read more about BAE Systems Launchpad at: baesystems.com/launchpad The civilian dividend One of the leading spinoffs from BAE Systems’ Launchpad incubator is start-up Nuclear Turbines of Manchester, UK, which has raised £15 million from four venture funds to begin developing a novel, passively safe 20-megawatt small modular nuclear reactor. The reactor is designed to power data centres and industrial plant. Its novelty is a straightforward one, says CEO Jeremy Owston: instead of driving a vast steam turbine, like a regular reactor, its reactor heats and compresses air to much hotter temperatures to drive a small, commercial gas turbine – greatly simplifying and shrinking the overall power plant. “We’re not building Hinkley Point. We’re targeting the early 2030s to get our reactor up and running and we don’t need tens of billions of pounds to do that. What we’re talking about will need a fraction of the price,” Owston says, adding that he is impressed by the willingness of the VC sector to back such ideas. There are plenty more opportunities worth investing in, says Hetti Barkworth-Nanton at the MoD’s intellectual property spinoff unit, Ploughshare. “Of the billions spent every year in the UK on defence innovation, only a fraction results in IP being spun out. The track record of that innovation reaching civilian markets is weak.” VCs are beginning to notice this opportunity. “There are so many of these funds and they just want to take a bet on something, and that’s great,” says Owston.
