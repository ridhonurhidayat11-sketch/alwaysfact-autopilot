It’s tough to tally the injuries or deaths that don’t happen thanks to seat belts, lead testing, smoking bans and vaccines. “It is the invisibility paradox,” says Georges Benjamin, CEO of the American Public Health Association in Washington, D.C. “We are invisible because when our best work is done, nothing happens.” But when disaster strikes — say, an outbreak or a pandemic — people take notice. This year’s outbreaks of measles, Ebola, hantavirus and cyclosporiasis brought recent federal reductions into focus — and exposed cracks in a system that aims to keep people healthy. For years, that system functioned “reasonably well” despite chronic underfunding, Benjamin says. “But since the Trump administration has come on board, they have really made the system at all levels much more dysfunctional.” Sign up for our newsletter We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday. In 2025, the second Trump administration instituted sweeping federal funding cuts and layoffs , dismantled the global health–focused U.S. Agency for International Development and withdrew the United States from the World Health Organization . Officials reversed some of those changes in the wake of lawsuits and public backlash. But the damage persists. Two stalwarts of U.S. public health — the Food and Drug Administration and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention — have lost between 20 and 30 percent of their staff, due largely to layoffs and early retirements. They have had to scale back, pause or cancel crucial disease-tracking programs at home and abroad. Some CDC divisions don’t have enough staff to function. And both agencies have cut funding to the local governments and nonprofits that shoulder much of the nation’s public health work. Combined with deep cuts to international aid, that has stymied efforts to contain infectious disease outbreaks around the world, with Ebola in particular going undetected for months, public health experts say. Domestically, 2026 saw a record-breaking measles outbreak, with some 5,412 confirmed cases as of November 12. That’s more than double last year’s count, and it is the highest number of annual cases since 1991. In mid November, the Pan American Health Organization determined that measles is again endemic to the United States and has stripped the country of its elimination status. Against a backdrop of shifting federal vaccine guidance, fewer children are receiving the MMR shot that prevents measles. Roughly 92 percent of kindergartners across the United States had gotten the vaccine in the 2025–2026 school year, down from 95 percent in the 2019–2020 school year, according to the CDC. At least 95 percent of people in a community need to be vaccinated to prevent the measles virus from sparking an outbreak. “The fact that we are spending so much time and energy [on measles] when we have a reliable vaccine is very disconcerting,” says Lori Handy, a pediatric infectious diseases physician and associate director of the Vaccine Education Center at Children’s Hospital of Philadelphia. The CDC has not responded to Science News requests for an interview. Such issues do not bode well for the future. Public health is tested during times of crisis, and the world will eventually face another pandemic, says Michael Osterholm, director of the Center for Infectious Disease Research and Policy at the University of Minnesota in Minneapolis. “We’ve not responded [to the 2026 outbreaks] in a way that would give me confidence that the public health system … is prepared for what’s coming down the pike.” A preventable outbreak On the heels of multiple 2025 outbreaks, measles continued to wallop parts of the United States this year. Communities in South Carolina, Utah and Wisconsin have been among the hardest hit, with sporadic outbreaks also popping up in other states. Until August, however, none of this year’s outbreaks had turned deadly. That all changed in Pennsylvania. Since April, seven people in the state have died, including two infants too young to be vaccinated. The CDC has so far attributed just six deaths to measles, despite the insistence of Pennsylvania state public health officials. Three children died of measles in November in New York and Wisconsin. Measles’ improbable comeback As of November 12, U.S. health officials recorded 5,412 measles cases for 2026. The last time this many cases were recorded was 1991, nearly a decade before the disease was eliminated from the country. Measles spreads like wildfire, and one sick person can infect more than a dozen others in an unvaccinated community.
