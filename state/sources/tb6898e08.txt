Credit: Laura Olivas/Getty People born later in the twentieth century have a better memory when they enter old age and experience slower cognitive decline than do those born earlier, according to a study 1 published in Nature Medicine on 17 November. The analysis offers clues about why dementia rates have fallen by 13% per decade over the past 27 years in Europe and the United States 2 . The researchers analysed data from memory tests that required participants to recall lists of words. The study included 219,000 people aged 50 and older who were born between 1910 and 1964, and tracked changes in their scores over three decades. These list-based tests are commonly used to screen for dementia and Alzheimer’s disease. Consistent with previous studies 3 , people born more recently entered old age with better memory performance compared with those born in earlier generations. A ten-year jump in birth date was associated with a 10% higher memory score at the age of 60. Memory decline also appears to have become less steep across successive generations, says study co-author Anders Fjell, a neuropsychologist at the University of Oslo in Norway. The findings are “confirmatory” of other studies on the topic, which indicated that the rate of memory decline is slowing down but weren’t statistically significant, says Carol Brayne, an epidemiologist who studies dementia at the University of Cambridge, UK. “It is another piece of the jigsaw.” Stable memories Because many populations around the world are ageing — that is, older individuals are becoming a larger proportion of the total population — the number of people living with dementia is increasing worldwide. But an individual person’s risk of dementia at any given age has dropped by two-thirds over the past 40 years in the United States 4 . “For those who reach 80, a smaller percentage [of people] will get dementia than before,” explains Fjell. Memory stays strong in old age when this brain enzyme is active To understand what might be driving this trend, Fjell and his colleagues used longitudinal data on individuals’ cognitive performance to estimate their memory trajectories over time. The authors examined whether people born later in the twentieth century showed differences in their memory performance and their rate of memory decline compared with those born earlier. The analysis found that with each successive birth decade, people were 64% more likely to maintain stable memory function — that is, experience little or no memory deterioration. The authors also investigated brain imaging data from nearly 2,000 people over a median of four years. They found that a stable memory during ageing also corresponded to a decrease in shrinkage of the hippocampus — a brain region involved in memory formation.
