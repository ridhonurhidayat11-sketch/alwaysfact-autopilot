Mongabay speaks with Maria Amália Souza, the founder and director of Global Philanthropy Strategies for Fundo Casa Socioambiental in Brazil, about how their movement-based approach differs from conventional philanthropy, describing it as “power with” rather than “power over.” She reflects on the interplay between philanthropy, democracy and nature conservation in Brazil, the legacy of COP30 and the stakes of the recent federal elections. See All Key Ideas In November 2025, Indigenous and Afro-descendent peoples, frontline defenders and civil society movements mobilized in unprecedented numbers in Belém, Brazil, to advocate for their demands at the 30th U.N. Climate Change Conference (COP30). One of the key demands was for direct access to climate finance. At COP30, donors announced a new $1.8 billion pledge to support Indigenous peoples’ and Afro-descendent and local communities’ land tenure and resource rights. However, despite certain advances in climate finance and philanthropy, major bottlenecks still remain a year later when it comes to implementation of such commitments. For example, under the previous $1.7 billion pledge , only 7.2% of the funds in 2025 went directly to Indigenous and community organizations, with the vast majority routed through international NGOs, consultancies and development banks. “That system is ill-designed and it is trapping the money. We know what we need. We have the systems and we don’t want anybody else in the middle creating these layers,” Maria Amália Souza, Fundo Casa Socioambiental’s founder and director of Global Philanthropy Strategies, tells Mongabay. “We can attend to the demands of our societies, our movements and our causes much better because we belong to them. The belonging is the part that nobody else can claim.” Determined to not replicate the shortcomings of conventional philanthropy, Souza gathered community and movement leaders at Fundo Casa Socioambiental , a Brazilian socio-environmental fund established in 2005, to design a system to rapidly redistribute funds through thousands of small grants across the country. In partnership with others, they have contributed to a wide range of community-led initiatives such as demarcation and defense of Indigenous peoples’ territories, solar energy and agroforestry cooperatives, and networks of women environmental defenders. For more than four decades, Souza has helped build movement-based philanthropic infrastructure within Brazil, across the Global Majority and internationally through several organizations and networks. She is also a driving force behind the Alliance of Socio-Environmental Funds of the Global South, which recently received a WINGS Ecosystem Builder Award . In an interview with Mongabay’s Holly Jonas, Souza reflects on the legacy of COP30 — also known as the Amazon COP — the political stakes of Brazil’s recent elections, and why philanthropy is effective when it is rooted in trust and belonging. The following interview has been edited for length and clarity. Mongabay: It has been one year since COP30 was held in Belém, Brazil. Looking back, how do you think that moment will be, or should be, remembered? Maria Amália Souza: I think it should be remembered, first of all, for the number of civil society organizations mobilized and present. Brazilian civil society is really organized and has an unquestionable strength. Organizations in the Amazon have been meeting for decades, including at the Pan-Amazonian Social Forum . People came by boat from all over the Amazon. It was really beautiful. I don’t think that any COP will ever compare. Second, an extremely important lesson in democracy was the day the Indigenous Munduruku people occupied the front of the Blue Zone [where negotiations take place]. They didn’t let anybody in, so the conference couldn’t begin. They wanted to talk to the president of Brazil to question why permits that would affect them were being granted. He didn’t come, but the president of the COP [Andre Corrêa do Lago] did. They know each other personally. The Munduruku organized, the government didn’t come with bombs and tear gas, and they negotiated. I think the biggest lesson of COP30 is that this can be done: You actually can respect the people who have the right to demand protection from the state. The Munduruku were right to do it. They are warriors. They were here before, and they are threatened all the time in all kinds of ways. I think this was a really brilliant moment that I hope reverberates for a very long time. Third, we launched the Global South House [an initiative of the Socio-Environmental Funds of the Global South and the Comuá Network in Brazil], with a weeklong program in Belém. It is a platform that calls attention to the infrastructure of Global South philanthropy that has existed for decades, but is completely underfunded because the money is blocked elsewhere. The Global South House now has 44 Global South funds from the Pan-Amazonian region, Congo Basin and Indonesia, and community funds of Indigenous Peoples, Afro-descendant peoples, forest dwellers and women, as well as other aligned actors. We’ve been taking the Global South House out to the world, including at London Climate Action Week and COP31 in Türkiye, and the next big one will be at COP32 in Ethiopia. There is a momentum that started in Belém. Mongabay: What happened after the Munduruku peoples’ action at COP30? What are some of the longer-term efforts that led to that flashpoint moment? Maria Amália Souza: There was a whole infrastructure behind it; it wasn’t just making noise. It was a demand to negotiate and to be heard. They knew they had the right to do it, and they used the legal systems they have. They’re very strong and they got their land demarcated. Of course, it should have been done decades ago, but it’s a constant struggle. Indigenous Munduruku demonstrating at COP30 in November 2025. Our constitution respects Indigenous peoples’ lands, and Brazil ratified ILO Convention 169, which reinforces free, prior and informed consent. The state has legal mechanisms to enforce these rights, including public prosecutors and the structure in charge of protecting traditional rights. The Munduruku have been developing a protocol to tell the government how they wanted to be consulted and what free, prior informed consent means to them. Many other Indigenous groups and also Afro-descendant peoples have also been developing these protocols of consultation. We [Fundo Casa] have been funding a number of aspects of their struggles for 20 years. That’s the difference when you are a local funding structure dedicated to actually pushing your democracy forward and ensuring participation: There is a whole other way of making those grants. The rights-based approach is for the long haul, and you need to be there for whatever may happen. If something needs to happen tomorrow, we need to make sure money is there for them. Mongabay: How would you describe Fundo Casa Socioambiental’s approach to philanthropy? How does it differ from conventional philanthropy? Maria Amália Souza: The way we do philanthropy is based on the science of systems dynamics. It’s about being so deeply rooted in the struggle that we are like a financing arm with territorial intelligence. We are efficient because we understand how to move within the system. Nobody is going to plan our causes for us. Philanthropy is less effective when it is limited to someone else’s idea of what they want to fund.
