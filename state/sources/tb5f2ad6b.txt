Manage your subscription Your newsletters Subscriber benefits Sign out Subscribe now Physics Ultracold atoms could help unravel a major flaw in quantum theory Standard quantum theory struggles to predict how long a particle will take to get from one place to another, but an experiment with ultracold atoms could help rule out some interpretations By Karmela Padavic-Callaghan 26 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Extremely cold atoms form a state of matter called a Bose-Einstein condensate NIST Extremely cold atoms could help physicists answer a long-debated question about quantum particles and time. This may shed light on the accuracy of different interpretations of quantum physics. When it comes to time, quantum physics fails to answer even the simplest questions. For instance, predicting how long it will take a quantum particle to travel from some point A to a nearby point B has been debated since quantum theory’s earliest days. There are several competing ideas for calculating the particle’s arrival time in standard quantum theory, as well as in its non-standard versions. Pascal Naidon and Lucas Happ at RIKEN in Japan and Denis Boiron at Paris-Saclay University in France now argue that an experiment with ultracold atoms could add clarity. Advertisement “[Ours] is the first proposal whose concrete implementation has been checked by an experimentalist and has a good chance of being successfully realised in the future,” says Naidon. Boiron specialises in atomic experiments. Read more Ultracold clocks could reveal how quantum physics alters time The researchers’ proposal starts with a collection of tens or hundreds of thousands of atoms that have been chilled to temperatures remarkably close to absolute zero, the ultimate coldness limit , by using lasers and electromagnetic fields. Under these conditions, atoms’ behaviour is dominated by quantum effects. In the experiment, the atoms would be corralled into the shape of a cigar, then allowed to fall onto a detector about half a metre below. They would move vertically from their initial position, point A, towards the detector, point B. Because quantum objects behave probabilistically, neither every atom nor every run of the experiment would exhibit identical behaviour, but repeating the experiment a few hundred times would give the researchers a distribution of detection times. They could then compare this data with different predictions based on various interpretations of quantum theory. Naidon says the experiment could test ideas such as the “pilot-wave” or “trajectory-based” quantum theory pioneered by Luis de Broglie and David Bohm . In standard quantum theory, quantum objects’ reality is fuzzy and indeterminate until they interact with a measuring device, but this proposes that their behaviour is guided by a very real pilot wave caused by some yet-undiscovered physical field. “It is possible that the trajectory-based interpretations will be ruled out from the results of the experiment,” says Naidon. Read more ‘Reverse physics’ could help bridge the classical and quantum worlds Sheldon Goldstein at Rutgers University in New Jersey says past experiments have failed to discriminate between different versions of quantum theory, but ideas like the new proposal can highlight the limitations of orthodox quantum theory. While it can get to grips with particles’ properties, such as their position, standard quantum theory doesn’t offer a recipe for calculating time, he says. “Usually some analysis of an experiment or a theoretical issue requires a sequence of steps. This issue of arrival times goes off the rails at step zero,” he says. “What is even meant by the arrival time in textbook quantum theory? It’s not clear what it is.” This makes it difficult to compare standard quantum theory with alternatives that can produce clearer arrival-time predictions, says Siddhant Das at Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich. But some ideas for solving the arrival time problem with standard theory have been formulated anyway and could be tested, and even eliminated, by the proposed experiment, he says. Importantly, the arrival time of quantum particles is often an ingredient in other physics experiments, such as those involving beams of particles , so the stakes of understanding it transcend purely theoretical debates, says Das. “We have had enough theory papers. We can keep arguing about this till the cows come home,” he says. “I would love to see the experiment being done, and then we’ll see how much we can interpret.” Journal Reference: Physical Review Letters DOI: 10.1103/683f-3qxt Topics: Physics / Quantum physics Advertisement Sign up to our weekly newsletter Receive a weekly dose of discovery in your inbox.
