Credit: Mariana Bif Summer 2026 saw record-breaking heat across much of the planet, including in Europe and the United States . It also saw anomalous precipitation trends, such as substantial monsoon deficits in India and Southeast Asia and wetter-than-normal conditions in South America’s Atacama Desert and western Australia . Numerous news and official reports have linked the exceptional conditions to this year’s emerging super El Niño. Air temperatures and rainfall over land aren’t the only things affected by the El Niño; it influences ocean temperatures, chemistry, and nutrient distributions as well. We know this because of an array of hundreds of oceangoing floats carrying scientific instruments that monitor ocean conditions day and night and across depths. This year, the array recorded the ocean’s response to the growing El Niño, revealing changes as they happened, before their wider ecological consequences have become apparent. Such a detailed picture of the chemical and ecological conditions throughout the water column was not available prior to the advent of the Biogeochemical Argo, or BGC-Argo , float network and its multiple deployments at the equator since 2019. These floats offer valuable information both for scientific research and for operational and policy decisions related to marine ecosystems, including vital fisheries. The floats have limited lifetimes, however, and as individual floats reach the end of their service, they must be replaced to avoid leaving gaps in coverage. With U.S. support for the BGC-Argo network ending this year, sustained monitoring of the equatorial ocean is at risk: The country is the major contributor to the region’s fleet of robotic floats. National governments and agencies must step up to recognize and preserve the value of vital ocean-observing infrastructure. El Niño Affects More Than Temperatures and Rainfall One of the clearest signs of El Niño is warmer-than-usual surface water in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. Most years, trade winds blow westward across the equatorial Pacific Ocean, driving warm water from South America toward Asia. Cold water then rises, or upwells, from the ocean depths toward the surface in the eastern Pacific. Rain clouds loom over Puerto Villamil’s beachfront on Isabela Island in Ecuador’s Galápagos on 2 November 2026. El Niño conditions, as already demonstrated in 2026, typically bring heavier-than-normal rainfall to the Galápagos Archipelago. Credit: Mariana Bif However, during El Niño years, the trade winds weaken, and the warm waters of the eastern Pacific remain close to the west coast of the Americas, suppressing the upwelling of cold deep water. As a result, the atmospheric polar jet stream shifts southward, producing characteristic weather patterns such as increased winter storm activity and precipitation in the southern United States and western South America, whereas drought conditions commonly develop over southern Asia, Indonesia, and Australia. These tools are complemented by data from other “core” Argo floats that monitor temperature and salinity but not chemistry. This past spring, forecasters were already signaling the likelihood of a strong El Niño this year, and as of mid-November, NOAA reported that this year’s event was still strengthening and had a greater than 79% chance of remaining a “strong-to-very strong” event through fall and winter. But temperatures alone do not give us the whole picture; in particular, they don’t tell us how the event is affecting the nutrients that support marine life and fisheries. The robotic floats of the BGC-Argo array can provide that missing view. Distributed throughout the global ocean, these floats repeatedly descend and rise through the upper 2,000 meters of the ocean, measuring nitrate, oxygen, and other properties and occasionally surfacing to transmit their observations (Figure 1). Nitrate is an essential nutrient for phytoplankton, the microscopic organisms that form the base of marine food webs. When nitrate availability changes, the conditions supporting ocean productivity change too. Fig. 1. As of October 2026, the entire BGC-Argo array comprised 985 floats. Credit: OceanOPS/Argo This Year Is Different We can pair the BGC-Argo array’s nutrient observations with daily ocean data to follow the developing 2026 El Niño. The Niño 3.4 region , a rectangular area along the equatorial Pacific that is affected by El Niño’s expanding warm tongue, provides a case study. This is the same region used by NOAA to monitor and classify the strength of El Niño and La Niña states. In Figure 2, an animated sequence of maps of daily sea surface temperatures across the Niño 3.4 region, shown with superimposed nitrate measurements from BGC-Argo floats, reveals how temperature and nutrient conditions changed between 1 April and 9 November. We can also compare the developing 2026 conditions with those during the same period (and later) of the 2023–2024 El Niño event, when BGC-Argo floats were also collecting data. Fig. 2. This animation pairs daily ARMOR3D sea surface temperature (SST) data from Copernicus Marine with BGC-Argo nitrate measurements (available at argo.ucsd.edu ) in the equatorial Pacific during (a) the 2023–2024 El Niño (1 April 2023 to 31 March 2024) and (b) the developing 2026 event (1 April 2026 to 9 November 2026). Colored circles mark float nitrate measurements at sampled locations. The nitrate scale spans 0–10 micromoles per kilogram (µmol kg -1 ). White circles represent low nitrate (0–2 µmol kg -1 ), and circles marked with an x indicate concentrations below the 0.5 µmol kg -1 detection limit. In the western Niño 3.4 region, float measurements show an early decline in surface nitrate (i.e., in the upper 10 meters of depth) during this year’s El Niño onset. Measurements from farther east suggest that low-nitrate conditions, associated with warmer waters, spread as the event developed.
