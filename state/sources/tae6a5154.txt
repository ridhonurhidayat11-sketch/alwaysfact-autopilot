After two years with nearly 100 measles outbreaks, thousands sickened and nearly a dozen deaths, the United States has officially lost its measles elimination status. The highly contagious disease is now endemic in the United States, the Pan American Health Organization declared November 17. Analyses of cases dated from when the first outbreak began in 2025 in West Texas through part of 2026 revealed the virus has been spreading uninterrupted for more than a year. Mexico and Bolivia also lost their measles elimination status, PAHO announced; Canada had lost it in 2025. “Measles is resurging globally amid persistent gaps in vaccination coverage,” PAHO director Jarbas Barbosa said in a Nov. 17 news conference. “And this global trend is also affecting the Americas.” Countries will need to not only stop ongoing outbreaks, but also prove that no one caught measles within their borders for a full year to regain measles-free status. That won’t happen overnight. But there is a way, experts say. Vaccinating people across the country, particularly in areas with low vaccination rates, will stifle the virus’s spread. But so far, the U.S. federal public health response has taken a back seat to efforts from state and local health departments to curb ongoing outbreaks. Measles’s return “is an enormous black eye on the reputation of the United States,” said epidemiologist Jennifer Nuzzo of the Brown University School of Public Health in Providence, R.I., in a Nov. 9 webinar hosted by the university. The Americas led the global campaign to control the virus, and in 2016 the region became the first to eliminate measles transmission from all its countries. “To lose [elimination status] means we’re going backwards… meaning decades of progress lost,” Nuzzo said. “It’s an indicator of our lack of protection. It is an indicator of our lack of preparedness for disease threats in general.” Measles made an unwelcome comeback PAHO’s verdict comes amid a surge of measles not seen in the United States since 1991. Health officials have recorded more than 7,000 cases since the beginning of the West Texas outbreak. That tally includes nearly 2,300 measles cases in 2025 and 5,412 in 2026 as of November 12, according to the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. Over 90 percent of cases have been in people who are unvaccinated or whose status is unknown. States report that 13 people have died, although the CDC so far recognizes only nine of those deaths. While mild measles symptoms include high fever, cough and a rash, roughly 1 in 5 people will be hospitalized with complications such as pneumonia or brain swelling. Children younger than 5 years and adults over 20 are more likely to get sick, as are pregnant women and their infants. At least 13 babies were born infected , called congenital measles, since the beginning of 2025, researchers report November 16 in NEJM Evidence . Those babies are at risk for developing dangerous brain swelling that is almost always fatal later on in life. The measles, mumps and rubella, or MMR, vaccine helped eliminate measles from the United States in 2000. After that, cases still sometimes popped up. Those cases were mostly in unvaccinated people traveling from parts of the world where the virus still widely circulates. At least 95 percent of people in a community need to be vaccinated to prevent outbreaks. But widespread misinformation, including a disproven claim that the vaccine causes autism , means fewer children are receiving the MMR shot and more people are questioning its safety. According to the CDC, roughly 92 percent of kindergarteners across the United States had gotten the vaccine in 2025, down from 95 percent in 2019. And in 2025, 83 percent of U.S. adults viewed the MMR vaccine as safe , down from 86 percent in 2024, according to a survey from the University of Pennsylvania’s Annenberg Public Policy Center. Yet decades of evidence show the vaccine is safe and highly effective. That measles made a comeback is not a surprise, says Nathan Lo, an infectious diseases physician and epidemiologist at Stanford University School of Medicine. Until recently, outbreaks were largely isolated to communities with low vaccination rates and quickly contained. But now, outbreaks seem to be more likely to spill over into neighboring communities, Lo says. “There’s a lot of potential connectedness between the outbreaks.” Sponsor Message What’s more, some infected people aren’t getting tested, making them invisible to officials who are now struggling to trace chains of transmission.
