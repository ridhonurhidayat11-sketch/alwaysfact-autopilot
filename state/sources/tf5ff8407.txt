Sign Up I have ζ(s), the zeta function at the center of the Riemann hypothesis, tattooed on my leg, so I took last Friday’s news harder than most. That morning I read my colleague Joseph Howlett’s story : Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said he had proved the hypothesis, with a heavy dose of help from the company’s artificial intelligence model Claude. By Saturday, outside experts had validated the proof. This is likely the capstone of AI’s yearlong rampage through mathematics. Earlier results dazzled mathematicians and former mathematicians like me, but this was the first one to flip my stomach. Honestly, I felt like I was going to throw up. The Riemann hypothesis and I have history. In college I studied it and its connections to the primes and cry ptography , and after about 18 months I made the relationship permanent. My tattoo artist admitted he had limited experience with math but said he enjoyed learning more about primes once he had heard my request. Instead math’s most famous problem was resolved in a way that, so far, no one can figure out. And figuring it out is meant to be the fun part. Much of this problem’s beauty lies in how every aspect of it unfurls until it touches far-flung areas of mathematics. The zeta function starts as a simple sum over the integers, but a mathematical trick rewrites it as a product over the primes. That’s usually the first hint of what lies beneath the surface. Mathematicians can then extend it across the entire complex plane, the plane containing numbers with real and imaginary parts. There, other formulas tie it to other famous functions, such as the factorial. The hypothesis itself can be generalized to an infinite family of functions, which Alpöge says he proved. Even the problem’s consequences seem limitless because the primes are the building blocks of numbers. Understanding the Riemann hypothesis is like sticking your finger into a puddle, expecting to hit bottom quickly, only to realize the pool stretches deeper than you can reach. Honestly, I felt like I was going to throw up. Yet most of us, myself included, had long assumed the hypothesis was true. The first 10 trillion or so solutions to the function were already known to satisfy the hypothesis, and there was little reason to think the streak would end. Even the Clay Mathematics Institute, which offers a $1-million prize for a resolution, mentions only “a proof that it is true” in a summary. The way I always heard it, the real prize would be the techniques used to prove it. Nobody had any idea how. Whoever finally cracked it would have to dredge up something from the depths, and that discovery could lead to whole new ways of thinking about numbers. It’s more than a little ironic, then, that the problem is now solved, and those techniques have yet to surface. Or rather they’re stuck inside millions of lines of code written in the programming language Lean and in an AI-written explanation that Alpöge hasn’t released. (He first said that explanation ran past 250,000 pages and then backpedaled on X, calling the number a joke: “I find big number extremely hilarious.”) Some experts say true human understanding could be months or years away. And who knows what that understanding will be worth next to the models of a couple of years from now? I used to think the issue at the heart of AI and mathematics was labor, the same concern artists have raised about AI’s use in their own field. My mathematician and computer scientist friends worry most about their jobs: whether there will be any and what the work will look like. Anyone finishing a typical American Ph.D. this year probably started it before ChatGPT existed. They had no way of knowing the job description would one day include interpreting an AI oracle. I still believe labor is an essential part of the conversation, but Friday showed me what AI’s prowess means for doing math in its purest and most mathemagical sense. And while I am in awe of what we’ve seen so far and excited for what is to come, I do feel a little emptier, still waiting on an answer to the real question: How?
