Manage your subscription Your newsletters Subscriber benefits Sign out Subscribe now Comment The 28 best popular science books of 2026 From space-time to saunas, here are the very best popular science books published this year that you should be adding to your Christmas list By New Scientist 19 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print New Scientist writers have picked their favourite popular science reads of the year It’s the perfect time to dive into the year’s best non-fiction, whether you’re looking for gift-giving inspiration or a brilliant read for yourself. To help, here are the best popular science reads of 2026 according to New Scientist staff and writers. You’ll find the big names of science writing here, of course: Carlo Rovelli, Xand Van Tulleken, Maggie Aderin, Jim Al-Khalili. But there are also plenty of lesser-known gems, whether you’re interested in the power of heat to boost your health, a history of plants, why exactly humans sleep so little, or how to deal with the generally overwhelming nature of life. And if you think we’ve missed anything, do come and recommend your own favourite popular science reads here . Alison Flood On Time by Jim Al-Khalili The nature of time is a subject that has perplexed us for centuries. What Jim Al-Khalili does so expertly here is to break down the topic into a series of separate mysteries, explain each one and then build back up again to show how they interrelate and inform each other. Why do we feel time flowing, why does that flow feel continuous like a river, and what exactly is the difference between the past and the future? Al-Khalili tackles all this and much more, and I finished the book feeling like I understood more or less everything about time I would ever need to. Admittedly, that probably isn’t the case, but the fact that Al-Khalili created that illusion is itself a testament to this brilliant book. Joshua Howgego Hotwired by Bill Gifford Hotwired : How the hidden power of heat makes us stronger explores how saunas and other forms of heat exposure can boost health and athletic performance, and possibly prepare us for a hotter world. Gifford once hated being hot, having grown up in “steamy, swampy” Washington DC, but his interest was piqued by studies from Finland showing that frequent sauna use reduces the risk of heart attacks, stroke and Alzheimer’s disease. Gifford brings us on his personal journey of learning to love heat. He doesn’t do anything in half measures, even signing himself up for one of the world’s most gruelling bike rides under the ferocious Texan sun. The rigorous science mixed with personal stories, humour and tales of tragedy and triumph make this a highly engaging read. Alice Klein The Edge of Space-Time by Chanda Prescod-Weinstein This is a stunningly rich and interdisciplinary book that invites the reader to understand our universe from the ground up, challenging us to think creatively and with urgency. Prescod-Weinstein doesn’t shy away from bringing anthropology, history and politics into the story of the cosmos, which enriches and expands it past more conventional approaches. And she has fun, too, as her book is replete with pop culture references, from Star Trek to Missy Elliot. Reading The Edge of Space-Time requires time and focus, and generously rewards both. Karmela Padavic-Callaghan Read more Our verdict on Chanda Prescod-Weinstein’s The Edge of Space-Time: a wild ride The Beginning Comes After the End by Rebecca Solnit The world desperately needs us to change our attitudes to ecology, nature, food, energy, consumption and race. As Rebecca Solnit reminds us in this slender, hopeful and galvanising book, we’ve already come a long way. I was especially pleased to see the acknowledgement that symbiosis is a vital process in ecology, and a discussion of the growing understanding that we are part of, not separate from, nature. These are key points that Solnit and I chatted about when I interviewed her for the New Scientist podcast. “There is no going back,” Solnit writes, “though how we go forward is the work.” Rowan Hooper On the Equality of All Things by Carlo Rovelli The theoretical physicist sets out here to deliver a theory of everything, from space-time to ethics and consciousness. Based on a series of lectures, the book is rooted in quantum physics and divided into themed chapters that progressively expand Rovelli’s frame. He writes not just about the nitty-gritty of doing physics, but the character of science itself, what it can offer and how we should adjust our philosophical views of it – and the world. It’s an impassioned book well suited to readers who may know a little bit about physics, but crave answers to the biggest questions about physical existence. Karmela Padavic-Callaghan Make Me Well by Xand Van Tulleken Knowing how to care for ourselves feels harder than ever, thanks to the burgeoning wellness industry. Navigating the sea of supplements, wearable health monitors and at-home health tests is nearly impossible – even I don’t have the time to dig through every claim, and I am a full-time health journalist! The good news is that doctor, writer and TV presenter Xand Van Tulleken has. He takes on the wellness industry in his latest book Make Me Well : One doctor’s quest to understand the confusing world of wellness. It is a comprehensive, entertaining and informative read that will make you think twice before you buy into the latest wellness trend. Grace Wade Xand van Tulleken on the 3 wellness trends to be most wary of The wellness boom has brought a wide array of consumer products that promise to boost your health and happiness. Xand van Tulleken has delved into it all and explains how to think about the evidence behind the hype The Sea-King and the Sorceress by Neil Price Neil Price is unmatched at conveying the humanity, history and, frankly, weirdness of the Vikings. The Sea-King and the Sorceress almost doubles as a travelogue, taking us across – and, at times, well beyond – the medieval Norse world to visit the final resting places of an array of different people, not all of whom were professional raiders. Each person’s grave contains tantalising clues about the world in which they lived, including what they valued and who, in turn, cherished them, and why. Surprises and rich detail abound; it will make you think about the Viking Age in an entirely new way. Kelsey Hayes In Search of Now by Jo Marchant We live in the moment, the “now”, yet according to physics, there is no such thing. Similarly, neuroscience tells us that “now” is just a construct, an illusion put together by the brain to help us get through the world. So why is now so central to our very existence? That is the subject of this enthralling and enlightening read by my colleague Jo Marchant . There are plenty of books on neuroscience, many on cosmology and even quite a few on quantum physics. But I don’t know of many that combine deep thinking from all these fields, or many writers who could manage it with such clarity. Yet that’s what Jo achieves in this fascinating book. Rowan Hooper Alive by Melanie Challenger What is life, asks Melanie Challenger . She pushes an answer: life is matter that pursues goals, that has agency. A thunderstorm may be violently active, but it isn’t trying to achieve anything. In contrast, animals seek out comfort and pleasure, they try to mate, they avoid dangerous predators. Even a single-celled bacterium makes efforts to feed itself and to maintain its own internal chemistry. This may seem obvious, but different ideas – notably that living organisms are machines for propagating their genes – have seized many biologists’ minds. Challenger seeks to reclaim life’s active nature. Either of them is worthy of your time. How Flowers Made Our World by biologist David George Haskell is a lyrical contemplation of flowering plants, the angiosperms. The book’s central theme is their staggering importance to humanity and most other land-based life.
