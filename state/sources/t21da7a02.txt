Leading mathematician Terence Tao — who has been called the Mozart of maths — explains how it can in his latest book, Six Maths Essentials . Can maths survive AI? Fields medallist Terence Tao calls for a ‘Maths 2.0’ world For Tao, the six concepts are the building blocks of reality that together provide an opportunity to better understand it. They are, he writes: “numbers as the gateway to quantitative thinking, algebra as the gateway to abstraction, geometry as a way to go beyond what we can see, probability as a tool to navigate uncertainty with rigorous thinking, analysis as a means to tame the very large or very small, and dynamics as the mathematics of change”. Each is described with elegant minimalism, consistent with Tao’s vocation of writing concise mathematical proofs. In its brevity, the book evokes physicist Richard Feynman ’s Six Easy Pieces (1994), which explains fundamental physics concepts to the layperson. Similarly, the beauty of Tao’s book is seeing maths through his eyes, describing its spirit as well as generalizing across the discipline. For instance, Tao describes how artificial intelligence inspires awe and magic — it easily lives up to science-fiction writer Arthur C. Clarke’s famous statement that “any sufficiently advanced technology is indistinguishable from magic”. But AI has a dark side, too. It can lead us to become superstitious and think that the tool cares about us, or to develop a sense of fear that we wouldn’t know how to live if it malfunctioned. Pioneering figures Tao makes the case that maths has played an important part in the story of humanity. We meet famous mathematicians such as ninth-century Islamic astronomer Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī, who pioneered algebra, following in the footsteps of the ancient Babylonians, and gave his name to the Latin form of the term algorithm. Others solved real-world problems, from calculating the distance between Earth and the Sun to understanding the 2008 global financial crisis. We learn about mathematicians who overcame the desire of philosophers such as Immanuel Kant to pigeonhole geometry into a single ‘true’ concept, instead finding that there is a plurality of connected geometries. Other mathematicians revealed the laws of chance through their passion for gambling. AI has transformed maths — this is how we got here Tao invokes hardly any equations, opting instead for intuitive explanations. It is through this same intuition, Tao argues, that children work out that there is no highest number, thus deducing the concept of infinity. He discusses the elegance of algebra and its ability to solve many problems without requiring a tailored equation for each application. This generalizability, he notes, is what distinguishes maths from organic chemistry — which was his least favourite subject in school, he writes, because it required memorizing disparate facts without organizing principles like those found in maths. He is at his best explaining difficult topics to the public. On analysis, he writes that there are both hard and soft aspects, corresponding to quantitative and qualitative modes. He calls analysis the accountant of maths: “auditing and proving which techniques work under what circumstances”. He uses mathematician John Conway ’s Game of Life to demonstrate the need to study dynamics. In this game, simple rules are applied to a matrix of elements that are either ‘on’ or ‘off’ as they unfold into complex patterns. The grid functions as a rudimentary yet, in principle, fully functional computer. John Conway used games to transform mathematics. Credit: Dith Pran/NYT/Redux/eyevine A rebellious side Importantly, Tao conveys how mathematicians think as they discover new maths. He distinguishes the ‘cold’ maths found in textbooks from the active maths that happens as mathematicians explore fresh domains. For example, he describes how Carl Friedrich Gauss imagined what it would be like to be on the surface of a sphere — a view that led to his “remarkable theorem” on the properties of curvature, proved in 1827.
