Sign Up Undeniably, one of the best parts of Thanksgiving is the leftovers —specifically, the pie. As a child, pumpkin pie was king: I feel like I could have eaten it all day, every day, without getting tired of it. My deep love for pumpkin pie sparked a vital question: What would happen to my body if I ate nothing but pumpkin pie for a week? I decided to investigate so you don’t have to. And nor should you want to: the experts I spoke to were unanimous in their analysis—an all-pie diet would wreak havoc on your body. The ingredients in any given pumpkin pie vary. But even accounting for those differences, you would likely struggle to meet your nutritional needs, says Christopher Damman , an associate professor of gastroenterology and medicine at the University of Washington. Pumpkin pie is often full of sugar and saturated fats and short on protein and fiber, he says. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, a slice of pumpkin pie contains about 25 grams of sugar and 323 calories. If you ate six slices, or 1,938 calories, per day, that might be a typical number of calories, but you’d be consuming 150 grams of sugar daily. The American Heart Association recommends that adults get no more than 6 percent of calories from sugar per day, or about 25 to 36 grams. A single pumpkin pie slice also contains about 5.2 grams of protein and about 2.4 grams of fiber, according to the USDA; six slices per day would give you 31.2 grams of protein and 14.4 grams of fiber. For most adults, that’s far below the recommended daily intake. More positively, you would get certain vitamins and macronutrients “in spades,” such as carotenoids, which give pumpkin its yellow-orange color, along with vitamin A, Damman says. But others—vitamins C, B6 and B12, along with magnesium, potassium and zinc—would be severely lacking. You might also see drastic changes to your microbiome . “Your gut bacteria reflects the diversity of your diet,” Damman says. A diverse diet yields a more diverse microbiome, and a “monotonous” diet of pumpkin pie yields a more homogenous microbiome. Essentially, the bacteria that break down pumpkin pie would flourish, while others would diminish. “You’ll have a pumpkin pie microbiome,” he says. That could happen quickly: in a 2013 study published in Nature , for instance, researchers found that shifts in the human microbiome can occur in a matter of days . That could cause some gastrointestinal discomfort. “My guess is, as those microbes shift, you’re going to feel some GI side effects,” Damman says, such as bloating or diarrhea. All the excess sugar could also set off a blood sugar roller coaster. “Your body is going to have to pump out a lot of insulin to bring that blood sugar down,” Orandi says, possibly resulting in “rapid rises and falls” in blood sugar levels throughout the day. That tends to make people feel tired and generally “lousy,” he adds. On top of that, as the extra sugar is processed by your liver, it goes through a process called lipogenesis, Orandi says, and gets converted to fats that get deposited there. If you were to look at your body with a microscope, among the first changes you’d likely eventually notice on a pumpkin pie diet is extra fat in the liver, he says. In the long term, an all-pumpkin-pie diet could contribute to chronic disease , Damman adds. But, perhaps most obviously, after a week of eating nothing but pumpkin pie, you’d start to crave other foods, Orandi says. “I think the very first meal and maybe the second meal of pumpkin pie, you’ll be like, ‘Oh, this is great! This tastes so good! I’m happy,’” Orandi says. “And then with each subsequent meal, you’re going to get sick and tired of it.” Well, we’ll see about that.
