The world’s leading science city is testing AI and intelligent transport at scale, but linking data across its innovation hubs remains a challenge. By Hepeng Jia 0 Hepeng Jia Hepeng Jia is a freelance science writer based in Suzhou, China. Credit: Qilai Shen/Bloomberg via Getty Images In Yizhuang, a high-tech industrial zone in Beijing, drivers have access to parts of roads, highways and intersections where they might experience green lights all the way. Networks of road cameras and sensors feed data to a local control centre, which issues app notifications to drivers about upcoming interruptions, recommended driving speeds and lane suggestions to keep the traffic moving. On these ‘green-wave corridors’, the average number of traffic stops throughout the day has dropped by 40.6%, and the average travel speed has increased by 15.1%, according to local reports. Also known as the Beijing Economic-Technological Development Area, or Beijing E-Town, Yizhuang was created in the early 1990s by the Beijing Municipal People’s Government to act as the city’s leading smart-city demonstration zone. Alongside its intelligent transport systems, it has become a key area for autonomous-vehicle development and robotics, often showcasing emerging technologies at public events. Nature Index 2026 Science cities The annual Beijing E-Town Half-Marathon, for example, attracts hundreds of humanoid robots built by companies and university teams to compete with humans over an almost 22-kilometre track. In 2025, the inaugural year of the race, many robots failed to finish. But in this year’s race, held in April , the robots showed major improvements in speed, reliability and autonomy. Alongside Yizhuang, Beijing has many other smart-city ‘brains’ — hubs of innovation that are fast-tracking technological development in strategic areas such as artificial intelligence, digital infrastructure and data governance. One such area is Haidian in northwest Beijing, which runs a spatial-mapping platform that functions as a digital twin of the district. Gathering data from some 170,000 buildings, plus tens of thousands of road cameras and sensors, the platform flags local faults and automatically generates work orders to speed up repairs. The repair-response time for missing manhole covers, for instance, which is a major hazard for drivers and pedestrians, has been reduced from a whole day to just four hours. Haidian is home to Peking University and Tsinghua University — two of China’s leading academic institutions in the Nature Index — as well as the country’s greatest concentration of Chinese Academy of Sciences (CAS) institutes. Underpinning Beijing’s smart-city ambitions is its enormous investment in science and technology. Beijing has been the leading science city in the Nature Index since 2016 , and in 2025, its Share in the natural and health sciences was about two-thirds higher than that of its closest competitor, Shanghai. That year, Beijing spent around 6% of its gross domestic product on R&D — the highest share in the country. The city’s sprawling research system has formed close ties with its private AI sector, which, as of 2024, encompassed about 2,400 companies worth nearly 350 billion yuan (US$51.7 billion). A key challenge for Beijing is how to link all of the data being collected by its various districts and zones. Different departments might not feel incentivized to share their data or link up with central databases, says Yuan Zhibin, a senior research fellow at the CAS Institute of Science and Technology Strategy Consulting in Beijing. Strong competition between Beijing’s leading technology districts, such as Yizhuang and Haidian, as well as the costs involved in changing or updating systems, are major factors, says Yuan.
