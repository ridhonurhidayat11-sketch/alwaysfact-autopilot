Mission: Biomes About About Us Subscribe 🛜 RSS Contact Us Search 4 min read El Niño Rains Paint the Atacama Image of the Day for November 19, 2026 Winter precipitation helped wake dormant seeds in northern Chile, carpeting the desert with what may be the region’s most extensive flowering of the satellite era. NASA Earth Observatory Nov 19, 2026 Article View more Images of the Day: Nov 18, 2026 Instruments: Landsat 8 — OLI Landsat 9 — OLI Photograph Topics: El Nino Southern Oscillation Land Cover Vegetation September 14 October 24 NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison September 14 October 24 NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison NASA Earth Observatory/Michala Garrison September 14 October 24 Curtain Toggle 2-Up Parts of the Atacama Desert in northern Chile, normally one of the driest places on Earth, turned red-purple, green, and white in austral spring 2026 following an unusually wet winter. The rain woke seeds lying dormant in the soil, setting off what scientists say may be the most extensive desierto florido , or “flowering desert,” of the satellite era. The rains came as El Niño was strengthening in the tropical Pacific. During an El Niño, the subtropical Pacific high that normally keeps the region dry weakens, while a blocking high tends to form in the South Pacific near the tip of South America. Together, these shifts push the Southern Hemisphere storm track equatorward. Beginning in mid-July, a series of storm fronts fed by an atmospheric river brought one of the heaviest July rain events in decades to a long stretch of Chile, from the Atacama region south to Los Lagos, according to Chile’s national weather service (Dirección Meteorológica de Chile). In August, back-to-back winter storms blanketed parts of the Atacama Desert in rare snow . The spring blooms that followed are visible in these satellite image pairs, which show the desert at different stages of flowering on September 14 (left) and on October 24 (right). The OLI (Operational Land Imager) on Landsat 8 acquired the September images, and the OLI on Landsat 9 acquired the October images. The pair above shows a part of the desert about 30 kilometers (20 miles) southwest of Copiapó, near the eastern sector of Desierto Florido National Park. The red-purple areas prominent in the October 24 scene are vast blooms of pata de guanaco, most likely Cistanthe longiscapa or another Cistanthe species, according to Roberto Chávez, a remote sensing scientist at Pontificia Universidad Católica de Valparaíso.
