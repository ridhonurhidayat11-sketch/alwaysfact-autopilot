Lewis Pugh, who is a UN Patron of the Oceans, swam about 15km (9.3 miles) through the strategic waterway, a key route for oil shipments from the Middle East. Pugh, 56, who was born in Plymouth, Devon said "war causes immense human suffering" but nature is "another casualty that receives far too little attention". He is calling for an end to the fighting, the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz and action to address environmental damage caused by the conflict. Image source, Lewis Pugh Foundation Image caption, The Strait of Hormuz carries a significant share of global oil shipments from the Middle East The swimmer is believed to be the first person to attempt a long-distance swim in the Strait of Hormuz since the conflict began in March . He swam from Musandam Island in the strait to the Cape of Hormuz, at the northern tip of Oman's Musandam Peninsula. According to the Lewis Pugh Foundation, an oil tanker was struck by a projectile about 4km (2.5 miles) from where he was swimming on the first day. On the second day, he saw flames rising above a headland separating him from the burning liquefied petroleum gas carrier Tara Gas and later passed close to the vessel while it was still on fire. "My immediate thoughts were for the seafarers on board," Pugh said. "The flames were enormous. These vessels carry highly flammable gases, and an attack on one can have catastrophic consequences - not just for crew members, but also on the surrounding marine environment." Image source, Lewis Pugh Foundation Image caption, Pugh has completed long-distance swims in every ocean of the world Pugh said environmental damage caused by conflict often receives less attention than the human cost. "War causes immense human suffering. Lives are lost, families are torn apart, and homes, hospitals and schools can be destroyed in minutes," he said. "But there is another casualty that receives far too little attention: nature itself. And the damage can last long after the fighting has stopped." The Strait of Hormuz is one of the world's most important shipping routes and is also home to wildlife including dugongs, sea turtles and whale sharks. Pugh said oil spills and other contamination could have long-term consequences for marine ecosystems including coral reefs, seagrass meadows and mangrove forests.
