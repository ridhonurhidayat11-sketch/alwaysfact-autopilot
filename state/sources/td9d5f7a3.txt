Rich in combustible peatlands, Indonesian Borneo, known as Kalimantan, typically bears an outsize portion of the country’s fire activity. As fire seasons intensify due to climate change, communities and organizations in Kalimantan are seeking to shift traditional slash-and-burn farming to fire-free methods. Farmers who have adopted fire-free agriculture report benefits, but practitioners say more widespread adoption and government support is needed for it to scale up. See All Key Ideas In Kalimantan, the Indonesian portion of the island of Borneo, rain is bringing relief to the months-long fire season. Around 684,000 hectares (1.69 million acres) of land burned between January and September, amounting to 36% of the national total, despite the region comprising just 28% of the country’s land area. However, even with the rain, the threat has not completely passed. Satellite data from NASA show many fires continuing to burn in the forests of Kalimantan, while observers told Mongabay fire hotspots have shifted to other, drier areas. This year’s intense fire season has been fueled by the super El Niño weather pattern, which dramatically reduced seasonal rainfall across the country and prompted the dry season to last unusually long — even longer than during the catastrophic fire events of 2015 and 2019 . By September, some 12.5 million people in Indonesia had been impacted by fire or smoke, according to the Indonesian Ministry of Health. Katarina Rina, 53, is one of those affected. Rina, a farmer from Hulu Sungai subdistrict in the hills of the Gunung Naning protected forest in Ketapang district, West Kalimantan province, said the level of fire activity was unprecedented in her area. “We have had fires before, but they were quickly controlled, it has never been this bad,” she said in a video interview with Mongabay. “This time the fires spread into the forests and farmland. It was very traumatizing.” Rina told Mongabay her jengkol (dog fruit) garden was “completely burned,” which is a “big loss” as it was one of her family’s main sources of income. Rina added that while the fires have now finally been contained thanks to rainfall, residents are still suffering from smoke exposure. Motorcyclists ride shrouded in smoke from wildfires around Palangkaraya, Central Kalimantan, on August 28, 2026. Image by Wiki Mod 526 via Wikimedia Commons (Public domain). Fire-free agriculture solutions Many of Indonesia’s wildfires began as intentionally set burns to clear vegetation. Such slash-and-burn, or swidden, techniques are commonly used by small-scale farmers and industrial plantations alike to clear land for cultivation. Kalimantan’s vast peatlands, composed of carbon-rich, waterlogged soil, are highly combustible when drained, which is done primarily for industrial plantation crops such as oil palm and pulpwood. Once ignited, a peatland fire is particularly difficult to extinguish. Traditional slash-and-burn fire mitigation practices rely on farmers working together to prevent fires from spreading into surrounding areas. But in this El Niño-fueled extended dry season, and with climate change more broadly, these methods are no longer working, according to Anindya Hadi, director of community organization Gawi Bapakat, based in Seruyan district in Central Kalimantan province. “Now even farmers in upstream areas where there is mineral soil [which is less likely to burn than peat] are experiencing the same situation as fires in other parts of Kalimantan,” Hadi told Mongabay during a video interview. “They cannot really control the fires from spreading anymore.” But farmers say they can’t afford alternatives to slash-and-burn methods, even as the risks of wildfires intensify. “Farmers are actually aware that burning is not sustainable, but they also do not have the support they need to make the change, and they have to make a living,” Saro Halawa, 39, a farmer living near Rina’s village in Ketapang district, told Mongabay during a video interview. To address this gap in support, community-led programs have been developed in several regions of Kalimantan by NGOs in partnership with local governments. Rina is part of a farmers’ group that is receiving training and equipment to adopt climate-smart agriculture (CSA) methods via a program run by the NGO Planet Indonesia. One of the aims of the CSA program is to reduce the use of fire in small-scale agriculture. A farmer in Sintang, West Kalimantan, tends to crops grown through a climate-smart agriculture project. Image courtesy of Planet Indonesia. Through the program, farmers also learn how to plant fruit and native trees on fallow land no longer used for single-season crops, using agroforestry techniques that blur the line between farmland and forest. The objective is to help families get more from land that’s already in production so there’s less pressure to clear more forest, according to Planet Indonesia field officer Hanna Runtu. The trained farmers then become mentors, and demonstration plots are established to show incoming farmers how the model works, she said. “It’s difficult to ask farmers to change practices that have been passed down for generations without first showing them that the alternatives actually work,” Runtu told Mongabay over a video call. “It’s a gradual process, and the idea is to help farmers move away from burning step by step, as they gain confidence in these alternatives on their own land.” Halawa said the program has been helpful for him. “It takes some time to make the shift to farming in one place. But once the shift has been made, it’s easier because I no longer have to keep moving around [for new areas to cultivate],” Halawa told Mongabay. “But the practice is still new, and not all farmers around have adopted it.” Over in Central Kalimantan’s Seruyan district, one of the areas hit hardest by fire, a similar approach is being rolled out by community organization Gawi Bapakat, part of Indonesian NGO Kaleka. Debuted in Seruyan in 2024, Gawi Bapakat’s fire-free agriculture (FFA) program is focused on helping farmers in six Indigenous Dayak villages adopt farming techniques that do not rely on burning. Under the FFA program, farmers are provided with equipment such as hand tractors, along with seedlings and training in how to make alternative, organic fertilizer, according to Gawi Bapakat field staff member Alfiyan Amruddin. A farmer uses a hand tractor to prepare farmland as part of a climate-smart agriculture project. Image courtesy of Planet Indonesia. “The farmers normally use ash [from slash-and-burn techniques] as fertilizer, but we try to educate them that there are so many options for making their own organic fertilizer with no cost to help them to transition to fire-free agriculture,” Amruddin told Mongabay during a video call. One advantage of this approach is that farmers can have more than one planting cycle, whereas with slash-and-burn they can only plant once. “The fire-free agriculture method means they don’t have to keep opening forest to move to a new area, they can utilize the exact same place without using fire,” Hadi told Mongabay during a video interview. Yadi, 66, a Dayak farmer from the village of Tumbang Gugup, who is participating in Gawi Bapakat’s program, said the alternative fertilizers are superior to ash. “When the land is burned, the fertility doesn’t last long,” he told Mongabay via a video call. “When it isn’t burned, the soil stays fertile for a long time, especially with the organic fertilizer. That’s what I’ve experienced. The soil also doesn’t dry out quickly.” A single planting can produce up to four harvests, potentially providing a yield three times that of burned land, Yadi said. However, he added that productivity has been affected by this year’s drought conditions.
