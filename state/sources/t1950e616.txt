(Image credit: NOAA Satellites) Download Image The 2026 hurricane season, which officially ends November 30, will be remembered as a year of contrasts between the Atlantic and Pacific, a classic indication of a hurricane season influenced by a very strong El Nino. Below normal activity across the Atlantic basin was offset by above-average activity in the Pacific, including two disasters in Hawaii. “NOAA’s unparalleled forecasting skill and scientific expertise provided the early warnings needed to protect lives and property this hurricane season,” said Neil Jacobs, Ph.D., NOAA administrator. “This included accurately predicting this year’s very strong El Nino months in advance, which gave us the critical insight we needed to accurately forecast this season.” Atlantic basin The Atlantic basin produced 10 named storms , of which one (Isaias) became a hurricane. Isaias was also the season’s only major hurricane with top sustained winds of 120 mph. An average season has 14 named storms (winds of 39 mph or greater), seven hurricanes (winds of 74 mph or greater), and three major hurricanes (winds of 111 mph or greater). Tropical Storm Arthur kicked off the season, forming near the Texas coast on June 17, which is close to the average date for the first named storm of the Atlantic season to develop. Though it was a short-lived tropical storm, its remnants contributed to widespread heavy rain and flooding in the southeastern United States, along with numerous tornadoes and other severe weather. More than a month passed before the next named tropical storm, Tropical Storm Bertha, which formed on July 20 along the northern Gulf Coast. Bertha was a highly sheared tropical storm that brought gusty winds, rough surf and minor-to-moderate coastal flooding from Florida to Louisiana. (Image credit: NOAA NWS) Download Image The season’s most notable storm, Hurricane Isaias, made landfall on October 9 as a Category 2 hurricane near Destin, Florida, and was the first hurricane to make landfall in the mainland United States since Milton in October 2024. Isaias brought a significant storm surge to Panama City Beach, Florida, and damaging wind gusts over a large portion of the U.S. Southeast, in addition to flooding rain. “Despite being a relatively quiet season overall, this hurricane season was a clear example of the message we often say: it only takes one storm for it to be a bad season,” said Ken Graham, director, NOAA’s National Weather Service. Transcript Transcript Text Transcript The Atlantic and Eastern Pacific hurricane seasons officially end on November 30. This year, they highlighted the powerful influence of El Niño on tropical cyclone activity, producing a striking contrast between the two basins. While the Atlantic experienced a notably quieter-than-average season, the eastern Pacific saw an exceptionally active year, with numerous hurricanes and several storms reaching major hurricane strength. This contrast was in line with NOAA’s seasonal outlooks, which accounted for the influence of a very strong El Niño. El Niño is a natural phenomenon marked by warmer-than-average sea surface temperatures in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific and associated variations in winds and precipitation. This year’s El Niño event is among the strongest on record. El Niño can influence weather patterns around the world, including the conditions that help tropical cyclones form and strengthen. In the Atlantic, it typically increases upper-level wind shear, which can disrupt developing storms and make it harder for them to organize and intensify. In the eastern and central Pacific, El Niño generally creates more favorable conditions for tropical cyclone development. The 2026 Atlantic hurricane season was considerably quieter than average, with 10 named storms through November 24, compared with the long-term average of 14. Typically, seven of those storms would become hurricanes, including three major hurricanes (Category 3 or stronger). This year however, only one storm, Isaias, reached hurricane strength, which became a major Category 3 storm. Isaias made landfall as a Category 2 storm on the Florida Panhandle, near the city of Destin, bringing a significant storm surge to Panama City Beach, Florida, as well as flooding rain and damaging wind gusts across the southeastern U.S. Based on the preliminary data, Isaias’ formation on Oct. 7 marked the latest date for development of the first hurricane of the Atlantic season since the satellite era began in 1966. In contrast to the Atlantic, the Pacific experienced a remarkably active hurricane season. By November 24, the eastern Pacific had produced 20 named storms, compared with the long-term average of 15. Ten of those storms became hurricanes, including eight major hurricanes. Among the season's most powerful storms were Hurricanes Genevieve, Lowell, and Polo, all of which rapidly intensified into Category 5 hurricanes. Polo became the most intense hurricane of the season and had the second-lowest observed pressure in the eastern Pacific basin on record, behind Hurricane Patricia in 2015. Polo eventually made landfall in Baja California Sur as a Category 2 hurricane before crossing the Gulf of California and making a second landfall near Guaymas, Mexico. Numerous other storms impacted Mexico as well, and Hurricanes Polo, Rachel, and Simon carried surges of tropical moisture into the southwestern United States. The Central Pacific also experienced above-average activity. By November 24, eight named tropical cyclones were observed, including five that had moved into the basin from the eastern Pacific. Five of these storms were named and became hurricanes, with three reaching major hurricane strength (Category 3+). The average number of tropical cyclones in the central Pacific is four to five per season. Hurricanes Lala, Lowell, and Nolo brought direct impacts to Hawaii, including damaging winds, destructive coastal waves, and catastrophic flooding to parts of the state. Hurricane Nolo passed close enough to Hawaii to bring two rounds of flooding to the islands. Together, the central and eastern Pacific experienced their most active hurricane season on record, based on the Accumulated Cyclone Energy. Throughout the season, NOAA's satellites played an important role in monitoring these powerful storms. Geostationary satellites, including GOES East and GOES West, provided continuous views of developing storms across the Atlantic and Pacific, allowing forecasters to track their movement and changing structure. Meanwhile, NOAA's polar-orbiting satellites, including NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, collected detailed observations of storm structure, intensity, and surrounding environmental conditions. Together, these observations help forecasters monitor rapidly changing storms and provide critical information to protect lives and property. This year’s El Niño was among the strongest on record and played a key role in shaping the 2026 hurricane season. The Pacific Ocean saw an exceptional amount of hurricane activity.
