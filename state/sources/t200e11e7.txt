Got feedback? Take our survey . (See our AI policy here .) For the past few years, addiction specialist Roopa Sethi has been seeing a new set of patients. They show up at the University of Kansas Health System hospital in Kansas City with what looks like severe opiate withdrawal: nausea, vomiting and muscle aches. They’re extremely anxious and sometimes hallucinating. But they haven’t taken an opioid, like fentanyl or heroin. Instead, they’ve used something labeled as kratom, though it may not contain kratom at all. Kratom is a plant used for chronic pain relief and to help people stay off opioids. But souped-up new versions with hyperpotent derivatives of the active compound are sold online and in gas stations. The derivatives act strongly on the body’s opioid receptors and are often far more powerful than the opioid drugs they are sometimes intended to replace. Kratom products — sold as pills, liquid shots, edibles and more — are often mislabeled, and state and federal regulations lag far behind. A 2026 survey estimated that about 5 million Americans have used kratom or similar products, and the increased use has sent kratom-related exposures up 1,200 percent from only 258 exposures in 2015, according to the National Poison Data System. Now, newer versions of the substance can produce withdrawal symptoms like nausea and vomiting, which are often so severe they bring people to the hospital. Here’s what to know. How kratom works Kratom comes from the dried leaves of Mitragyna speciosa , a tree native to Southeast Asia. Dutch scientist Pieter Willem Korthals first described it in 1839. But “without a doubt, it was used likely for centuries prior to that” in countries including Thailand, Indonesia and Myanmar, says Oliver Grundmann, a pharmacologist at the University of Florida in Gainesville. “Wherever there is a stream, these trees are growing there.” People chewed the leaves for a stimulant effect and brewed them into a tea to treat pain,” Grundmann says. In the United States, kratom is billed pain treatment that is more “natural” than opiates like morphine, Sethi says. “When you take this product at low doses, it is used for waking up … but at higher doses it becomes opiate-like.” Others take it to treat withdrawal or cravings from opioid use. The leaf form contains more than 40 different naturally occurring chemicals called alkaloids that could have psychoactive effects. The most important is mitragynine, which makes up two-thirds of the plant’s alkaloid content. Mitragynine acts on the body’s mu opioid receptors. These receptors — found in the brain, spinal cord and gut — usually respond to natural peptide signals in the body such as endorphins, signaling cells to produce pain relief and release chemicals like dopamine. The receptors get their name from the most famous drug that binds to them: opium. Mitragynine is not derived from or related to opium or morphine but it binds partially to the receptor. “It’s kind of like a dimmer switch versus turning on the lights,” says Mallory Pullman, a clinical toxicologist at the Mississippi Poison Control Center at the University of Mississippi Medical Center in Jackson. “You don’t fully light your room.” Mitragynine also hits receptors that bind norepinephrine, serotonin and dopamine receptors, says Grundmann. The activity at adrenergic receptors creates a stimulant-like effect. “Now I think when we look at mitragynine, it’s primarily kind of this duality of the adrenergic and the opioid receptor activity,” he says, producing stimulating and depressive results in turn. As the kratom leaf is dried, Grundmann says, tiny amounts of mitragynine metabolize into another alkaloid, 7-hydroxymitragynine, or 7-OH. After someone takes kratom, the body also converts some mitragynine to 7-OH. The small chemical tweak changes the compound from one that binds partially to the mu opioid receptors to one that binds fully — like morphine or opium, Grundmann says. 7-OH binds 10 times more strongly to mu opioid receptors than mitragynine does. The resulting product is far more potent in its effects and addictive potential. When people use the leaves alone, the amount of 7-OH is extremely limited. But by the mid-2010s, scientists found products with more than 500 percent more 7-OH than in the plant alone, and now, consumers can buy products labeled as pure 7-OH. While 7-OH can be described as “natural” because it can, in theory, occur in nature, toxicologists like Pullman are also seeing synthetic derivatives that are even stronger, including one molecule named MGM-15. MGM-15 is sold as a “research chemical” and is four times more powerful than 7-OH. What is in kratom products may not always be what’s on the label, leaving users in the dark about what they are taking.
