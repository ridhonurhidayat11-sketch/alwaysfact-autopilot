The state has strategically stockpiled sandbags, pumps and generators . Debris has been fished out to clear storm drains, seepage blankets shore up aging levees and dams, and wire mesh cages hold back precariously placed boulders. What is expected to be the strongest El Niño on record is already pounding the state with giant waves. The fury of this year’s El Niño is being unleashed around the globe as well, from inundating Florida with floods to exacerbating drought in the Amazon. And much more is still to come , forecasts show, with the peak of the climate phenomenon expected to arrive between November 2026 and January 2027. Californians know all too well the threat. El Niño has historically brought relentless rainfall to the state, flooding cities and triggering landslides. People prepare sand defenses along the coast of Seal Beach, Calif., on November 15. Large waves, driven by the 2026–2027 El Niño, began pounding the California coastline in October. APU GOMES/AFP/Getty Images State officials learned a lot about preparedness and emergency water management from previous El Niños — particularly the strong event that spanned the winter of 1997–1998. But this year’s event is likely to be even more challenging, a “Super” El Niño superimposed on the backdrop of global climate change. Climate change has made water management in California more complicated than it was just 30 years ago — as it has elsewhere around the world, says environmental scientist Letitia Grenier, the director of water policy at the Public Policy Institute of California, a nonprofit, nonpartisan think tank based in San Francisco. In 1997, California’s overall climate was much drier; El Niño brought a big, long rainy season that spanned the state, Grenier says. But now, “it’s just so humid. The air is warmer, so it can hold more water.” That waterlogged atmosphere means that even without El Niño, there’s been less of a distinct rainy season in California, and more rapid flip-flop between intense episodes of rain dumped on the ground followed by periods of heat and dryness. “It’s these strange, erratic patterns that we’ve never seen before,” she says. And the state is bracing for how the Super El Niño will clash with this new climate normal. A climate event becomes a household name 1997 was the year that the term “El Niño” shot to global fame. It was the most powerful El Niño in recorded history, and the first to be scientifically monitored in real time, continuously tracked by satellites and breathlessly reported on by news outlets. El Niño’s rise to household name status was even cemented in pop culture by an appearance on Saturday Night Live , in a classic sketch by comedian Chris Farley. But the event was no laughing matter. It caused global devastation during its brief reign. El Niño events form as westward-blowing trade winds over the eastern tropical Pacific Ocean weaken, allowing warm water to accumulate at the surface. Those waters can then release massive amounts of heat to the atmosphere, altering the position of the Pacific jet stream and helping to disrupt global weather patterns. In 1997–1998, global temperatures spiked by 1.5 degrees Celsius on average. Along with the torrential rains in California, the event brought brutal drought to Southeast Asia and deadly summer floods to central Europe and Peru. Record-breaking floods also hit East Africa, which in turn triggered a surge in mosquito-borne Rift Valley fever. The oceans were so warm that 16 percent of the world’s reefs died. In all, the El Niño–related weather extremes were linked to more than 22,000 fatalities around the world, including nearly 200 in the United States, and an estimated $5.7 trillion in global economic losses. Not every El Niño is equally intense, or impacts weather in quite the same way. “There’s a lot of variability event to event,” says Washington, D.C.–based climate scientist Tom Di Liberto, of the nonprofit climate news organization Climate Central. But there is a general overall pattern to how El Niño tends to affect weather around the planet, Di Liberto says. For example, there tend to be more tropical cyclones in the Pacific Ocean, and fewer in the Atlantic — a pattern also observed in 2026. El Niño also tends to flatten and accelerate the Pacific jet stream, shifting its flow southward, says Mike Anderson, California’s state climatologist for the Department of Water Resources, who is based in Sacramento. And that has historically meant that a conveyor belt of back-to-back moisture-loaded storms is set on a collision course with the U.S. West Coast.
