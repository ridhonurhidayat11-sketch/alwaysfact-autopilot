Now, conservationist Reshu Bashyal is leading an effort to protect them. 25 November 2026 Words by Saugat Bolakhe and photos by CJ Karki A couple of hours drive from Kathmandu, in the hills near Chitlang village in central Nepal, a group of locals and forest officials gather in a dense subtropical woodland. Beneath a canopy of pines, Himalayan chestnuts and Nepalese alders, the group listens as wildlife conservationist Reshu Bashyal guides them through a questionnaire designed to document the signs of poaching. Bashyal refers to this team as a taskforce — one on the hunt for illegal activity. But instead of focusing on animals, as many conservation projects do, the group’s objective is to protect flora, specifically orchids. These flowering plants are harvested and sold across Nepal’s borders. A series of three photos of Reshu Bashyal walking through the forest inspecting trees for wild orchids., image The flowers are prized for their ornamental value, but they are also used in traditional medicines, including Chinese, Tibetan and Ayurveda healing products. Multiple parts of the plant, including the stem and fruit, can also be used as ingredients. “It’s as if these plants have some magical properties about them,” Bashyal says. Nepal has more than 500 orchid species, many of which are heavily harvested. Between 2008 and 2016, the country legally exported more than 47,000 kilograms of wild-collected orchids. However, discrepancies exist between Nepal’s export data and those of its trading partners, arising from issues such as mismatched and unreported trade amounts and species being sold under different names. The trade in animal and plant species is regulated mostly under the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) agreement, which is designed to protect wildlife from exploitation. Although Nepal joined the convention more than 50 years ago, it did not introduce legislation to implement CITES until 2017. :first-child" direct-filter="true"> The orchid Dendrobium longicornu is sought after for its black-haired canes and fragrant white and orange blooms. All but two species of orchid in Nepal are now listed in CITES Appendix II, which includes species that are not currently threatened with extinction but could be if their trade is not regulated. The government must create a management plan and a formal assessment to confirm that harvesting a species for trade won’t harm the survival of the wild population. “We haven’t prepared such a management plan for any orchid species yet. So, their trade is still considered illegal here,” Bashyal adds. Violation of the CITES Act can lead to a fine of up to 100,000 Nepalese rupees (US$660), one year in prison, or both. Click to enlarge media. Bashyal first became involved in orchid research by chance. In 2018, while working on a conifer tree conservation project for non-profit organization Greenhood Nepal in Kathmandu, she visited several regional sites across the country and was often approached by people asking about the demand for orchids in the city. When her team investigated further, it realized that many people living in the villages were harvesting or actively trading the plants. This illegal trading was often overlooked by authorities, according to Bashyal. Before suggesting a way to stop it, the team had to understand the market — specifically which species were being taken, their conservation status and how the supply chain operated. To do this, it visited orchid trading hubs across Nepal and spoke to harvesters, traders and enforcement officials. During these visits, the team shared its research and spoke about the importance of conservation, which was well received, she says. “After all, an unsustainable harvesting of the orchids severely threatens local livelihood and income,” she adds. Bashyal’s team at Greenhood Nepal learnt that several orchid species, including Satyrium nepalense and Pleione praecox , are harvested in large quantities for their medicinal value. Overharvesting an orchid species that is in decline can easily lead to its extinction in the wild, which makes understanding a species’ population and distribution crucial for creating sustainable harvesting practices. A series of three photos of the instruments used to record GPS coordinates, measurements and observations of floral parts of wild orchids., image After the field trips, Bashyal’s team created a series of policy briefs, drafted conservation frameworks and started the anti-poaching taskforce in collaboration with the region’s governmental forest management office to document illegal activity and conduct patrols. The team is also developing comprehensive identification manuals and guidelines for regulators. Many existing manuals focus on identifying whole plants and flowers, but traded items can include the orchid’s thickened stem and fleshy underground storage organs, called tubers. Some orchids are boiled, or dried for several hours or days before being traded. In some parts of Nepal, communities can earn a good income from harvesting and selling orchids alongside other medicinal plants. For many, medicinal plants are their sole source of income. “Simply telling the locals ‘you are not supposed to do this’ will not stop them from harvesting,” Bashyal says. For this reason, imposing a blanket ban on trade might not be effective. Bashyal emphasizes that tracking the wildlife trade is complex and often requires navigating delicate legal territory. Researchers must also have patience when interviewing informants. “Thus, it also comes down to following research ethics, respect and treating local knowledge as a valuable resource to build trust,” Bashyal says. “Only after that, can we have data and develop any intervention.” :first-child" direct-filter="true"> The forests of Makawanpur support an array of native orchids.
