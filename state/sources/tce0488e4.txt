It was an unprecedented year for El Niño, the weather-changing ocean pattern defined by warmer-than-normal surface waters in the central and eastern equatorial Pacific. By October 18, the ocean area that researchers use to monitor El Niño’s severity climbed to an average temperature of over 30° Celsius (86° Fahrenheit) for the first time in history. From there, it kept on rising. How hot will it get? In September, the region’s daily average sea surface temperature reached the most extreme level ever recorded: more than 3.03 degrees Celsius above the 30-year average (dashed line). The World Meteorological Organization predicts that sea surface temperatures may peak at 3.7 degrees above that average by January. The two previous strongest El Niños on record emerged in 1997 and 2015. Sign up for our newsletter We summarize the week's scientific breakthroughs every Thursday. All that hot water has fueled the most active Pacific hurricane season on record. By mid-October, 331 units of cyclone energy had accumulated, beating out 2018’s record of 318 units. It has also unleashed heat into the atmosphere, affecting distant circulation patterns and leading to an extraordinarily quiet Atlantic hurricane season. 2026’s El Niño will be felt well beyond the New Year. Precipitation and drought impacts are expected across the world through spring 2027. And the global heating effect could compound with human-caused climate warming to make 2027 the hottest year ever recorded. Far-reaching climate impacts The heat that El Niño unleashes into the atmosphere shifts storm tracks and weather patterns globally. Here are some of the most significant observed and anticipated effects. Region Impact Pacific Ocean Enhanced hurricanes; El Niño may have made Hurricane Polo, the eastern Pacific’s second strongest tempest on record, about 14 times more likely. Atlantic Ocean Dampened hurricanes; the 2026 season’s first cyclone formed October 8, the latest arrival for a first storm in 121 years. Southeast Asia Widespread drought that threatens agriculture and raises wildfire risk. North America Higher-than-average temperatures in western Canada and the northern United States; enhanced precipitation in the U.S. South and Southwest. Central and South America Drought conditions so extreme that Panama, El Salvador and Honduras have declared national emergencies. East Africa Enhanced rainy season and increased flood risk into early 2027. Earth overall Increased average temperatures could lead to 451,000 additional heat-related deaths by February.
