Can we save this ‘pale blue dot’? Kim Heacox Since the spacecraft’s 1977 launch, our wonderful and terrible species has done great damage to our planet. Voyager has offered us a clear view of ourselves Wed 18 Nov 2026 06.00 EST Last modified on Thu 19 Nov 2026 07.03 EST Share Imagine traveling nonstop for 49 years, alone in the dark with no destination in sight, powered by curiosity and engineering. Further imagine making that journey not at bullet train or jet plane speed, but much faster: at 37,800 miles per hour, free from friction in a vast sea of cold, soundless space, bombarded by radiation and cosmic rays yet still able to communicate with folks back home. Today, 18 November, that intrepid traveler – Voyager 1 , a robotic space probe the size of a Mini Cooper – is exactly one light day away from us: 16.1bn miles. No other human-made object has ever gone this far. Radio signals from Earth (traveling at the speed of light, 186,000 miles per second ) now take 24 hours to reach it, and another 24 hours to return. The return signals are so weak that scientists at Nasa’s Deep Space Network must use a massive satellite dish to hear them. Nasa launched Voyager 1 in early September 1977, just weeks after the first Star Wars movie hit theaters with Luke Skywalker and his charming sidekick droid, R2D2. It took photos of the planets Jupiter and Saturn , then continued onward. In 2012 it exited the sun’s protective shield of charged particles and magnetic fields, crossed a boundary called the heliopause , and entered interstellar space. That’s when it became a message in a bottle. View image in fullscreen Hundreds of visitors watch images of Saturn being relayed from Voyager 1, at the Smithsonian Institution’s National Air and Space museum in Washington DC, on 13 November 1980. Photograph: Len Hawley/Consolidated News Pictures via Getty Images That message consists of a 12in, gold-plated copper disk engraved with information to showcase human culture and the diversity of life on Earth, should any advanced civilization discover it. The disk contains photos and greetings in 55 human languages, music by Mozart and Chuck Berry, the sound of a baby crying and a whale singing. It also has a time stamp and a map that orients the position of our solar system to 14 pulsars – dense remnants of massive stars – that rotate rapidly and emit beams of radiation like flashing cosmic lighthouses. The message says, in essence: this is where we live and who we are. We thrive on connection. Come see us sometime. The astronomer Carl Sagan chaired a committee that designed the disk. In the late 1980s, as Voyager 1 hastened through the outer solar system, he tried to convince Nasa to turn the probe’s sensitive cameras back toward home. Nasa resisted, concerned about the risk of instrument damage. Finally, on Valentine’s Day 1990, the cameras turned. The result was one of the most profound images ever captured in space exploration. Sagan called it the “ pale blue dot ”, wherein Earth appears as a fraction of a pixel, “like a mote of dust caught in a sunbeam”. At that moment, Voyager 1 began to probe us , forcing us, from a great distance and with great irony, to take a close look at ourselves. “From this vantage point,” Sagan said, “the Earth might not seem of any particular interest. But for us, it’s home … On it everyone you love, everyone you know, everyone you ever heard of, every human being who ever was, lived out their lives.” View image in fullscreen Carl Sagan filming Cosmos. Photograph: Science History Images/Alamy On this small planet and against all odds, molecules composed themselves into organic matter that over billions of years evolved into complex living organisms. Geologic epochs came and went. Continents moved. Species thrived and perished, with one, only very recently, standing upright and coming to dominate all others. That’s us: a remarkable biped capable of love, compassion and war; of making music, felling forests, creating literature, burning oil, building probes and exploring space. Scientists today say humans have “gravely altered” the chemistry, biology and physical structure of Earth’s land and water , with much of that damage occurring since Voyager 1 left us. We (or some among us) have destroyed countless habitats and damaged ecosystems to where wildlife populations are now collapsing worldwide. Building a global economy on fossil fuels has so warmed and toxified our planet that few places – if any – are pristine any more . “Dear Future Generations,” the author Kurt Vonnegut said shortly before he died in 2007. “Please accept our apologies. We were rolling drunk on petroleum.” It wasn’t always this way. For most of human history, long before we stared at our computer screens and cellphones, we sat around campfires and studied the night sky. We found patterns in the stars, named them, and told stories. 17:20 Nasa’s Voyager 1 just reached a massive milestone in space - video Much later, we invented science and turned our rational minds toward a better understanding of ourselves and everything around us. It is science today that tells us we are made of star stuff; that every element in us was forged in the fiery furnaces of stars. “When we look out into space,” says the professor of astronomy, Brian Cox , “we are looking into our own origins, because we are truly children of the stars.” When astronauts orbit Earth (many of them for months on end), they see no flags or national boundaries or competing interests. Instead, they see a single home shared by billions: spaceship Earth, a blue world wreathed in clouds and surrounded by a cold, sterile darkness. One of those astronauts, Ron Garan , concluded that if humanity intends to flourish far into the future, it must shift its priorities from economy-first to planet-first, followed by society, then economy. Without clean air, fresh water, stable ecosystems and a suitable climate, society and the economy mean nothing. They collapse. View image in fullscreen The Voyager control center at Jet Propulsion Laboratory, at the California Institute of Technology in Pasadena, California, in 1980. Photograph: NASA/Getty Images If we get things right and survive our technological adolescence, thousands of future generations will enjoy this still beautiful, bountiful planet: a world of suitable temperatures and robust (though rapidly diminishing) biodiversity. Much damage has been done, mostly by ignorance and greed. Global warming is accelerating , and hard challenges lie ahead. But this crisis and others can be mitigated if we set our hearts and minds to it; if we learn to live together and strive to belong, not to dominate. Sometime soon, probably in the 2030s, Voyager 1 will lose all power and go silent. But it will sail on, carried by inertia, headed not for Proxima Centauri , the nearest star to us after our sun, but toward the constellation Ophiuchus . In roughly 17,600 years, it will be one light year from Earth .
