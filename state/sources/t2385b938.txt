See All Key Ideas LONDON — On Nov. 10, Mongabay went to the site of the annual meeting of the body that regulates fishing in the northeastern Atlantic, where a little-known geopolitical dispute over marine resources was playing out behind closed doors. The North-East Atlantic Fisheries Commission (NEAFC), a multilateral body, governs the fishing of three major stocks of fish in international waters near Europe, as well as associated species and habitats. Disagreement between countries over how to manage the stocks has had serious consequences, experts say. All three species — Atlantic mackerel ( Scomber scombrus ), Atlanto-Scandian herring ( Clupea harengus ), and blue whiting ( Micromesistius poutassou ) — have been subject to overfishing for years. The Northeast Atlantic mackerel population is in particularly bad shape, currently 22% below the NEAFC’s target threshold, having been fished above scientifically advised levels every year since 2008. The NEAFC is one of the world’s 17 regional fisheries management organizations (RFMOs), and a fisheries governance campaigner has called it the most “fraught” and “problematic.” It has six member parties: the European Union, the United Kingdom, Iceland, Norway, Russia, and Denmark (on behalf of the Faroe Islands and Greenland; the delegation is commonly called DFG). The three fish species are prey for marine mammals and seabirds, and are sought after on global markets. Mackerel is sold in tins, herring is eaten pickled or smoked, and blue whiting is used for aquaculture feed. At this year’s annual meeting, held Nov. 10-13 in London, the parties made no breakthrough on how to sustainably divide the catch for the three stocks between member parties. Discussions about alleged illegal fishing by Russian vessels occupied much of the event. The parties did adopt a statement committing the NEAFC to develop an ecosystem approach to fisheries management, earning praise from NGO observers who said this could have positive impacts in the long term. Jonny Hughes, senior policy manager at U.K.-based NGO Blue Marine Foundation, told Mongabay that managing the three stocks was the NEAFC’s “raison d’être,” and that the lack of coordinated action to keep catches within scientific recommendations was therefore a major failure. “The biggest problem remains that it is seemingly institutionally incapable of doing the primary part of its job,” Hughes said. “These are countries that consistently position themselves as global leaders on conservation, particularly marine conservation,” he added. “When push comes to shove, when it comes to fishing, all of those commitments disappear.” The map shows eight general RFMOs that cover a range of stocks, including the North-East Atlantic Fisheries Commission (NEAFC). Coastal complications For all the organizational drama, the NEAFC’s annual meetings can feel anticlimactic: Some of the most important stock decisions occur just outside the NEAFC’s remit, in what are known as “coastal states” meetings. The coastal states meetings, which involve mostly the same parties as the NEAFC, determine total catch across the Northeast Atlantic ranges of the three stocks, which are worth billions of dollars. (One back-of-the-envelope estimate from Pew Charitable Trusts, a U.S.-based NGO, put their collective annual dockside value at roughly 3.5 billion euros , or $3.84 billion, with most of that coming from mackerel.) These meetings are, in effect, a series of side deals to the NEAFC. All three stocks straddle the borders between waters under national jurisdiction and the high seas, which is the NEAFC’s regulatory area. In 2025, about one-third of the Northeast Atlantic mackerel catch was in the NEAFC’s area, and the rest of was in national waters. There are coastal states groups for each of the three fish stocks. For mackerel, the parties are the same as for the NEAFC, except the Faroe Islands and Greenland participate separately and Russia has no seat at the table. (For herring, Russia is in and the EU and Greenland are basically out.) Norway and the U.K. consistently catch the most Northeast Atlantic mackerel, together accounting for almost half of the catch (a different stock of the same species is found near the United States and Canada). NGO observers have for years sharply criticized NEAFC members for their mismanagement of the three stocks, citing the coastal states meetings as a major cause. The observers are generally conservation groups that monitor and advise RFMOs like the NEAFC. They say the separate coastal states talks inhibit transparent, coordinated management. Few, if any, other RFMOs in the world have such side-dealing arrangements that shape core organizational decisions. Daniel Steadman, international fisheries officer at Pew, said the arrangement was “unique.” “You have an agreement whose scope is effectively the entire Northeast Atlantic, and it’s a multilateral treaty, and it has defined signatories, and it has a treaty that’s deposited in a host nation, and it has a secretariat,” he said. “But then in parallel to it, you have this forum where exactly the same parties in a slightly different configuration meet to make the big, substantial decisions that really the RFMO seemingly should make.” In October, the coastal states groups met at the NEAFC headquarters in London. Each agreed on a total allowable catch for 2027 that was in line with scientific advice from the International Council for the Exploration of the Sea (ICES), which is the case for most years. For mackerel, this was 352,938 metric tons . The problem, say NGO observers, is that year after year, the parties don’t collectively adhere to the total allowable catch — they can’t agree on how to share it. “They are not able to actually really share … to really divide the cake,” Jean-Christophe Vandevelde, a senior manager at Pew, told Mongabay. “Which is the most important thing.” Steadman wrote following the latest coastal states meetings that “each state will return home, set its own share and the sum of those results will exceed the TAC [total allowable catch] they set.” On Sept. 30, ICES forecast that the parties will collectively exceed the scientific advice for mackerel catch in 2026 by 128%, a gap Steadman described as “ staggering .” Hughes said he suspects parties are reluctant to reduce their catches because they expect any eventual allocation deal to be based on historical catch.
