Wilson rounds up the best science-fiction novels of the year. But one stood out as her absolute favourite By Emily H. Wilson 25 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Sci-fi columnist Emily H. Wilson rounds up her top reads of the year If I had to sum up 2026 in science fiction, I would say it’s been heavy on time travel and journeys between parallel worlds. It’s also had lots of upbeat, feel-good stories, but was a bit light on attack ships exploding off the shoulder of Orion. What I mean by that last remark (a quote from the first Blade Runner movie, of course) is that we have been somewhat lacking in old-fashioned, quite serious sci-fi, with big spaceships and galactically high stakes. However, that is a crude summary of a year that offered many and various delights in this very broad category of literature, which I interpret as including anything about the climate, anything “speculative” and anything set in the future. On the aforementioned time-travel front, I enjoyed The Traveler by Joseph Eckert, in which a man starts jumping ahead into the future… and every time he does it, he goes twice as far. If nothing else, it’s a lesson in how big numbers get, fast, if they keep doubling. I also liked Time to Burn , by Ellery Lloyd, a twisty thriller with trips back to the Blitz. However, my favourite was As We Fall Through Time , by Claire North, about an experienced time traveller trying to save his lover from history and badly messing up history in the process (except it’s not so simple). Advertisement Free newsletter Sign up to Book Club Sign up to newsletter When it comes to journeys between parallel worlds, my pick of the bunch would be Exit Party by bestselling author Emily St. John Mandel, which begins with a party where there are too many people. And not because the invite went out too widely… It’s the story of parallel worlds in wildly different political states and the people who travel between them. I also really enjoyed Sublimation by Isabel J. Kim, about a world where people keep getting split into two. Not quite a parallel-worlds book, I know, but certainly a parallel-souls book. On the more “cosy” sci-fi front, I appreciated the comic stylings of Moss’d in Space by Rebecca Thorne, a story about some (intelligent) moss in space, as well as Adrian Tchaikovsky’s raccoon hero in Green City Wars . But my top pick would be A Wall is Also a Road by New Scientist columnist Annalee Newitz, the story of a single-celled, galaxy-travelling scientist called Gardenpath. I’m not sure Newitz’s story fits neatly into “cosy”, but then books don’t always fit neatly into genre boxes. No one could call M. John Harrison’s novel hopeful, and I find I do like some hope now and again On that note (of non-box-shaped books), I was blown away by The End of Everything by M. John Harrison, a story about an alien invasion (sort of), which won the Booker prize. I thought it was brilliant; not everyone I forced it upon agreed, however. Another category-defying (and potentially opinion-dividing) book this year was Adam Roberts’s new novel Frankenstein Rex , the story of a world ruled by a real monster. Not an easy, traditional read by any means (also with some ghastly torture early on), but I find everything Roberts writes to be interesting. On the more traditional, outer-space-set sci-fi front, there was Ann Leckie ‘s Radiant Star , a quiet but beautifully rendered addition to her Radch space sequence. I would also highlight Richard Swan’s very assured The Infinite State , a smart and enjoyable tale about a woman trying to buy her own planet in order to escape a tyranny. Finally, I liked Garth Nix’s Massif , with a wonderful premise involving sentient mountain ranges that travel between the stars. It opens, very pleasingly to me, with a whole chapter of exposition. I love books that defy the supposed “rules of writing” and get away with it triumphantly. If I had to pick a book of the year? When I first read it, I would have said it was definitely going to be Harrison’s very other-worldly alien story (and the Booker judges certainly would have agreed with me). But no one could call it a book that makes you feel very hopeful, and I find I do like some hope now and again. So, if I had to choose a book of the year, I would go for one with some hope, and that would be When There Are Wolves Again , by E. J. Swift, a bold story of ecological collapse but also reclamation in our near future. Winner of this year’s Arthur C. Clarke award for science fiction, it is a wonderful piece of work. Emily H.
