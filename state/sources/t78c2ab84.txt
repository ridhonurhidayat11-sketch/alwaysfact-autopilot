Preference Eos in your searches! Go to Google Since 2021, the Mars Perseverance rover has been roaming Jezero Crater, gathering details about the Red Planet’s geology and hints about its past. On its journey, the rover took close-up images of the Séítah formation, a nearly 4-billion-year-old layer of rock at the base of the crater. A new study published in Communications Earth and Environment posits an explanation for how this rock layer formed, giving researchers a better glimpse into Mars’s history and geology and even the extent to which it could have hosted ancient life. The results are also encouraging to scientists attempting to model planetary histories using information from Earth, said Lionel Wilson , a planetary scientist at Lancaster University in the United Kingdom who was not involved in the new study. The research “suggests that the long-term evolution of the mantles of Earth and Mars are not enormously different,” he said. This newsletter rocks. Sign up now An Olivine Enigma Previous research indicated that the Séítah formation was made of an olivine-rich type of rock called komatiite. On early Earth, komatiites formed from runny, low-viscosity lavas that flowed “almost like water,” forming lava lakes, said Adrian Brown , a planetary scientist at Plancius Research and lead author of the new paper. But Mars has a cooler interior that would, theoretically, make the formation of olivine more difficult. In other words, the discovery of large grains of olivine on Mars was unusual. Brown and the research team wanted to test just how similar the Séítah formation was to komatiites found on Earth. One key element they focused on was viscosity—how easy it was for the lava to flow across the ground. Lavas with high viscosity form rock features that emerge from the landscape, such as a classic, cone-shaped volcano , while lavas with low viscosity form flatter, wider layers of rock. “That’s what we were trying to test: Where does the viscosity lie in this range?” Brown said. “Viscosity is something that hasn’t been explored so much, because we haven’t had as many geochemistry measurements as we do now.” One 2008 model of how magma moves on Earth related geochemistry data to lava flow viscosity. Brown and the researchers applied this model to geochemistry data from Perseverance to see whether they could narrow down the viscosity of the lavas that created the Séítah formation. The geochemical data came from the Planetary Instrument for X-ray Lithochemistry (PIXL), an X-ray spectrometer on board Perseverance that can identify the chemical elements present in rock and soil. The geochemistry of the Séítah formation pointed to a low-viscosity flow along the surface of Mars. Additional images from PIXL showed that the large grains of olivine in the formation were actually interlocking crystals. On Earth, in places such as lava lakes found in Hawaii, interlocking crystals form as a lava flow cools over hundreds or thousands of years. It’s likely that the Séítah formation was created as a low-viscosity lava lake cooled over a similarly long time period, Brown said. “What [the authors] suggest seems to be the right explanation for how [Mars] accumulated the rocks that we see” in the Séítah formation, Wilson said. Magma on Mars One large obstacle in modeling the histories of other planets is that scientists aren’t sure whether all the rocky planets in our solar system were constructed with the same proportions of elements. Some research indicates that Mars’s mantle is similar to Earth’s, which this new research supports. “What we’re seeing here is a very strong hint that at least for the Earth and Mars,” the evolution of their mantles was “very similar,” Wilson said. “We’re seeing the same trends.” Having data on the viscosity of certain lavas also tells scientists how far a lava flow can actually travel and, therefore, where it might have come from. One interesting problem for planetary scientists studying Mars is how to explain some of the very long lava flows that are present there, Wilson said. Viscosity can be a factor, but so can the eruption rate, which relies on how subsurface magma is stored. The new research shows that in the case of the Séítah formation, viscosity was responsible: Geochemistry data and modeling suggest the low-viscosity flow traveled about 200 kilometers (124 miles) from its magma vent. “I see this [research] as throwing light on the bigger picture of what the overall history of volcanic activity is, not just at the surface,” Wilson said. Other possible explanations for how the Séítah formation emerged hypothesize that the lava was an intrusion: a result of rising molten rock from Mars’s interior that crystallized within preexisting layers of rock. If that were true, Brown said, it would have required the rock to be uplifted and then exposed, which would mean that Mars would need active plate tectonics. Current scientific evidence suggests that plate tectonics are not active on Mars. “If it was an intrusion, that would change a lot of our concepts of things on Mars,” Brown said. Knowing the geological history of Mars can also indicate how warm the planet’s interior was billions of years ago. Low-viscosity lavas would have required Mars’s interior to be quite warm, which may have made the planet more hospitable to life, Brown said. —Grace van Deelen ( @gvd.bsky.social ), Staff Writer This news article is included in our ENGAGE resource for educators seeking science news for their classroom lessons.
