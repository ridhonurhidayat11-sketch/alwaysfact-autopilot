McDermott, along with other researchers studying male psyches, was tired of the field’s long-standing assertion that the manlier the man, the worse his outcomes. It was high time to measure masculinity in a positive light. For almost half a century, the many social science scales that measure gender or masculinity have consistently shown that rigid fealty to Western masculine norms — such as prioritizing work, power-seeking and status — link with poor mental and physical health. Endorsement of such norms also correlates with misogynistic beliefs and violent behaviors, including sexual violence, studies have shown. Nor does that negative focus help men thrive. But what approach would help? “We don’t really have a definition of men’s health. If we don’t know what we are going towards, how can we even do this [research]?” says Matt Englar-Carlson, a counseling expert at California State University, Fullerton. What traits, the researchers wondered, define a healthy man? And could a healthy reframing of manhood improve men’s outcomes, such as by getting them to the doctor or lowering their high rates of social isolation, depression and even death by suicide? Those questions would cleave the field, though as fights go, this one has been downright nice. McDermott, a psychologist of masculinity at the University of South Alabama in Mobile, would ultimately question positive masculinity’s very existence. Meanwhile, his friends and collaborators at Movember — a nonprofit research organization based in Richmond, Australia that encourages mustache-growing in November to spark conversations around men’s health issues — are poised for a 2027 launch of the Healthy Manhood Index. That scale uses a positive framing and is designed to gauge men’s strengths, while illuminating areas for improvement. Masculinity is treated as a barrier to health The first gender measurement scale emerged almost a century ago. It positioned femininity and masculinity at opposite poles, with researchers assuming that the most gender-conforming men and women were the healthiest. That thinking has since unraveled. As women began smashing gender barriers, by doing everything from donning pants to entering the workforce , penalties for women acting more masculine largely disappeared. Men are less likely to visit a doctor than women. Masculinity researchers are trying to empower men to see help-seeking as a sign of masculine health. geogphotos/Alamy Inspired by the feminist movement, researchers began studying the masculine psyche. Yet they largely framed masculinity as a barrier to health. The Male Role Norms Inventory, one of the most popular masculinity scales in use today, exemplifies that focus. Individuals rank statements like: “A man must stand on his own two feet and never depend on other people to help him do things.” The higher the score on that and more than 60 similar statements appearing in popular masculinity scales used over the past 50 or so years, the worse men’s outcomes , McDermott and his colleagues at Movember reported in July in Men and Masculinities . “Manlier” men often struggle with depression and substance use and engage in sexual aggression. They also tend to avoid asking for help or taking precautionary health measures, such as wearing a mask during the COVID-19 pandemic. But almost 80 percent of the statements used frame masculinity as a problem to be solved, the team found. That deficit lens biases the findings, argues coauthor and social psychologist Peter Fisher of the Movember Institute of Men’s Health, the organization’s research arm. “If the only tools you have available to you are looking for deficits, you will find deficits,” says Fisher, who is based in Santa Monica, Calif. Sponsor Message Researchers pivot to studying positive masculinity That negative framing has also seeped into public discourse, so much so that some people now intuitively link “masculinity” with the word “toxic.” Yet those most macho of men are the outliers , the small percentage who score highest on masculinity scales, Englar-Carlson says. He suspects that what really matters for health is flexibility around when to behave in certain ways. Stoicism, a typically masculine-coded trait, can be calming in emergencies but scary when a man experiences a mental health crisis and refuses help. Englar-Carlson and psychologist Mark Kiselica, of Penn State University, Harrisburg, developed the Positive Psychology Positive Masculinity , or PPPM, paradigm in 2010.
