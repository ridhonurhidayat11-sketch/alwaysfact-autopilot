Giant El Niño, historic droughts and declining rainfall across the region are turning vital waterways into impassable barriers of mud. “You know you’ll face rain, sun, thorns, mud,” nurse Alexandra Fernandes said of the grueling medical missions that serve as a vital lifeline for isolated riverside residents. See All Key Ideas RIO BRANCO, Brazil — The sun rises as the riverine healthcare crew starts disembarking medical supplies. They pass down boxes and coolers of all sizes from boat to canoe to smaller canoe, from the far end of the ship to the water’s edge, where a staff member with mud halfway up their shin will hand the load to the next on dry land to carry it uphill where the rest of the team is setting up today’s medical outpost. Every year, doctors, nurses, dentists and community health agents sail along Riozinho do Rôla, delivering medical care to the riverine population of the rural area of Rio Branco, capital of Acre state, in the Brazilian Amazon. Barro Alto, where the fleet is docked, is the first stop. Across the Amazon, a landscape of forests and rivers poses unique obstacles for accessing healthcare. “There are technical and operational challenges that are characteristic to this region,” nurse Alexandra Fernandes, who oversaw this year’s edition of Rio Branco’s healthcare flotilla, told Mongabay. In riverine communities like Barro Alto, isolated for months during wintertime floods, waterbound medical attention becomes a lifeboat. But with climate change and increasingly extreme drought seasons, channels that once connected people to the care they needed are now turning into barriers, as water gives way to mud. According to the November bulletin of the Drought and Immobility Risk in the Amazon Basin report, river levels across the Brazilian Amazon are “below or far below average.” In Acre alone, six of its 22 municipalities have more than 20 communities under the threat of drought-induced immobility. For more than 20 years, crews of up to 70 professionals set sail from Rio Branco and made their way through 11 outreach sites , offering thousands of medical procedures to people from roughly 150 communities. The scene from Barro Alto plays itself out at each of the stops. Personnel, medicines and gear meet existing structures — schools, churches, barns — that will house a “pop-up clinic” for the day. Once the work is over, it all goes back on the boats, and they set forth to the next site to start all over again. Flotilla staff disembark medical supplies and carry them uphill to set up the medical outpost at the first stop in Barro Alto. Image by Amanda Magnani for Mongabay. In other parts of the Amazon, medical attention arrives in the shape of floating primary healthcare units , large boats equipped with hospital-grade facilities, eliminating the need to set up shop on dry land. But Riozinho do Rôla and its tributaries are neither wide nor deep enough to harbor those units. Instead, Rio Branco’s initiative employs a fleet of five wooden boats of roughly 16 by 6 meters (52.5 by 19.7 feet): One for the crew, one for medical supplies, one for larger equipment, one that houses a kitchen and, at a safe distance, one carrying fuel. From the third stop on, even those boats get too large for streams that narrow stretch by stretch, so the crew must resort to a handful of motorized canoes known as voadeiras . Once everything is loaded, they sail along a path that, for the most part, a smaller team has cleared ahead with a chainsaw. Planning logistics for such a detailed enterprise takes months. “From the water we’ll drink over that time, to the fuel we’ll need, to motors and tools to unblock creeks, everything must be calculated,” sanitarist and public healthcare specialist Rejane Almeida told Mongabay. She’s been a part of the flotilla for 13 years, nine of which as its coordinator. But all planning in the world isn’t enough to dodge climate change’s curveballs. Stranded between too little and too much water By the time João Batista joined the flotilla as a pilot, he had already been working in the region as an endemic disease control agent, the “malaria guard,” for more than a decade. Back then, dry and wet seasons were more predictable, and “there was more water,” he told Mongabay. Standing by the shore, he points at a wooden fence some 50 m (164 ft) ahead, at the foot of a hill. That’s where, in previous years, they used to tie up the boats. “We wouldn’t have been able to stay where we are standing now,” he said. The Amazon is growing drier with every passing day. In Brazil alone, the forest has lost 14.5% of its water surfaces in two decades. It has also seen a 74% decline in rainfall across 35 years. The 2023 record-breaking drought that threw the region into a humanitarian and environmental emergency was only surpassed by the one in 2024 . This year, drought and fire outbreaks are sparking fears of another dry spell in the rainforest, this time fueled by a giant El Niño . In 2024, rivers were so low that Almeida recalls drastic schedule delays: Stretches they used to cross in two hours now took an entire day. The flotilla didn’t reach the 11th stop. As the community saw water levels drop even further, they warned the crew there would be no way through. “We made it as far as we could,” Almeida said. At the same time, some of the worst floods in the Amazon’s recorded history happened in the past decade. In 2024, the Acre River overflowed in Rio Branco, reaching its highest level since 2015’s historic flood. There was so much damage that the flotilla had to be canceled, as the healthcare crew was needed for emergency response. Batista says the abundance of water that causes havoc in the city makes it easier for the fleet to reach its destinations. But even then, it doesn’t always mean improved access. In previous years, river levels were so high, people had to swim from their home communities to the medical outpost. “There are families who lost their children in a strong stream,” Almeida said. So when the choice is between the one chance of seeing a doctor, doing a medical procedure or getting a vaccine and risking the life of a loved one, many choose to stay home. “And I won’t argue with them.” João Batista points at a wooden fence where, in previous years, the flotilla used to tie up the boats. The river mission that changed healthcare in the Amazon On muddy riverbanks, people huddle close to the water, some with children on their shoulders, phones held up and ready. Soon, videos and photos will appear in WhatsApp groups announcing: The health mission is passing by. A few weeks before, Almeida visited all 11 stops to check access and facilities and to confirm dates with community leaders. “Communication has always been important to get the word out,” she said. “Even after 21 years, many people still don’t know this program exists.” Deysi Nadi, 37, a seamstress, left home around 9 a.m. with her 5-year-old daughter and three other relatives on an all-terrain vehicle (ATV) and took a ferry. The trip lasted about an hour. “You can see how the whole community turns out,” she said. “People think: ‘It’s only today. Let’s drop everything and take care of our health.’” People come to receive assistance however land and river allow: By canoe and motorboat, on horseback or by ox cart, by motorcycle and ATV.
