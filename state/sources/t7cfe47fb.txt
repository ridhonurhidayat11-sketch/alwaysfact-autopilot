Manage your subscription Your newsletters Subscriber benefits Sign out Subscribe now Mind Toddlers form complex memories but may then lose access to them Few of us can remember events from our earliest years, but that might be because we lose the ability to recall memories - not because we didn't make them in the first place By Sascha Brodsky 24 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Young children can make and retain complex memories Anastasiya Amraeva/Westend61/Getty Images We can form complicated memories in our earliest years, even though most of us ultimately lose the ability to recall them, a study of almost 400 toddlers suggests. The research throws new light on infantile amnesia, which leaves most adults unable to remember events that occurred when they were under the age of 3 or 4. It builds on a 2025 study showing that infants do, in fact, have the capacity to form specific memories before infantile amnesia kicks in. Tristan Yates at Columbia University in New York, who worked on that study, says the new experiment shows that early infant memory includes the ability to recall complex information, linking the location of an object to its surroundings. Markus Werkle-Bergner at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development in Berlin and his colleagues tested 388 toddlers, aged 18 to 24 months at the time the research began, using a game of hide-and-seek. The children entered a room with four boxes, its walls transformed by projected images into either a jungle or an underwater world. The children then returned a day later – and again after delays of one, three or six months – and were asked each time to remember where Corky was hiding. Some of the children retained a full memory of the hide-and-seek game a day after they played. They remembered that Corky’s location changed depending on the surrounding scene – either underwater or jungle – and went to the hiding place that matched the scene in question. Other children had a partial memory. They muddled up the two hiding places, approaching Corky’s underwater hiding place in the jungle scene, or vice versa. Some children seemed to retain no memory of the game at all, and simply chose a box in which Corky had never appeared. However, 45 per cent showed no reliable memory of the game after the months-long interval. Age and language development did little to explain the differences, according to the researchers. “What remains unclear is why the toddlers differed so greatly,” says Werkle-Bergner. A possible explanation is that memory is tied to how well children learned the game in the first place. For example, the children who couldn’t remember anything about Corky’s location a day after playing may not have understood the rules of the game in the first place. But among the children who could remember the toy’s location a day later, and so did understand the game, this understanding didn’t predict which of them would remember Corky’s location months later. Among children who correctly recalled Corky’s location, a distinctive pattern of activity appeared in their brains just before they did so. This pattern became even clearer in the brains of children who could successfully recall Corky’s location after a delay of several months. The researchers say this suggests that later forgetting might reflect difficulty retrieving a memory rather than simply a failure to store it in the first place. Paul Frankland at the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto says this idea fits with findings he and his colleagues made about a decade ago during experiments in mice , in which apparently forgotten memories could sometimes be restored by activating neurons involved in their formation. What is a ‘normal’ memory slowdown, and when should I worry? Lapses in memory are a normal part of ageing but can also be signs of dementia. Here’s how to distinguish between typical brain ageing and cognitive decline However, the EEG readings in the current work cannot establish beyond doubt that infant memories remain intact in the brain even after they can no longer be recalled, Frankland cautions. The experiment also cannot show whether memories formed in infancy survive in an inaccessible form into adulthood, says Robyn Fivush at Emory University, Georgia. Language, social development and a growing sense of self all influence what people remember, she says. Frankland thinks a more direct test might reveal more. “Ideally, we would want to identify the neural representation of a memory when it is first formed and then determine whether that same representation can be reactivated after the memory has been forgotten,” he says.
