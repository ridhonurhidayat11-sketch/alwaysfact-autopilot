Sign Up A change in how the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention collects vaccination data during flu season could lead to worse outcomes for patients and hamstring the work of primary care and emergency doctors in combating the disease. In a now unavailable but archived version of a page on the CDC website, the agency said collection of influenza , COVID and RSV (respiratory syncytial virus) vaccination data for the 2026–2027 season began on October 1 and would be released on a monthly basis. That’s a stark contrast to previous years, when CDC data on respiratory illness vaccinations were made available in September or October, says Katelyn Jetelina, an epidemiologist at Yale University. Because of the change, the CDC’s latest statistics did not come out until last week, well into flu season. Family physicians and other clinicians rely on frequent, up-to-date information to keep an eye on respiratory viruses, know what is circulating in the community and recommend vaccines to patients and their families at the right times. The more current the data, the better we can protect our communities.” Local health departments also use the data to pinpoint at-risk populations that have not received their shots and conduct outreach operations, says Neha Pathak, chief physician editor of health and lifestyle medicine at WebMD. But that is made more difficult by having less up-to-the-minute data. The reason for the change from weekly to monthly was to "reduce administrative burdens, improve operational efficiency, and allow additional time for CDC and jurisdictions to collect, review, and validate data submissions," a CDC spokesperson told Scientific American , in a statement. "CDC recognizes the importance of timely and accurate vaccination data, particularly during respiratory season, and will continue evaluating reporting practices to ensure they meet public health needs," they added. But over the past year, the CDC has struggled to provide current data on an assortment of vaccines. In February an audit revealed the agency had not updated 38 routinely maintained databases, most of which were related to vaccinations, since at least the spring of 2025. The CDC, which is overseen by Secretary of Health and Human Services Robert F. Kennedy, Jr., a notorious vaccine skeptic, also did not hold its usual flu and COVID shot campaign this year—leading top medical organizations to launch their own campaign as a proxy. The data lag ultimately makes physicians’ jobs harder at an already challenging time of year, when emergency rooms and primary and urgent care offices are slammed with people with respiratory ailments, Pathak says. Physicians use the data to estimate how big of a surge in patients with flulike symptoms to expect—that can help ensure adequate staffing and protections for patients, she says. “That gives us a chance to prepare and think about what kind of staffing levels we might need,” Pathak says. “That’s particularly important in emergency rooms in different regions, so we really need that region-specific information.” Rights & Permissions Adam Kovac is a breaking news reporter at Scientific American .
