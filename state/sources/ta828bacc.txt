The measure is symbolic, but it calls into question the international leadership Colombia had built in a region approaching a tipping point in forest transformation. The Colombian Amazon alone contains 14 million hectares (34.6 million acres) of oil and gas blocks. Experts consulted by Mongabay warn that the extent of the Colombian Amazon’s loss of protection will also depend on the Colombian government’s recognition of Indigenous Territorial Entities — with which it has held no dialogue. The Colombian Ministry of Environment has defended the move, saying 75% of the Amazon remains under protection schemes and that the repealed resolution was merely an administrative act that created “economic and territorial uncertainty.” See All Key Ideas Colombia’s government, led by far-right President Abelardo De la Espriella , has sought to distance itself from the environmental policies of his 2022-2026 predecessor, left-wing Gustavo Petro, including those affecting the Amazon. The new administration in Bogotá recently repealed Resolution 0961 , which prohibited large-scale mining and hydrocarbon extraction in this region, a move that experts say weakens protections not only for the biome but also for the Indigenous peoples who sustain it. The Ministry of Environment argues that the resolution was repealed because it was enacted without prior consultation with the region’s Afro-Colombian, Raizal and Palenquero traditional communities, and the measure does not, by itself, guarantee the Amazon biome’s protection. However, experts consulted by Mongabay say that, beyond repealing a measure — which was not the only one protecting the Amazon — the decision is also a political gesture that casts doubt on Colombia’s hard-won leadership in environmental protection. Organizations say that despite this leadership role, fossil fuel extraction and its infrastructure have accelerated forest loss in the Colombian Amazon, paving the way for other industries such as logging, mining and agriculture. A 2025 report by Earth Insight, the International Institute for Sustainable Development (IISD) and the National Organization of Indigenous Peoples of the Colombian Amazon (OPIAC) warns that 14 million hectares (34.6 million acres) of oil and gas blocks cover 28% of the Colombian Amazon — with 8 million hectares (19.8 million acres) overlapping intact forests. The Amazon is approaching a tipping point in forest transformation, while the Colombian region alone contains 14 million hectares (34.6 million acres) of oil and gas blocks. Image courtesy of Earth Insight/International Institute for Sustainable Development (IISD)/National Organization of Indigenous Peoples of the Colombian Amazon (OPIAC). A symbolic measure that raises alarms for the Amazon The resolution the government has recently repealed ordered the protection of more than 48 million hectares (118.6 million acres) of the Amazon from new large-scale mining and fossil fuel extraction. Although incumbent authorities argue it was an “administrative act” that created “economic and territorial uncertainty,” civil society organizations say it was an important complementary measure, marking the first time a country had moved to declare the Amazon biome a zone free of extractive industries. Raphael Hoetmer, director of Amazon Watch’s Western Amazon Program, says the repeal can be seen as an endorsement of efforts to advance oil and mining projects in the region. “The measure was probably not entirely effective as a protection, and neither is the repeal, but politically it was extremely important, and the repeal is as well,” the researcher says. Colombian Environment Minister Fabio Arjona has defended the repeal of Resolution 0961, saying it was merely a confusing administrative act and that 75% of the Amazon is already protected. Image courtesy of Fabio Arjona. Colombian Environment Minister Fabio Arjona said in a video shared on social media in November 2026 that the Amazon protection did not begin with the resolution issued by the administration of former President Petro, arguing that a large percentage of the Amazon is covered by protection measures for lying within Indigenous territories and national parks. He also said he had designated 25 areas of the country as Other Effective Area-Based Conservation Measures (OECMs). “We have more than 75% of the Amazon under protection, also including reserves established under the Second Law. Protection did not begin with this rushed resolution that ‘magically,’ on top of all the existing protections, imposes restrictions across the entire Amazon,” Arjona said. The minister dismissed the possibility that the repeal would lead to the granting of mining licenses or titles in the Amazon. “It simply repeals the previous resolution, because it was handled poorly and failed to take into account consultation with the Amazon’s Afro-descendant communities . A partial repeal is not legally permitted; it has a fundamental flaw,” he insisted. Mongabay contacted Colombia’s Ministry of Environment for further comment on the government’s position regarding its decision on mining in the Amazon , but the agency declined to discuss the issue. Hoetmer, however, warns that the measure comes amid mounting extractive pressure in the Colombian Amazon, especially in the Putumayo region (bordering Ecuador to the south and Peru to the southeast), which contains more than 70% of production blocks and more than 250 active wells, according to the organizations’ 2025 report. “We have to leave the oil underground; we cannot continue to damage the Amazon when it is close to the tipping point of transforming from a tropical rainforest into a dry savanna. This message from the Colombian government conveys an intention not to consider any of this,” the expert warns. Chart courtesy of Earth Insight/International Institute for Sustainable Development (IISD)/National Organization of Indigenous Peoples of the Colombian Amazon (OPIAC). Juan David Varela, coordinator of the socio-legal laboratory for public-sector innovation at the Gaia Amazonas Foundation, says a series of measures can still safeguard the Amazon and strengthen Indigenous governance. However, he says the recent government decision eliminates a resolution that complemented existing environmental protections. “Repealing this resolution does begin to weaken that entire protection framework, but it is important to say that what is written on paper is one thing and what happens in reality is another. Even if legal hydrocarbon and mining activities are excluded, this was not a safeguard against the illegal activities taking place in the Amazon either,” the expert says. Varela recalls that the protections had also been insufficient to address illegal mining and emphasizes that the repeal simultaneously struck down a resolution that resulted from dialogue with Indigenous communities. Earth Insight, an organization that develops tools to halt the expansion of fossil fuel and mining industries in key ecosystems and Indigenous communities, says repealing the resolution on the grounds that “Afro-Colombian communities were not consulted” is contradictory, even as the government pushes changes to the rules governing this process . “It is contradictory that one week before overturning Resolution [0961] on those grounds [the lack of prior consultation], the government announced changes to the consultation regulations,” the organization told Mongabay.
