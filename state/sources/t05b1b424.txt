Celsius Pictor for Quanta Magazine By Jay Bennett Contributing Writer November 25, 2026 View PDF/Print Mode astrophysics axions dark matter experimental physics physics All topics For the most part, we are blind to the universe. Almost a century ago — in 1933, to be exact — Fritz Zwicky’s studies of distant galaxies led him to suggest that “dark matter is present in very much greater density than luminous matter.” Observations by the astronomer Vera Rubin would later substantiate the case for the intangible, leading her to write in 1980 that “the conclusion is inescapable that non-luminous matter exists.” The elusive stuff appears to react with ordinary matter only through gravity, allowing scientists to estimate how much of it there is. Beyond that, we know very little. That’s not for lack of trying. Elaborate detectors across the globe have failed to catch any glimpse of dark matter. If you also consider dark energy , which accelerates the expansion of the cosmos, “more than 90% of the matter-energy contents of the universe are unknown,” said Atsushi Taruya , an astrophysicist at Kyoto University in Japan. “That’s a bit embarrassing.” As null results pile up, scientists are expanding the search, particularly for the tiniest candidates, called ultralight dark matter. Some of the more inventive experiments involve clouds of atoms in quantum superpositions, minuscule levitating magnets, nuclear atomic clocks, and Earth’s geomagnetic field itself. One of these approaches may yield a clue to a whole new realm of physics. Problem Solver Ultralight dark matter wasn’t always a popular target. For decades, the leading candidate was a “weakly interacting massive particle,” or WIMP, but tests have steadily ruled out WIMPs with different characteristics. In September 2026, researchers working on an experiment called Lux-Zeplin said they had detected a mysterious particle collision in an underground vat of liquid xenon. The cause could have been a WIMP, but many scientists remain skeptical because the collision was so energetic — if WIMPs can have that much energy, several lower-energy collisions should have been detected already. As with other compelling apparent WIMP detections, the peculiar signal may have been a false alarm caused by rare interactions with background particles or a flaw in the data analysis. For years now, WIMP searches have been giving way to the hunt for lighter particles — particles so wispy that they would behave more like immense waves flowing through space. For many scientists, the most appealing ultralight dark matter candidate is the axion. Named in the 1970s for a laundry detergent, this hypothetical particle would clean up a major problem in physics. The problem concerns the neutron, which is made up of fundamental particles called quarks with opposing electrical charges. According to theories governing the strong nuclear force — the force that holds together atomic nuclei — these charges should be slightly offset. But precision measurements find them almost perfectly aligned, a mystery known as the strong CP problem. A hypothetical field of axions would pull the neutron’s internal charges into the observed alignment. But searching for axions is a painstaking process. If they account for all or most dark matter, theories suggest that a single axion should be about a trillionth to a billionth of the mass of an electron. So far, physicists have explored only a tiny fraction of these possibilities. It’s possible that … there’s a whole other world out there that is not, at the moment, visible to us. Reina Maruyama, Yale University One axion-hunting tool is a haloscope, a hollow device with conductive metal walls placed inside a strong magnetic field. If axions from space fly through the magnetic field inside this cavity, they should convert to photons, producing an electromagnetic signal that the experiment would amplify. The challenge is that, for every possible axion mass, you need a different size detector. For the lighter axions, the cavity needs to have a diameter about that of a beer keg. For heavier axions, you need a smaller cavity. Haloscopes such as the Axion Dark Matter Experiment at the University of Washington have thoroughly searched much of the lighter range without finding anything. At Yale University, the physicist Reina Maruyama and her colleagues are developing a haloscope filled with an array of copper rods that alter the experiment’s electromagnetic field. The new setup will let them search for higher-mass axions. Other possible designs split the cavity into segments, either with thin conductive walls or with honeycomb-shaped metal tubes. These modified haloscopes should allow scientists to slowly test unexplored segments of the axion’s mass range. But to search for particles outside this range, researchers need entirely new setups. Dark Worlds While the axion could solve the strong CP problem, it may not account for all dark matter. In fact, many physicists argue that we shouldn’t assume dark matter is just one particle. Visible matter, after all, is a particulate soup of many ingredients. “The world that we normally interact with, it comprises all sorts of different particles,” Maruyama said. “It’s possible that … there’s a whole other world out there that is not, at the moment, visible to us.” Some physicists believe dark matter could consist of dozens or hundreds of axionlike particles. The ALPS II experiment at DESY is formally known as a “light shining through a wall” experiment. © DESY/Heiner Müller-Elsner To search for them, researchers are currently upgrading a 300-meter-long apparatus in Germany known as a “light shining through a wall” experiment. The setup runs a high-powered laser through a magnetic field. The idea is to convert photons to axionlike particles, which would then pass through a barrier that blocks light. On the other side, a second magnetic field would convert the particles back to photons. “The goal would be to open up the window to the dark sector of nature,” said Axel Lindner , a physicist who works on the experiment at the German Electron Synchrotron (DESY). Another approach is to use Earth’s magnetic field as a detector. A group in Japan including Taruya recently analyzed 10 years of data from a magnetometer in Scotland, searching for possible axionlike particles in our planet’s ionosphere. They found 65 electromagnetic signals that match the type of signal expected from ultralight dark matter. But to conclude that a signal is from dark matter, scientists would need data from all around the globe, because Earth’s magnetic field varies at different locations. Taruya’s team is planning to analyze additional data taken in Japan, where the signal from any axionlike particles is expected to be different. With time, perhaps scientists will be able to read the signs of dark matter in the sky.
