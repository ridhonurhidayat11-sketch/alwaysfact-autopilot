Sri Lanka is constrained by the lack of technology such as an operational Doppler weather radar system and hence cannot release accurate forecasts in time, according to weather officials. Despite being a RAMSAR-accredited wetland city, the water retention capacity of most wetlands in Colombo have declined due to urbanization, poor waste disposal and spread of invasive species. Economists suggest creating a dedicated financial reserve akin to an adaptation and resilience fund to enable local authorities to build resilient infrastructure. See All Key Ideas COLOMBO — On Nov. 6, a 129-millimeter (5-inch) downpour flooded parts of Sri Lanka’s commercial capital within minutes, stranding commuters and submerging a large number of vehicles. Siva Sivasagunthan, a three-wheeler driver working at a local ride-hailing app, told Mongabay that the water levels quickly increased and his vehicle was partially submerged in water. It took him around two hours to reach a location that would have usually taken 30 minutes to reach on a normal day. The downpour left many Colombo residents panicking. According to Ajith Wijemanna, director of Sri Lanka’s Department of Meteorology , there have been a few instances over the decades when Colombo has experienced rainfall exceeding 400 mm (15.7 in). In June 1992, the city experienced a rainfall of 493.7 mm (19.4 in). In November 2010, a 440.2 mm (17.3 in) rainfall was recorded in 24 hours. Wijemanna told Mongabay that the intensity of the rainfall — the speed of rainfall received in an hour — is extremely important. According to meteorological department readings, the rainfall intensity on Nov. 6 was more than 120 mm (4.7 in) per hour. The rain gauge reading at another location in Colombo indicated more than 135 mm (5.3 in) of rainfall per hour. “We believe that the main cause of flooding was the high intensity during a short period of time, such as an hour, which was similar to a gushing downpour,” Wijemanna said. He noted that Sri Lanka currently lacked technology such as a Doppler radar system to support better forecasts with good lead time. Once the system is made fully operational, the agency will have the capacity to issue forecasts and predict rainfall with a lead time. Pressure on an ancient drainage system The flash flood occurred soon after Sri Lanka’s prime minister Harini Amarasuriya instructed officials in local authorities, including the department of irrigation and divisional secretariats, to clean canals and drainage systems in Colombo to ensure proper flow of rainwater. Following the flash flood, several authorities deployed workers to clean drains and canals and identified 60 key locations with blocked drains and waterways obstructing the flow of flood water. Dark clouds hovering over Colombo is now a cause for worry for some urban dwellers. Image by Thuwan Mansoor. According to Deepika Wijethilake, deputy director engineering of drainage design at the Colombo Municipal Council ( CMC ), the sewer and storm water systems in Colombo are about a century old and have been designed to function separately. She told Mongabay that the storm water system has been designed to withhold a capacity of 30 mm (1.18 in) of rainfall per hour. “But on Nov. 6, there was a rainfall exceeding 100 mm [3.9 in] per hour. Since it rained heavily, there was extensive pressure on the system and it took some time for the water to recede,” she said, adding that the construction of more pavements also means less ability for rainwater absorption. Real estate boom impacting wetlands Besides grey infrastructure, Colombo’s wetlands also play a role in flood retention and mitigation. However, due to various development projects, Colombo’s wetlands are now under threat. A 2025 study by the Colombo-based nonprofit Centre for a Smart Future ( CSF ) looked at the emerging environmental pressures from the booming real estate development in Colombo’s suburbs, particularly around the Thalangama environment protection area, a picturesque Colombo suburb. According to the study, wetlands, rice fields and peri-urban landscapes are being positioned as exclusive residential enclaves and marketed as ‘green living.’ The study showed that the desirable features of the wetlands were being highlighted by property developers to increase demand. Interestingly, it also referred to areas such as Kaduwela, Malabe and Thalawathugoda — areas considered residential and all situated around wetlands — having witnessed an annual spike in property prices by about 30-40%. According to CSF co-founder and director Anushka Wijesinha, these property developers are doing very little to ensure what they market. “This is a very sensitive wetland complex. They are able to leverage on the wetland to create demand and price premia, but hardly ensure that their projects are nature-positive, which is problematic. The ecological value of the wetland is not reflected in the prices that are being charged or the fees paid to local councils or environmental authorities,” he told Mongabay. Clogged drains have become a burden for urban authorities in Sri Lanka. Image courtesy of the Ministry of Transport, Highways and Urban Development. Wijesinha said that most people see this green space idea narrowly and only from an aesthetic point of view. “But beyond that, it is a fundamental element of the city that makes the city resilient to various climate impacts. Over 45% of the country’s GDP is generated by the Western Province, of which Colombo is a major part. Then there’s a huge amount of logistics and international connectivity. There are supply chain risks when the city floods. Rather than concentrating on just the aesthetics, we need to look at it from a point of resilience, where these issues are a risk to the economic competitiveness of the city,” he said. He highlighted the importance of assessing the economic worth of a wetland. Citing a 2003 study of the Muthurajawela wetland, a coastal peat bog near Colombo, Wijesinha said how this wetland complex provides certain ecosystem services such as flood control. “Without these services, the country would have to spend billions to put up [human]-made solutions,” he said. Improving wetland connectivity Colombo lies in a low-lying area downstream of the Kelani river basin and is a RAMSAR -accredited wetland city. Chethika Gunasiri, an environmental scientist who focuses on urban wetlands and sustainability, noted that colonial cities are located in coastal or flood plain areas where floods are a natural phenomenon. “To mitigate these disasters, flood plain areas have natural infrastructure called wetlands. Their main function is to absorb rainwater, temporarily hold it and slowly release this water to the drainage system. Part of this water also goes through the soil and recharges the groundwater table,” she said. The Colombo Wetland Complex (CWC) comprises several urban and municipal council areas and not only the Colombo Municipal Council area. According to Gunasiri, 37% of rainwater that comes into Colombo is retained by this wetland complex. “But we now see how these wetlands are being filled and developed. The drainage system is not sufficient to manage high volumes of rainfall. There is no natural vegetation to retain or temporarily hold the water coming into the city,” she said.
