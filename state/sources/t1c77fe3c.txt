Between Nov. 13 and 16, nearly 800 attendees from more than 65 countries convened in the Scottish capital, Edinburgh, for the eighth International Marine Conservation Congress (IMCC8). The conference is led by the Society for Conservation Biology (SCB), a U.S.-based international professional organization focused on the science and practice of conserving biodiversity. This year’s event brought together marine researchers, conservation practitioners, policymakers, journalists and others to discuss issues such as illegal fishing, sustainable fishing practices, marine protected areas (MPAs), community-led conservation efforts and deep-sea mining. Some presentations focused on solutions to reducing bycatch and illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing, while others discussed the successful implementations of MPAs. There were also sessions about ocean stewardship opportunities, how to use data to inform conservation and restoration efforts and ways to navigate science and policy in understanding deep-sea mining developments. Nearly 800 attendees from more than 65 countries convened in Edinburgh, Scotland, for IMCC8. Image courtesy of Cat Kutz/SCB Marine. Kathryn Matthews, chief scientist at the U.S.-headquartered ocean advocacy NGO Oceana, who was a plenary speaker at IMCC8, described the biennial event as a “congress unlike any other” due to the diversity of its attendees and the range of disciplines discussed at each conference. “IMCC is where the rubber meets the road — field researchers in the same room as government analysts and civil society representatives,” Matthews told Mongabay. “We need more spaces like this for mutual learning and building those relationships for the future. Meeting people face to face also reminds us to bring our humanity to these sometimes really difficult conversations.” Shaili Johri, a conservation biologist and molecular geneticist at Stanford University in the U.S., who has served as president of SCB since 2023 and chaired IMCC8’s organizing committee, said one of the most pressing issues facing the ocean conservation sector is translating scientific knowledge and innovation into conservation action. “One thing that we realized is we have a lot of data, we have a lot of tools in innovation, but when it comes to implementation of these and actually getting things resolved on the ground, there’s a huge barrier,” Johri told Mongabay. Johri said the congress sought to address these gaps through its programming, which included plenaries, research and project presentations, workshops and reflection sessions. IMCC8 also featured an Indigenous-led initiative that focused on solutions, Indigenous knowledge systems, and the integration of art and storytelling into conservation practice. Attendees in a session at IMCC8. Image courtesy of Cat Kutz/SCB Marine. While many parts of the IMCC8 program were designed to encourage open dialogue and reflection, some attendees pointed out that many voices were missing from these conversations, including those of policymakers, fishers, conservation practitioners and researchers who were financially or logistically unable to attend the conference. According to the event organizers, only 17% of IMCC8’s delegates came from less-industrialized nations, while the remaining 83% came from industrialized nations. This was due, in part, to visa difficulties faced by delegates seeking to travel to the U.K., organizers said. “This is hugely problematic and I think it’s really a reflection on the location we’re having these meetings,” Asha de Vos, a marine biologist, ocean educator and blue whale researcher from Sri Lanka, told Mongabay. De Vos said one of the most positive things about IMCC8 was its interdisciplinary approach. But she warned that without more participation from those in the less-industrialized world, the conference could reinforce the power imbalances that conservation efforts should be working to address. “When you have less of us in the room to speak up, when you have less of us representing the actual amazing work that’s going on … then we’re basically creating space for parachuting, and that’s a huge problem,” De Vos said. She was referring to “parachute science,” in which scientists from industralized countries conduct research in less-industrialized countries without meaningfully involving local researchers or sharing data with them. Another issue discussed at IMCC8 was declining financial support for both scientific research and conservation action. “What we are seeing is that funding across the board is going down,” Johri said. Matthews said funding for ocean advocacy, in particular, had become more limited following sweeping U.S. government cuts, including the 2025 dismantling of the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) , a formerly independent foreign assistance agency that funded non-governmental organizations, universities and numerous other sectors. “Some heroic and dedicated philanthropists, seeing the giant budget holes left behind in the wake of USAID’s elimination and other cuts to science, have stepped in to keep some of the most urgent capacity development and research going,” Matthews said. “But that can mean shifting money away from campaign work.” Matthews added that the “stagnation” of campaigning capacity has resulted in scientists “shouldering more of the public engagement than ever.” “That’s certainly not a solution to the funding challenge, but we need to recognize this reality and look for ways to support our scientist advocate colleagues,” Matthews said. “Everything from making policy engagement more valued during tenure reviews to expanding eligible expenses under grants to include public outreach.” Beyond increasing funding, IMCC8 delegates also called for funders to provide longer-term support for scientists to sustain data collection and for conservation groups to sustain projects over the long time scales needed for success. You can never collect enough data to come up with the best possible management responses in a year … it takes years for us to gather enough information to say, ‘Here’s the problem and here’s how we can solve it.’” Alongside discussions of the challenges facing ocean conservation, IMCC8 offered a space for delegates to share solutions, celebrate successes and embrace “ocean optimism,” according to David Shiffman, an interdisciplinary ocean conservation scientist based in Washington, D.C. and former IMCC co-chair.
