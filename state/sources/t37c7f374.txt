The excitement persisted despite a communication anomaly that cropped up on the probe, which the team remains confident is temporary. Voyager team members gathered for a press conference about the mission at the Von Karman auditorium here at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL) — the first such Voyager event since 1989, when Voyager 1's twin, Voyager 2 , flew past Neptune. The gathering served to celebrate Voyager 1 's distance milestone: At 5:16 a.m. EST (1016 GMT) on Wednesday (Nov. 18), the probe reached one light-day from Earth — the distance a photon travels in one day, which is about 16 billion miles (26 billion kilometers). That means it now takes a full day for a signal to travel from Earth to Voyager 1, the farthest human-made object out in space. "I remember in 1977 I was standing at the Cape watching Voyager launch, and I never thought I would be here today, 49 years later, to celebrate Voyager's one light-day. What an incredible milestone," Voyager project scientist Linda Spilker said during Thursday's event. An unexpected, but manageable, anomaly While this day and this week have been chock-full of excitement over the distance milestone, the Voyager mission team has been hard at work, as usual, keeping the two nearly 50-year-old spacecraft alive and functioning. Unfortunately, as JPL spokesperson Raquel Villanueva shared at this celebration, on Saturday (Nov. 14), Voyager 1 stopped transmitting science and engineering data.
