Windermere, in the Lake District, has been home to Arctic charr for more than 10,000 years, but campaigners warn extinction beckons as the fish's habitat is harmed by sewage pollution, invasive species competing for food and rising temperatures caused by global warming. Whitehouse urged Prime Minister Andy Burnham to act and said it would be "a crime" if the species were lost. The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) said it shared "the public's determination to protect this iconic lake". Whitehouse, who fronts the BBC's Mortimer & Whitehouse: Gone Fishing series alongside fellow comedian Bob Mortimer, said the "spawning grounds for this beautiful species are covered in algal sludge that is a direct result of sewage pollution". "This government needs to act now," he added. Image source, Lindsay McCrae/Freshwater Biological Association Image caption, Arctic charr have been described as a "living link to the end of the last Ice Age" The former Fast Show star is helping environmental campaigner Matt Staniek unveil a 1.3m (5ft 6in) ice sculpture depicting the prime minister outside No 10 North, in Manchester, to highlight the fish's plight. Staniek wants the prime minister to expedite a £3.7bn scheme that would include a huge tunnel carrying sewage to a new state-of-the-art water treatment works . It has been drawn up by the Only Rainwater In Windermere coalition comprising United Utilities (UU), the Environment Agency, Lake District National Park Authority (LDNPA), the Love Windermere partnership, Save Windermere campaign group and Westmorland and Furness Council. It comes as new research from the Freshwater Biological Association (FBA) also warns Arctic charr are in serious decline and face extinction in the lake without "urgent action". The fish, which is genetically distinct from Arctic charr in the region's other lakes, lives in four separate populations in Windermere that spawn at different times of year and different places and do not interbreed. The FBA said spawning habitat quality had "declined markedly" at Windermere since 2017 with levels of silt and algae increasing while introduced species, such as ruffe, roach and bream, were "on the rise". Image source, Press Association Image caption, Windermere has long been popular with visitors but it faces a number of pressures, campaigners say Dr Louise Lavictoire, the organisation's head of science, said the research had "shown very clearly that we are at risk of losing Arctic charr from Windermere completely". "The species is facing multiple pressures and research gives us the evidence, data and sense of urgency to take collective action to protect this ancient fish species before it's too late." The research was funded by groups including Natural England, the Environment Agency and the LDNPA. Natural England's Dave Ottewell described Arctic charr as "remarkable fish" and a "living link to the end of the last Ice Age". Tim Duckmanton, team leader for strategy and environment at the LDNPA, echoed that sentiment and said "thanks to data collected, we now have a better understanding of threats to the species". UU, which manages infrastructure around the lake, said protecting the area was a "priority" and that £200m was being invested over the next four years in an effort to cut spills from storm overflows and improve wastewater treatment. A Defra spokesperson said: "This government is already acting to reduce sewage discharges, including deploying 33 additional Environment Agency specialists in the region, increasing inspections and through our regional water council's giving the local community in Windermere a greater say in decision making. "Our strengthened Water Bill will bring in tougher regulation, stronger enforcement and greater accountability, so water companies deliver for customers and the environment."
