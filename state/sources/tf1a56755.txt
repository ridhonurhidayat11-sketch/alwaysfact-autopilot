Pandey The Himalayas continue to warm faster than the global average, and the resulting declining snow cover and accelerating glacier melt have left high-mountain communities grappling with seasonal water shortages. Some Indigenous communities in the western Himalayas have moved upslope , settling on rock glaciers in search of water and land to sustain their agropastoral livelihoods. Rock glaciers , in which ice and frozen sediments lie beneath a layer of rock debris, creep slowly downhill when active and are potential reservoirs of frozen water depending on their internal ice content. But as the cryosphere degrades, how much ground ice remains intact to sustain these communities? The first field-based study of inhabited relict rock glaciers—inactive landforms that show no internal movement—in the western Himalayas has attempted to answer this question, finding evidence of widespread permafrost degradation in two such landforms in the Spiti Valley, located in the Trans-Himalayan region in Himachal Pradesh, India. “It was very surprising to see that even at an elevation of around 4,200–4,500 meters, we could not get any intact ice within these bodies. Some ice is left that is at higher elevations, and how much of that is left, we have to investigate that further,” said Sheikh Nawaz Ali , a glacial geomorphologist at the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow, and the study’s corresponding author. Published in Science of the Total Environment , the study suggests residents rely upon surface streams, ponds, and wetlands sustained by the thawing of ancient permafrost in the upper reaches of these rock glaciers. Credit: S. Nawaz Ali and P. Pandey “At the present rate of melting, this is not a suitable reservoir for those communities,” Nawaz Ali said. The study highlights how relict rock glaciers can function as important local water reservoirs, even after losing much of their original ice content, said Remya S N , a cryosphere scientist at Azim Premji University, Bengaluru, not involved in this research. “However, this also raises concerns about the long-term sustainability of these water sources under increasing temperatures and changing snowfall patterns,” she wrote in an email to Eos . Mapping Buried Ice Rock glaciers remain relatively understudied , particularly in the Himalayas. “Not only rock glaciers, [but] permafrost studies in India are just in an embryonic stage,” said Nawaz Ali. The harsh climate, thin air, and complex terrain at high altitudes make fieldwork challenging. Most existing studies rely on remote sensing data, he said. “But what is the hydrological significance of that rock glacier? It has to be tested in the ground.” For this study, researchers mapped the landforms’ surface features using remote sensing and field investigations, classifying them as relict. “Relict does not necessarily mean hydrologically inactive. A rock glacier may have lost its original ice-rich structure and ceased moving yet retain buried ground ice and continue to influence local groundwater systems,” Remya said. “Increased thawing can temporarily release more water, but it may also indicate the progressive depletion of long-term ground-ice reserves.” To study their internal structure, researchers sent small electric currents into the ground through electrodes, taking eight measurements across various altitudes and locations. This technique, called electrical resistivity tomography , measures how strongly materials resist electricity. “It gives us a map with resistivity values,” said Nawaz Ali. Although values depend on regional lithology, solid materials like rock and glacial ice generally show higher resistivity, whereas water conducts electricity more easily, giving lower values. The surveys revealed shallow, lens-shaped low-resistivity areas that researchers interpreted as saturated zones with structurally weakened sediments. Nawaz Ali explained that this finding indicates ground ice has already thawed in places, leaving behind degraded ice or water. “Increased thawing can temporarily release more water, but it may also indicate the progressive depletion of long-term ground-ice reserves,” said Remya. Isotopic analysis of spring water samples showed groundwater emerging from these landforms has “preserved the signal of melting ice,” Nawaz Ali added. Because snow cover is limited and glaciers are absent in the upper reaches, researchers suggest this water may originate from melting subsurface ice, or permafrost. The data also indicate limited recharge of this diminishing store, as declining snowfall and rising temperatures make it hard to replenish or sustain permafrost. Uncertain Futures Climate perception surveys conducted during fieldwork found Komic and Chicham are already facing water shortages. To adapt, residents are constructing water reservoirs for irrigation and domestic use with the government’s help, Nawaz Ali said. Residents also reported declining agricultural productivity, leading men to migrate for work while women tended the fields. To adapt to water shortages, residents of Komic have constructed water reservoirs like this one to collect and store water from snowmelt or permafrost thaw. Credit: S. Nawaz Ali and P. Pandey “This [study] is an excellent example of how complex adaptation to climate change can be. Here we have a scientific question (how much ice is in the rock glacier) having important implications for social and cultural responses [to] climate change,” Stephan Harrison , a climate scientist at the University of Exeter in the United Kingdom, wrote in an email. Harrison was not associated with this research. “Eventually, the ice will all melt, but how long does this take? We don’t really know the answer to this question.” Remya stressed the need to map and incorporate these landforms into local water management and climate adaptation planning. Calling the study “an important starting point” that raises the need for regional assessments, she cautioned against generalizing the findings because the study covered two locations and relict rock glaciers’ hydrological behavior can vary regionally.
