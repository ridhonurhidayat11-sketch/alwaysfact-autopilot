Voyager 1 Makes History Reaching One Light-Day From Earth Jessica Taveau Nov 19, 2026 RELEASE 26-098 NASA Headquarters Voyager 1, depicted in this artist’s concept, reached one light-day from Earth on Nov. 18, 2026. Credit: NASA/JPL-Caltech NASA’s Voyager 1 probe, humanity’s farthest-flying spacecraft, has reached one light-day from Earth, after more than 49 years of constant travel. A light-day is the distance light travels in 24 hours, equaling about 16.1 billion miles (25.9 billion kilometers). The spacecraft achieved the milestone at 2:16 a.m. PST (5:16 a.m. EST) Nov. 18. Voyager team members at NASA’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory in Southern California, managed by Caltech, heard from the probe 24 hours later, the time it took for the spacecraft’s radio signal traveling at the speed of light to reach Earth, early Thursday. “Traveling a light-day is a singular feat for a spacecraft, but Voyager is so much more than a distant traveler. The Voyager mission would be remarkable just for what it contributed to planetary science with the Grand Tour of the outer planets,” said Joe Westlake, director, Heliophysics Division at NASA Headquarters in Washington. “But it went on to become central to NASA’s fleet of heliophysics missions offering insights into the star that we all revolve around, the Sun.” Scientists use light to measure the vast distances of space. Light travels at about 186,000 miles (299,000 kilometers) a second in a vacuum. A ray of sunlight passing Earth takes about 1.3 seconds to reach the Moon, about four hours to reach Neptune, and another 20 hours to reach Voyager 1. For comparison, a light-year is 5.88 trillion miles (9.46 trillion kilometers). Even though the team has not received science and engineering data from Voyager 1 since Nov. 14, the spacecraft is sending what is called a carrier signal, which typically conveys that data to Earth. Mission engineers used the carrier signal and trajectory calculations to confirm the light-day milestone. They continue to analyze the issue impacting the science and engineering data. Humanity’s only probes in interstellar space, Voyager 1 and its twin, Voyager 2, launched in 1977 to take advantage of a rare planetary alignment. Managed by JPL, they were only the third and fourth spacecraft to visit Jupiter and Saturn, and the first to return high-resolution imagery, transforming our understanding of these worlds and their moons. Voyager 2 went on to study the ethereal ice giants, Neptune and Uranus. Both spacecraft continue to measure changes in the Sun’s magnetic field across vast distances. “Voyager 1 crossing the light-day threshold is yet another historic feat on an extraordinary voyage of discovery,” said Dave Gallagher, JPL director. “Above all, this milestone stands as a testament to the exceptional teams who built, programmed, and fly these spacecraft today, and who have overcome countless challenges to keep them connected to Earth across the vastness of space.
