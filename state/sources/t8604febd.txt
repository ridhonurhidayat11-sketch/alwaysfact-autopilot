Am I wrong? A jury weighs in on whether it’s a researcher’s responsibility to convince colleagues that their work matters — or whether others should come to them instead. By Rachael Pells Rachael Pells View author publications Search author on: PubMed Google Scholar Email Bluesky Facebook LinkedIn Reddit Whatsapp X Find a new job Save article View saved research Credit: Gina Rodgers/Alamy The case Recently, I submitted my faculty file to be considered for promotion. I thought that being elected as a fellow at one of the most prestigious research institutes in my field — bioethics — would strengthen my case. But it seemed that no one I spoke to at my university had heard of the fellowship, and the value of it wasn’t recognized. I had an epiphany: if others don’t see the value, that’s on me. I need to better communicate why my work matters, rather than assuming that it speaks for itself. So I withdrew my file and will resubmit it when I have developed a stronger argument for the significance of the fellowship, and will gather support from senior colleagues on its merit. I know my work is valued outside my own university sphere; now I must make that case internally. If we want our work to count towards career advancement, it’s up to us to show our peers why it matters. Am I wrong? — Brandon Brown, public-health and biomedical-ethics specialist at the University of California, Riverside. Comments from the jury “I couldn’t disagree more. I think it’s a shame that scientists need to almost beg their peers to see the value of our work. Most of us invest huge amounts of time, effort and funding into what we do because we genuinely believe it matters. If you truly think your own work is irrelevant unless you can ‘sell it’ to others, then perhaps you’re in the wrong profession.” — Juliana Sanchez Alponti Millar, biologist at the University of York, UK. “The balancing act of spending your time doing what you think is important and convincing others about the value is tricky and always there — this is a good example of how you need your colleagues and mentors to understand your motivations and the ‘big plan’.’’ — Russ Altman, bioengineer and geneticist at Stanford University in California. “I completely agree with this. As scientists, we often assume our work will speak for itself, but communication and visibility matter a lot. Many of us also lack marketing and self-promotion skills, even though they can make a huge difference in these situations.” — Mariana Bel én Galeano, biologist at the University of Buenos Aires, Argentina. “The beauty of science is that the scientific process itself sorts out which research is useful. Scientists should do science, publish it and, rather than spend time campaigning for their work, go back and do more science.” — Metin Eren, anthropologist and archaeologist at Kent State University in Kent, Ohio. “Researchers do need to make their case, but they shouldn’t have to do it alone. Universities must do their part, too: set clear paths to promotion, review progress regularly and make sure managers explain to their staff members how promotion works.” — Timothy Davis, volcanologist at the University of Bristol, UK. “We are our own best advocates. It’s taken me some time to shed the modesty and be comfortable with promoting my own work and accolades, but a friend once told me ‘no one cares as much about your career as you do,” and that stuck. You have to show people why they should care.’ — Margaret Swift, ecologist at Cornell Atkinson Center for Sustainability and Cornell University College of Veterinary Medicine in Ithaca, New York.
