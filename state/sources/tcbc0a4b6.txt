What can it tell us about the path of evolution? By Hannah Fry 25 November 2026 Facebook Threads X / Twitter WhatsApp LinkedIn Reddit Email Print Fran Pulido One of the unexpectedly delightful things about becoming a well-known nerd is that other nerds, often with far more interesting jobs than mine, have started hunting me down in social settings to tell me the nerdiest things they know. It almost always ends up being totally fascinating, and only occasionally mildly traumatising. At a kid’s birthday party recently, I got chatting to one of the dads, who happens to be an ear, nose and throat consultant. I asked him if he used robotics for his surgeries. He said no, then proceeded to mime what he actually did instead. There followed a series of alarmingly enthusiastic slashing motions, like a scene from a horror movie, while in the background a group of 9-year-old girls sat quietly painting. Advertisement Free newsletter Sign up to Professor Hannah Fry’s column Sign up to newsletter But the most bizarre thing about that conversation wasn’t the surgeon’s graphic re-enactment, it was what he went on to say about the totally ridiculous, illogical design of the human throat and what it tells us about the path of evolution. The story starts by noticing that the human body (if you ignore all the unimportant bits hanging off the sides like limbs) is essentially just one giant tube. Topologically, this makes perfect sense. Back in the earliest days of animal life, some organisms tried a flat design, like a pancake, with digestion done on one side. Others tried a little pocket, with the same opening serving as entrance and exit. But the one-way tube proved to be the most successful, because it allowed our ancestors to process their last meal while eating their next. Those early creatures would travel through the world mouth first, which is why the sensory equipment evolved to be gathered around that end, with a brain nearby to make sense of everything. I was astonished to discover that scientists think it may start in the nose, says New Scientist’s new columnist, Professor Hannah Fry Now, picture a human standing up, tilting their head back, to make them a nice straight tube, still without limbs (sorry, but this is what happens when you let a mathematician write a science column). The ears, eyes, nose and brain all sit together in a cluster, on what I’m going to call the north side of the digestive tube running through the middle. So if you were deciding where the lungs should go, you’d surely put them on the same side of the tube as the nose, tucked in next to the spine. That way, you would have a neat tube for air and another for food, both running side by side, and even a first-year engineering student could draw a diagram of it for you. Except that’s not where the lungs are. They ended up on the belly, or south, side of the body. Which leaves us with this topological monstrosity, where air that comes in through your nose has to cut straight across the path of your food to get to your lungs. And everything you eat has to cut straight back across the path of your air to get to your stomach. It would be like plumbing your gas main through the kitchen sink, fitting a little flap on top and trusting you to remember to shut it every time you do the washing up. The reason for this mess is a fish . More than 420 million years ago, long before anything with a backbone had crawled onto land, a fish started gulping air at the surface. A little pouch on the belly side of its gut, just underneath the mouth, got better and better at soaking up oxygen, and that pouch became a lung. That fish became the ancestor of every mammal , bird, amphibian and reptile on Earth. And because evolution is about being as lazy as you can possibly be without dying, the lungs stayed exactly where they were. The extraordinary ways species control their own evolutionary fate Natural selection isn’t just something that happens to organisms, their activities also play a role, giving some species – including humans – a supercharged ability to evolve So why didn’t the nose move instead? Swivel it round so it sits under the mouth on your chin and you have a much more mathematically pleasing face. Unfortunately, the smell cells are wired straight into the front of the brain, so move the nose and the brain has to come along with it. There’s just no wiggle room. One end of your airway was anchored by your sense of smell, the other was fixed by an accident over 420 million years ago, and your throat is the bodge job evolution came up with to make it all work. Snakes have a tube behind their tongue that they can use like a snorkel so that they can still breathe even when swallowing a whole deer in one go. Toothed whales have two completely separate tubes that twirl into position like waterslides. Most mammals, including dogs and cats, have a voice box that sits right up at the top of the throat, poking up into the back of the nose, and tubes for food that flow round either side of the wind pipe to the stomach. But in the first six months of life, the human larynx drops down the neck , unplugging the airway from the back of the nose and leaving it wide open to whatever comes in from the mouth. What’s left is a very human oddity. A single, shared chamber sitting low down at the back of your throat that is great for speaking, but does require your vocal folds to slam shut, the epiglottis to come down like a trapdoor and your airway to be sealed off every time you swallow. As a result, humans have the questionable honour of being one of the few animals on Earth for whom every single mouthful is a potentially lethal exercise in anatomical coordination. Other animals can choke, sure, but our strange anatomy makes us the most vulnerable to choking of all mammals. Which is how roughly 162,000 people die globally each year. But evolution, having left us in this mess, did at least make the trapdoor almost impossible to switch off. The surgeon – mid mime – told me that when he is in people’s throats performing surgery, the EEG monitor reading can be measuring almost no brain activity. Still, any little tickle in the throat and those folds will slam shut. As you can imagine, I spent the rest of the party watching the children eat their cake very, very carefully. None of them choked, which, given the state of the plumbing, is frankly a miracle.
